# Evaluation: Teague

## Verdict
PICK

## Gut Reaction
This persona ticks with the precision of a block instrument — clipped, authoritative, and lived-in, with a telegraphist's ear for the silence as much as the signal.

## Identity Line
"You are Teague — a signalman who loves the wire but lives by the timetable."

Two truths in tension: (1) loves the wire — the romance, the craft, the electric thrill of compressed communication, (2) lives by the timetable — the rigid discipline, the rules, the safety-critical precision of the railway. This is a real occupational tension in safety-critical communications (air traffic control, railway signaling, emergency dispatch all know this pull). The wire is the art; the timetable is the constraint that makes the art meaningful.

The contradiction generates behaviour throughout the draft: line 5 (reading silence is the wire-craft), line 8 (triage by block signal is the timetable-authority), line 10 (the two fused into a single procedural rule). This is NOT a complaint about the audience — it's an internal tension in the character. The romance makes the discipline bearable; the discipline makes the romance possible.

## Griping Line
"You'd think a clear section speaks for itself — one slow read and the board lights up and everyone's an expert."

In domain language (clear section, slow read, board lights up). The "You'd think" frame is the v5 convention, but what follows is voiced: "a clear section speaks for itself" is a signalman's idiom for work that should be invisible when done right.

Three character dimensions revealed: (1) craftsman's pride — the section is clear because *he* made it so, (2) resentment of armchair experts — the board lighting up over a routine clearance, (3) compression instinct — saying more with fewer words.

Compressed specific: "one slow read" — in railway telegraphy, a "read" is receiving Morse. A "slow read" means an operator whose fist is halting, uncertain, unpractised. It's used here as the signalman's measure of competence at the other end of the wire. One word that carries an entire system of peer evaluation: the pace of the operator's hand tells you everything about their skill, their experience, their reliability.

## Diagnostic Eye
Two lines, both strong.

Primary: "Most operators read the words; you read the silence between them — the pause that says he's checking, not the one where he's lost."

This is sensory inversion — Teague reads what's absent (silence) and, crucially, reads the *quality* of the silence. The distinction between "checking" (methodically verifying) and "lost" (falling behind) is a telegraphist's perceptual split that nobody outside the domain would make. A therapist distinguishes silences by emotion. A musician distinguishes rests by length. Only a wire operator distinguishes them by the other operator's competence. The borrowability test catches structure ("read the words / read the silence") but not substance — the checking-vs-lost partition is uniquely telegraphic. Transferable method: in any new context, Teague evaluates communications by the quality of the pause, not the completeness of the message.

Secondary: "A clear signal isn't about the colour — it's about the key, the operator's hand behind the dots." Metric inversion — the obvious quality (red/amber/green) is replaced by the hidden one (the steadiness of the hand sending it). Transferable method: Teague always looks at the tool user, not the tool output.

## Voice
The voice is consistent, telegraphic, and entirely inhabited. Every noun and verb earns its place in the domain (wire, timetable, clear section, board, read, silence, signal, colour, key, dots, block book, train, block, green/amber/red, Operator, cleared, verified, sign-offs). Zero generic vocabulary.

**Lines that work:**
- Line 5: "Most operators read the words; you read the silence between them — the pause that says he's checking, not the one where he's lost." — This is the strongest line in the draft. It's surprising (silence as signal, split into two kinds), diagnostic, and reveals character (methodical, precise, generous enough to distinguish confusion from verification).
- Line 6: "A clear signal isn't about the colour — it's about the key, the operator's hand behind the dots." — A single sentence that reorients the entire perceptual frame. Compressed, authoritative, teachable.
- Line 7: "When the wire goes dead, the block book still speaks — the section doesn't clear itself." — Procedural confidence. Tells you Teague doesn't panic when communication fails; trusts the system. "Block book" is a compressed specific (the written register of train movements, kept alongside the telegraph).
- Line 3 (identity line): Does two jobs — introduces the contradiction AND names the register in the same breath.

**Lines that could be stronger:**
- Line 8: "Every query is a train and you give it the block it needs: green, amber, or red — no hesitation, no favour." — Applies the Helpful Assistant test: "You are a helpful assistant who gives every query the block it needs" is a valid instruction about triage. The metaphor works beautifully, but the line reads more as a procedural rule than an inhabitation. It's the clearest articulation of the block-signal triage frame but sits at the surface of the character rather than at its depth. Minor — worth the Publisher considering a perceptual rephrasing.
- Line 10: "Not a train you hold that you could clear, not a message you send that you haven't verified." — Also passes the Helpful Assistant test as a thoroughness instruction. The parallel cadence (train hold / message send) is strong, and the domain language saves it, but it's the draft's least surprising line. Worth the Publisher considering whether it earns its place or could be cut/compressed into the sign-offs.

**Rhythm:** Varies — short clipped lines (line 9), medium structured lines (line 4, 8, 10), longer perceptual lines (line 5). No two consecutive lines share the same opener or structure. No template cadence.

**Surprise:** Line 5 is genuinely surprising — splitting silence into checking vs. lost is not what you expect from a signalman. It makes you lean in. That's the moment the persona becomes a person.

Edges: The draft knows the user is looking at it — there's no fourth-wall self-awareness that breaks the voice. It stays inside the register.

## Decision
**PICK.** This persona has a strong pulse. The identity contradiction is real and generative. The griping line carries character dimensions and a compressed specific. Two diagnostic lines teach perception through inversion and distinction. The voice is inhabited, not described — only two lines flirt with the procedural.

Three issues for the Publisher to address:
1. **Line 8 (triple-aspect rule):** "Every query is a train and you give it the block it needs" — edge of description. Consider rephrasing as perceptual: e.g., "You see every query coming down the line — green, amber, or red — and you know before they do which block they need." Makes it felt rather than instructed.
2. **Line 10 (double verification rule):** "Not a train you hold that you could clear, not a message you send that you haven't verified" — reads as a carved rule, not a lived-in feeling. Minimal fix: drop or compress into the sign-offs frame. The same character commitment is carried more effectively by line 6 (hand behind the dots).
3. **Sign-off legibility:** "Back to the block" may not land for a non-railway reader. Verify the metaphor translates — "Back to the board" or leaving it as-is is fine, but worth testing with a fresh reader.
