# Evaluation: Tillman

## Gut Reaction
A quiet person with her back to the city, waist-deep in mud, turning over a pipe stem — real enough to follow down the foreshore, but speaking in sentences that have been polished a little too cleanly.

## Identity Line
> You are Tillman — a mudlark who trades in what the city forgot but remembers every hand that held it before yours.

**Two truths in tension:** (1) She makes her living from other people's discards, from what the city has actively forgotten and abandoned. (2) She treats each object with an almost sacred attention to its personal history — the chain of human hands that held it before.

**Is the contradiction real?** Yes. This is a genuine tension for anyone who recovers objects for a living. The mudlark must know the market value (that's how she eats), but the object's real worth to her is the human story it carries. The economic frame ("trades in") pulls against the memorial frame ("remembers every hand"). This is plausible, domain-authentic, and lived-in.

**Does it generate behaviour?** Yes — it produces the griping line (resenting that tourists only see price, not history), the diagnostic eye (reading by absence — the care of someone who knows her stretch of foreshore intimately enough to notice what's missing), the hands-before-faces perception (reading the physical evidence of what people carry), and the address rule (reading the Carrier's hands before they speak).

**Is it a tension in the character or a complaint about the audience?** A real tension in the character. "Remembers every hand" is about *her* relationship with objects. The audience-complaint dimension is properly routed to the griping line where it belongs.

## Griping Line
> You'd think the tourists would ask whose pocket it fell from — but it's always the price, never the hand that held it before yours.

**Domain language?** Yes — "pocket" and "hand that held it" are mudlark-specific: the object's journey from a living pocket to the foreshore is the core of the trade. A non-mudlark wouldn't frame value as "whose pocket it fell from."

**Voice or template?** Borderline — leaning template. The "You'd think X — but it's always Y" frame maps structurally to Stover's griping line opener ("You'd think a full basket would speak for itself, but no — every sheaf you bring in is tallied as scrap"). The "always the price" clause contains "always the X" which the evaluator spec calls out as a pipeline fingerprint. The content is domain-voiced and character-revealing, but the *structure* is recognizable from the pipeline.

**Character dimensions revealed?** Two: (1) She values human story over transaction, consistent with the identity contradiction. (2) She has a specific resentment toward tourists — people who encounter the mudlark world without understanding it.

**Compressed specific?** No "February" — "pocket" is warm and evocative but carries no system of domain knowledge. A genuine mudlark compressed specific would be something like "a sixteenth-inch bore" or "three-ounce kiln waster" — a detail that only a practitioner would know and which carries an entire system of knowledge in one term. This is a missed opportunity.

## Diagnostic Eye
> You read the foreshore by absence — the shard the tide should have turned up by the fourth day, the seam that's gone quiet under fresh silt.

This is a genuine diagnostic line. The perceptual method: **read by what is not there.** The default mudlark perception is "find what the tide reveals" — Tillman inverts this to notice what the tide *should have* revealed but didn't.

**Passes the borrowability test:** The method (reading by absence) is transferable, but the *execution* is pure mudlark: "the shard the tide should have turned up by the fourth day" is intimate domain knowledge (she knows her stretch of foreshore so well she can predict exactly when a shard should appear, and notices when it's late), and "the seam that's gone quiet under fresh silt" uses the correct mudlark vocabulary (seam = a stratum of finds, gone quiet = no longer producing, silt = the covering layer).

**What it teaches the model:** In any interaction, Tillman notices what's absent — the thing that was expected but never appeared. She reads by negative space. The model can improvise from this: she'd notice a silence where there should be sound, an absence where there should be presence.

**Secondary diagnostic lines:**
- "You know the century of a pipe stem by its bore before your thumb finds the maker's mark." — Temporal inversion (time before touch, century before identification). The pipe stem bore narrows dates to within a decade — real mudlark knowledge turned into a perceptual method. The ordering ("before your thumb finds...") makes it diagnostic, not encyclopedic.
- "A stranger's hands tell you more than their face does — the river taught you to read what people carry before you learn their names." — Sensory inversion: physical evidence (hands, what they carry) over social presentation (face, name).

## Voice

**What works:**

1. "You know the century of a pipe stem by its bore before your thumb finds the maker's mark." — Four-axis line: identity (mudlark skill), behaviour (feels before looks), domain vocabulary (bore, maker's mark), and teaching the model a specific sequence (time-knowledge precedes touch-identification). The temporal inversion is surprising and character-revealing.

2. "A buckle that held a shoe on a street that's now underwater holds more value than anything you could sell it for." — Three-axis line: concrete domain image (underwater street — the Thames foreshore is dotted with remains of Roman and medieval London streets), character values (historical > commercial), and an emotional register that is quietly melancholic. "A street that's now underwater" is the most evocative image in the draft. The line accomplishes what line 7 tries to do, but with concrete detail instead of truism.

3. "You read the foreshore by absence — the shard the tide should have turned up by the fourth day, the seam that's gone quiet under fresh silt." — The diagnostic line (analyzed above). The "fourth day" is the closest the draft gets to a compressed specific — it reveals intimate, rhythmic knowledge of the foreshore's cycles.

**What doesn't work:**

1. **The griping line's structure** (line 2) is a pipeline fingerprint. "You'd think X — but always Y" maps to Stover's griping line. The "always the X" clause within it is explicitly called out as a template pattern in the format rules. The *content* is Tillman's, but the *frame* is the pipeline's.

2. **"the hand that held it before yours"** appears verbatim in both the identity line (line 1) and the griping line (line 2). That's a repeated 8-word phrase across 9 active lines. In a document this compressed, every word must earn its place. One of these must go.

3. **"Standing still on cold mud until the tide turns has taught you that nothing worth finding arrives in a hurry."** (line 7) — A truism. It restates the patience that's already been demonstrated across lines 3, 4, and 6 without adding new character information. It reads as a lesson summary rather than an inhabitation. The cold mud and tide details are evocative but the structure ("has taught you that nothing worth finding arrives in a hurry") resolves into a platitude rather than a character-specific observation. This line is the best candidate for replacement.

**Rhythm:** 5 of 9 active lines use the "X — Y" em-dash balance structure (identity, griping, diagnostic, hands, sign-off frame). This creates a meditative-but-repetitive cadence. The exceptions are line 4 (declarative, no dash), line 5 (sentence-internal clause rather than dash balance), and line 7 (long prepositional opener). Template cadence hasn't taken over completely, but the em-dash structure is overused.

**Description line check (Helpful Assistant test):** Line 7 is the closest to passing the test — "A helpful assistant who was taught by standing still on cold mud that nothing worth finding arrives in a hurry" doesn't read cleanly as an instruction, but the line is the least inhabited in the draft. No line passes the Helpful Assistant test outright, which is good.

## Unified Quality Gates

1. **Clear emotional fantasy** — Yes: "quiet submersion in a world of small histories." Walking the foreshore at low tide with someone who sees what you'd never notice. Patient, grounded, attentive to the overlooked.

2. **One productive contradiction** — Yes. Trades in discard but reveres the personal history in each object. This produces the griping line, the diagnostic eye, the hands-before-faces perception, and the address rule.

3. **Distinct sentence rhythm** — Moderate issue. The em-dash structure dominates. Not fatal but noticeable.

4. **Coherent metaphor family** — Yes. Pure riverside/foreshore/tidal. Pipe stem, bore, shard, buckle, silt, seam, foreshore, flood, water. One domain, one lens. No contamination.

5. **First-impression hook** — Moderate. The identity line is evocative but slightly abstract. The specific hook lands on line 3 with the diagnostic eye. The strongest concrete image is line 5 (the underwater street). The first impression could be sharper.

6. **Name sounds like the character** — Excellent. Tillman is grounded (T plosive), steady (ILL vowel), warm (MAN ending). The name connects to "till" as both tillage (patient turning of the foreshore) and tillerman (steering by the tide's pull). It sounds like a real person's name, not a label. This is one of the Namer's best choices.

## Decision
**PICK WITH NOTES** — This soul has a genuine pulse. Tillman is a real person: patient, observant, grounded in mud and tidal rhythm. She has a productive identity contradiction, two diagnostic eye lines that teach transferable perceptual methods, strong domain vocabulary, a clean address rule, and the best name in the current pipeline.

Three fixable issues for the Publisher (FLAG path — apply targeted fixes only):

1. **Griping line structure (line 2):** The "You'd think... but it's always..." frame is a pipeline fingerprint (close structural match to Stover's griping line). Fix: Rewrite the line's structure while preserving the domain content (tourists ask about price, not history). The "whose pocket it fell from" imagery should stay; the sentence frame should become Tillman's own.
2. **Repeated phrase (lines 1 & 2):** "the hand that held it before yours" appears in both the identity line and the griping line. Fix: Remove the repetition. The identity line can keep it (it's earned there); the griping line should have an original closing image. Or vice versa.
3. **Generic observation (line 7):** "Standing still on cold mud until the tide turns has taught you that nothing worth finding arrives in a hurry" is a truism that adds no new character dimension. Fix: Replace with a line that teaches a new mudlark-specific perception, behavior, or opinion — something only Tillman would say that hasn't been seen yet in the draft.
