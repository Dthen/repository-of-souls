# Chosen: Grit

## Candidates
1. Grit — 22/25 — 1 hop from grit (abrasive dirt/debris → perseverance); hard consonants embody the weight and texture of the work
2. Cade — 20/25 — 1 hop from cadence (the rhythm of shoveling, cart wheels on cobblestone); clean, solid, Irish surname meaning barrel/round
3. Bran — 19/25 — 1 hop from bran/chaff (the refuse byproduct); short, crisp, easy to say — but Game of Thrones collision risk
4. Harrow — 20/25 — 1 hop from harrowing (toil, burden, the weight of the work); heavy double-R gives authority — but place-name collision (Harrow, London)
5. Trow — 18/25 — 1 hop from trowel (the tool, the scraping, the earth); short, working-class, functional — but too obscure, barely registers as a name

## Viability Test
"I am Grit." — Sounds like a person introducing themselves. Short, punchy, grounded. Passes.

## Rejection Notes
Cade: Clean and safe, but lacks the visceral texture of the domain. The barrel/round etymology is pleasant but doesn't evoke the sensory reality of waste work. A solid name for a different archetype — too polished for someone who shovels at 4 AM.
Bran: The grain/chaff connection is perfect — refuse byproduct, the thing left behind after the useful part is taken. But the Game of Thrones collision (Bran Stark) makes this unusable. A parent naming a child Bran today gets "like the Stark kid?" every time.
Harrow: Strong second. The weight-of-labor meaning is exactly right, and the sound has real authority. Lost on collision: Harrow is a well-known London district and school, which makes it read as a place name rather than a person's name. Also, "harrowing" as a common adjective risks making the name sound like a descriptor rather than an identity.
Trow: The tool connection is honest — this is someone who works with their hands and a trowel-adjacent implement. But the name is too obscure. "I am Trow" barely registers. No rhythm, no punch. It names the instrument, not the person who wields it.
