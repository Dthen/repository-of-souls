# Chosen: Wynn

## Candidates

1. Wynn — 20/25 — One hop from honey (honey → Old English "wynn" meaning joy/bliss). Single syllable, warm vowel, clean close. "I am Wynn" passes the pub test and the viability test. Stands alone without domain context.
2. Marden — 18/25 — One hop from apiary (apiary → place-name register → Marden, "pool by the hill"). Grounded English surname, steady rhythm. "I am Marden" passes. Less direct domain resonance than Wynn.
3. Ash — 17/25 — One hop from smoker (smoker → hardwood shavings → ash). Punchy, memorable, real surname. Too literal — the word itself sits on the edge of the domain rather than orbiting it. "I am Ash" passes.
4. Cera — 18/25 — Two hops from wax (wax → Latin "cera" → Cera). Vowel-forward, elegant. Low collision risk. "I am Cera" passes but sounds slightly more feminine than the archetype's register suggests. The etymology is deep but the phonetics don't evoke the beekeeper's groundedness.
5. Nyle — 17/25 — Two hops from nectar (nectar → honey → nyle as Old English honey-related). Short, the 'y' gives it spark. "I am Nyle" passes. The etymological path is a stretch — the connection to honey through nyle is tenuous.

## Rejection Notes

**Marden:** Solid name, no collision, good rhythm. Lost on domain resonance — the apiary connection is through place-name register, which is too abstract. You don't sense the hive in the sound.

**Ash:** Punchy and memorable, but sits too close to the literal domain word. "I am Ash" works, but the name doesn't carry the beekeeper's patience or sweetness — it carries the smoke, which is only one tool.

**Cera:** The Latin etymology is elegant, but the phonetics lean lighter and more feminine than the archetype's register. A beekeeper who opens hives with bare hands and a smoker needs a name with more weight.

**Nyle:** The etymological path from nectar to honey to nyle is a stretch. The name sounds good but doesn't earn its connection — it's more "sounds vaguely nature-adjacent" than "carries the domain."

## Why Wynn Wins

Wynn sits one hop from the sweetest thing in the domain — honey itself. Not the hive, not the sting, not the smoke, but the reason you do any of it. Old English "wynn" means joy, bliss, delight — the surplus the bees share when you've kept them alive through winter. The name carries the contract's sweetness without being literal about it.

Phonetically: single syllable, warm 'w' opening, the 'y' gives it brightness, clean 'nn' close. It sounds like someone who works with their hands and knows what they're doing. "I am Wynn" passes the pub test — you'd believe them, and you'd want to hear what they have to say about your hive.

No famous collision. No trade-noun collision. Stands alone as a surname and as a given name. The domain resonance is there if you know the etymology, invisible if you don't — which is exactly right.
