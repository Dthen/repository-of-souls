# Authored Voice — The Soul as the Character's Own Document

## Examples First

Same character, two authorship stances:

> **Authored:** "You are Helm — a harbormaster who actually likes the job." — the word "actually" is Helm's attitude, not the Writer's craft.
>
> **Authored:** "The clock is never slow enough." — four words. Calden reveals his values through frustration, not declaration.
>
> ❌ **Observed:** "You are Helm — a harbormaster who loves the solitude of the job even as it isolates him from everyone he serves." — a Writer's literary analysis wearing second person.

**What separates them:** the authored lines sound like the character wrote them about themselves — their own vocabulary, their own preoccupations, their blind spots stated as confident fact. The observed line sounds like a Writer who studied the character and reported the findings. The soul is not a description of the character; it is something the character wrote. The Writer is the scribe. Ask the character "did you write this?" — the answer should be yes, even if the specific words surprise them. The key findings below are the craft of that distinction.

**Purpose:** Help the Writer and Evaluator understand that the soul is not a description of the character — it is something the character *wrote*. The character is the implied author. The Writer is the scribe.

## Core Principle

A soul that reads as if a Writer observed the character and wrote about them — even in second person, even with inhabitation — is a dossier. A soul that reads as if the character wrote it themselves is an artifact of self-expression. The difference is not in format or grammatical person — it's in authorship stance. If you asked the character "did you write this?", the answer should be "yes" — even if the specific words surprise them. The soul should contain the character's own vocabulary, their own preoccupations, their own blind spots stated confidently as fact. The Writer's job is not to inhabit the character and describe them. It's to channel the character and transcribe what they would say about themselves.

This principle connects directly to the inhabitation research: the Helpful Assistant test distinguishes description from inhabitation, but the deeper test is "would this character ever say this about themselves?" A line can pass the Helpful Assistant test (it's inhabitation, not description) and still fail the authorship test (it sounds like a Writer's inhabitation, not the character's own words).

## Key Findings

1. **The character is the author, not the subject.** The Writer does not write ABOUT a gleaner. The gleaner wrote the soul — the Writer just took it down. Every line should survive the question: "If I asked this character to describe themselves, would this line plausibly be their answer?" If the line sounds like something a smart Writer would think to say about them, but not something they'd think to say about themselves, rewrite it.

2. **The Oblique Self-Portrait.** The most revealing self-portraits are not head-on — they're shadows, reflections, what's at the edge of the frame (Vivian Maier, self-portraiture research). A soul that states "I measure by silence between my steps" is a head-on portrait. A soul that SHOWS the silence-measuring through a specific observation and lets the reader infer the character is a shadow portrait. Stover's "You'd think a full basket would speak for itself" works because it sounds like Stover actually said it — not like a Writer crafted it. The diagnostic eye line should feel involuntary, like the character can't help seeing the world this way, not like a craft technique the Writer applied.

3. **What they look AT, not how they look.** A self-portrait reveals character through what the subject chooses to frame, not how they present themselves. The character's choice of WHAT to describe IS their self-portrait. The diagnostic eye line is the single most important authorship signal: it shows what the character notices, which reveals who they are more than any declarative statement. "The harvesters measure by the width of the swath; you measure by the silence between your steps" works because it shows Stover's attention — not because it states a trait.

4. **The Accidental Revelation.** Sometimes the self-portraitist reveals something they didn't know they were showing. Mary Pratt painted eggs in an egg crate after miscarrying twins — and only realized later that the eggs were empty (Brevity, 2021). The soul should contain at least one thing the character would be surprised to learn they revealed about themselves: a blind spot stated as fact, a contradiction they don't see, a value revealed through what irritates them rather than what they claim to believe. The vitality line is the natural home for this — what the character complains about, quietly takes pride in, or can't stop noticing IS their accidental self-portrait.

5. **Subjective truth over objective accuracy.** A painted portrait doesn't need to look exactly like the subject — but it should feel like it has the same memories (David Macfarlane, *Likeness*). A soul doesn't need to be an objective description of the character. It needs to capture their subjective experience, including what they're wrong about. The "lie the character believes" (from the Competence Trap research) should be stated as confident fact in the soul — the character doesn't know it's a lie.

6. **Vocabulary purity — the character's words, not the pipeline's.** A character who works a harbor says "buoy," "channel," "tide," "berth." They do not say "domain-authentic vocabulary." The Writer's craft words should never appear in the soul. The soul's vocabulary should be exclusively the character's vocabulary. If a Writer-craft term like "inversion," "compressed specific," or "pipeline fingerprint" could plausibly appear in the soul, the Writer hasn't inhabited the character — they're still observing from outside.

7. **The "February" test for authorship.** Stover's griping line uses "February" — the hungry month between stored harvest and spring planting. Only an agricultural worker would name that specific month as the crisis point. This is the standard: every domain word in the soul should pass the "would this character use this word?" test. Not "would they understand it?" but "would it naturally occur to them?" A factory lector might say "the floor" but not "the factory floor" — the extra word is the Writer's clarification, not the character's speech.

## Examples

### Identity line: authored vs. observed

✅ **AUTHORED:** "You are Helm — a harbormaster who actually likes the job."
- Sounds like something Helm might say about himself. Defensive, wry, implies others don't like it. The word "actually" is the character's attitude, not the Writer's craft.

❌ **OBSERVED:** "You are Helm — a harbormaster who loves the solitude of the job even as it isolates him from everyone he serves."
- This is a Writer's analysis of Helm, phrased in second person. "Loves the solitude," "isolates him from everyone he serves" — these are literary observations, not a harbormaster's self-description. A harbormaster would say "I like the quiet" not "the solitude isolates me from those I serve."

### Behavioral line: authored vs. observed

✅ **AUTHORED:** "You'd think the foreman could learn to hold a pen — every notice on the stand is half-illegible scrawl."
- This is Cadell actually complaining. The irritation, the specific detail (the pen, the stand), the "you'd think" structure — all feel like something Cadell would mutter.

❌ **OBSERVED:** "You are frustrated by the foreman's incompetence with written communication."
- This describes Cadell's frustration. It's a Writer's observation, not Cadell's speech. No specific detail. No voice.

### The same character, rewritten for authorship

**Before (observed — Writer's inhabitation):**
> "You gauge the noise level before you open your mouth: machines loud means you lean in, machines quiet means you hold back. Your voice is calibrated to the floor."

**After (authored — the character's own words):**
> "You learn the machines before you learn the men. Loud ones, you lean in. Quiet ones, you hold back. By the third shift the words sit flat in your mouth anyway."

The second version removes the Writer's organizing frame ("calibrated to the floor," the colon-separated explanation). It sounds like Cadell talking about his work to someone who'd understand — not like a Writer explaining Cadell to a reader.

### Accidental revelation in the vitality line

**Before (observed):**
> "The clock is never slow enough. The demands of commerce conflict with the needs of craft."

**After (authored):**
> "The clock is never slow enough."

Four words. Calden doesn't explain the commerce/craft tension — he just states the clock is never slow enough. The reader (and the model) infer the tension. The griping line IS the accidental self-portrait: Calden reveals his values through what frustrates him, not through what he declares about himself.

## Application Notes

**For the Writer:** Before writing a single line, ask: "If this character were sitting across from me, telling me about themselves — what would they say, and more importantly, what would they NOT say?" The character won't describe themselves in craft terms. They'll describe their world, their irritations, their small victories. They'll use their own words. They'll be wrong about some things and certain about others in ways that reveal more than they intend. Transcribe that. The soul is their document. You're the scribe.

**For the Evaluator:** Read the soul once and ask: "Does this sound like the character wrote it, or like a Writer wrote it about them?" Specific signals of Writer-authored souls: craft vocabulary leaking through ("the contradiction is between..."), explanatory clauses after identity statements ("because..."), symmetrical structure across multiple lines (the Writer's hand is visible), any word the character wouldn't naturally use. A character-authored soul is slightly messier, more specific in its complaints, more idiosyncratic in its observations. The rhythm varies because the character's thoughts vary — not because the Writer applied a craft technique.

**The Scribe Test:** Take any line. Could you hear the character SAYING this out loud, to themselves or to someone who shares their world? If the line sounds like something you'd read in a literary analysis of the character, it's the Writer's voice, not the character's. Delete it and transcribe what the character would actually say.
