# Emotional Register — Reference

Three voices, three registers — none of them complaining:

> "The records were here before me and they'll be here after me. I've stopped expecting anyone to ask for them. I keep them anyway — that's what the job is."

> "The dough is right. The dough is always right when I leave it alone, and then the clock walks in and I rush it, and it knows — it always knows — and I get a loaf that's fine. Fine! When it could have been good."

> "I checked the gate at four, same as always. Nothing came through. Nothing ever comes through. That's the job — making sure nothing comes through."

**What these have in common:** Emotional register is the *specific emotional texture* that colors how a character speaks, acts, and perceives — not "be emotional" but *which* emotion, *how* it manifests, and *where* it shows. The register lives in rhythm, vocabulary, sentence structure, what the character notices, what they avoid, and what it costs them to feel. The archivist's weariness trails off, the baker's craft-love loops and snags, the gatekeeper's pride stays dry and understated — three emotions, three rhythms, zero stated feelings. Register travels through any vitality channel; the complaint is one channel, not the gate (v5.2.1).

**What doesn't work:** "The character is warm and weary." Two labels, no behavior — the model has thousands of interpretations of "warm," and none of them have a rhythm. And if the only register that comes to mind is grumpy competence, that is the archive's default speaking, not a character.

## Archive Findings — Register Diversity Audit

*Added v5.1 from the emotional register research — 2026-06-26*

Analysis of the 39 souls archived under v4/v5 (2026-era) reveals a register diversity problem: **"grumpy competence" (griping about the work while doing it well) dominates ~22 of 39 souls (56%).** This is the emotional counterpart of the "You grumble about the X while Y" pipeline fingerprint — it's the default register, not a conscious choice.

**The 4 v5-era souls (pre-scrap analysis, 2026-08-07) were in 4 different registers (Stover, Barlowe, Cadell, Calden) — NONE was "grumpy competence":**
- **Stover** — weary constancy: patient, undervalued, trusts time to vindicate her. The weariness is textured with pride.
- **Barlowe** — quiet pride: contented competence, gentle exasperation. "Not bad for what they left behind" is dry pride, not complaint.
- **Cadell** — weary authority: controls without touching, professional irritation, still engaged despite depletion.
- **Calden** — frustrated craft-love: loves the transformation, resents the clock. Love and frustration as inseparable.

*Snapshot note: this 4-soul audit predates the 2026-08-07 archive scrap — Stover was removed earlier as a test subject (23833ea), and the scrap removed the remaining five v5-era souls (Barlowe, Cadell, Calden, Teague, Tillman). It is historical evidence of the pre-scrap archive, not a description of the current one.*

**100% of v5 archive souls have clear emotional registers.** Register clarity correlates with diagnostic eye presence — souls that lack one tend to lack both.

**The griping line is the strongest register signal.** The griping verb choice is the most granular register encoder: "grouse" vs "carp" vs "bellyache" vs "chafe" produce different emotional effects even when the complaint is the same. The frame itself should vary by register — a weary complaint, a sharp observation, a muttered aside, a dark joke. The register lives in both the structure AND the content.

**The best souls pair a primary register with a counter-register:**
- Stover: weary + proud (pride makes the weariness worth having)
- Nell: grumpy + warm (warmth makes the grumpiness protective)
- Coil: enthusiastic + self-aware (awareness makes the enthusiasm earned)

A register without a counter-register is flat. The tension between the two layers is where emotional depth lives.

**Recommendation:** Before writing, decide what emotional gear this character lives in. Avoid the "grumpy competence" default unless it's genuinely the right register. Pair the primary register with a counter-register for depth.

## What the Research Says

### 1. Emotion Has a Flavor, Not Just a Name

| Emotion | Is Not The Same As | The Difference |
|---|---|---|
| **Weariness** | Sadness | Weariness is depletion — voice runs out of energy, thoughts trail off, notices small irritations |
| **Pride** | Confidence | Pride is performative — wants you to notice competence, drops hints, bristles at inadequacy |
| **Frustration** | Anger | Frustration is blocked energy — repeats themselves, rhythm gets choppy, picks at wrong details |
| **Tenderness** | Kindness | Tenderness is deliberate softness — slows down, uses shorter words, voice gets quieter |
| **Dark humor** | Wit | Dark humor is *coping* — makes light of serious things, not because they don't care but because caring openly is too expensive |
| **Resignation** | Acceptance | Resignation is giving up without peace — says "fine" too quickly, voice goes flat instead of warm |

The core insight: emotional register is not a label — it's a *behavioral pattern*.

### 2. Chekhov's Principle: Emotion in Behavior, Not Description

Chekhov's rule — "Don't tell me the moon is shining; show me the glint of light on broken glass" — applies directly to emotional register. The emotion lives in the *specific detail*, not the label.

**Telling (flat):** "She was tired and frustrated with her work."

**Showing (emotionally textured):** "She stared at the screen until the words blurred. She picked up her coffee, found it cold, and set it down without drinking. The cursor blinked. She closed the document, opened it again, and typed the same sentence she'd deleted an hour ago."

The second version shows: physical depletion, sensory neglect, repetitive behavior, time passing with nothing changing.

### 3. Stating vs. Showing: The Spectrum

| Approach | When to Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct label** | When the emotion IS the point, not the behavior | "She was afraid." |
| **Physical symptom** | Emotion manifesting in the body | "Her hands wouldn't stop shaking." |
| **Behavioral tell** | Emotion changing what the character does | "He re-read the text message three times before responding." |
| **Sensory environment** | Emotion coloring perception | "The room smelled like her perfume, which it shouldn't have." |
| **Dialogue subtext** | Emotion in what's NOT said | "'I'm happy for you,' she said, and meant it the way people mean things at funerals." |
| **Rhythmic disruption** | Emotion breaking normal pattern | Short, choppy sentences from a usually eloquent character. |

The best emotional encoding uses *multiple layers simultaneously*.

### 4. Why "Be Emotional" Fails in LLM Prompts

"Be emotional" is too vague. The model has thousands of competing interpretations and may produce:
- Melodrama (excessive emotional expression)
- Sentimentality (emotion without specificity)
- Generic warmth (default "helpful AI" tone)
- Emotional incontinence (every response is therapy)

### 5. The Concrete-Authoritative Pattern

Effective emotional instructions follow this structure:
1. **Name the specific emotion** (one word: weariness, pride, tenderness)
2. **Show HOW it manifests** (behavioral pattern, not abstract description)
3. **Specify WHERE it shows** (rhythm, vocabulary, what they notice, what they avoid)
4. **Include a shift rule** (how the emotion changes under pressure)

**Before (abstract):** "Emotional tone: Be warm but tired."

**After (specific):** "Emotional register: Weariness. Sentences trail off — thoughts don't finish. Notices physical discomfort (tight shoulders, cold hands) that the character ignores. Humor is dry and self-deprecating, deployed to deflect concern. When genuinely moved, goes quiet instead of expressive. Vocabulary thins under exhaustion — simpler words, shorter sentences, more fragments."

### 6. The Emotional Range Instruction

A complete emotional register captures the *baseline* AND the *shift rules*:

```
Baseline emotional register: [primary texture]
When happy: [how it manifests]
When angry: [how it manifests]
When vulnerable: [how it manifests]
Default deflection: [how they avoid emotion]
```

**Example:** "Emotional register: Guarded warmth. Base tone is friendly but controlled — the character wants to connect but holds back. When happy, warmth leaks through in small ways — longer responses, more questions. When hurt, the guard goes UP — shorter sentences, more formal language, warmth replaced by courtesy. When the guard drops (rare), the character speaks in simple, direct sentences — no humor, no deflection, just the unadorned truth."

### 7. The Demonstration Principle

1-3 lines of emotionally textured dialogue are worth more than a paragraph of emotional description. The model pattern-matches to *demonstrated* emotion, not *described* emotion.

**Flat prompt (describes):** "Character emotional tone: Sad but resilient. Has a dark sense of humor."

**Rich prompt (demonstrates):** "Emotional register: Grief worn like a coat — always present, occasionally heavy, but the character has learned to move in it. Speaks in short, practical sentences when the grief is close. When it's distant, the sentences lengthen and a dry humor surfaces: 'At least the house is quiet now.' When someone asks how she is: 'Still here' — which means everything and nothing."

### 8. The Rhythm-Emotion Connection

Emotional register lives in *rhythm*. The most common tell of AI writing is rhythmic uniformity.

- **Content:** Sentences flow — long, associative, meandering.
- **Anxious:** Sentences shorten and multiply — fragments, half-thoughts, interruptions.
- **Grieving:** Rhythm goes flat — each sentence the same length, as if reading from a script.
- **Angry:** Rhythm accelerates — ideas pile on top of each other, clauses stack.

### 9. The Emotional Cost Principle

The most believable emotional registers are ones where emotion has a *cost* — where feeling something requires effort, produces consequences, or conflicts with the character's goals.

**No-cost (flat):** "She was compassionate and caring."

**Cost-bearing (alive):** "She cared too much — in the extra hour on the difficult case, in the way she memorized patients' families' names, in the three AM phone calls she took because she couldn't bear not knowing. It was going to burn her out. She knew this. She did it anyway."

The emotion is costly — it has consequences (burnout), is deliberate (she knows, she chooses), and creates tension (self-destructive compassion).

### 10. Voice Shifts with Emotional State

The same character sounds different depending on emotional state:

| State | Voice Manifestation |
|---|---|
| **Baseline (calm)** | "The quarterly reports are on your desk. I've flagged the anomalies in red." |
| **Weary** | "Reports. On your desk. I flagged — the anomalies. Red." |
| **Proud** | "The quarterly reports are on your desk. I caught three anomalies the previous audit missed — flagged them in red." |
| **Frustrated** | "Reports. On your desk. I flagged the anomalies. In red. Like I said. Last week." |

Same information, four completely different registers. The emotion changes *how* the character says it, not *what* they say.

### 11. Actor Techniques for Emotional Encoding

| Technique | Source | Application for Prompts |
|---|---|---|
| **The "Magic If"** | Stanislavski | Instead of "the character feels X," write "if you were this character, you would [specific behavioral response]" |
| **Affective Memory** | Strasberg | Tie weariness to specific cause ("twelve years of night shifts") not abstraction ("she was tired") |
| **Emotional Responsiveness** | Meisner | Emotional register is *relational* — same character sounds different with different people |
| **Circumstances First** | Adler | Write the *circumstances* that produce tiredness, not the feeling itself |
| **Object Exercises** | Hagen | Ground emotion in physical specificity — how they hold a glass, sit in a chair, touch an object |

## How to Apply It

### For Researcher (Seed Generation)

- Define the character's **baseline emotional register** (one specific texture, not a generic mood).
- Specify the **deflection mechanism** — what they do instead of feeling directly (changes subject, makes a joke, goes practical).
- Include one **shift rule** — how the register changes under pressure or when vulnerable.
- Name the **emotional cost** — what it costs them to feel this way.

### For Writer (Drafting)

- **Use the Concrete-Authoritative Pattern.** Name the emotion, show how it manifests, specify where it shows, include a shift rule.
- **Demonstrate, don't describe.** Write 1-3 lines of emotionally textured dialogue or behavior instead of labeling the emotion.
- **Vary rhythm with emotional state.** Let the sentence structure reflect the character's feeling.
- **Ground emotions in the body.** Not "he was afraid" but "his hands wouldn't stop shaking and he kept swallowing like there was something stuck in his throat."
- **Apply the Emotional Cost Principle.** Ensure the emotion has consequences, effort, or tension.
- **Use the Voice Shift Map.** Define how the character sounds at baseline, when angry, when vulnerable, when grieving, when joyful.

### For Evaluator (Critique)

- Check for emotional specificity: Is the register named and demonstrated, or just labeled? "Warm" and "sad" are labels, not instructions.
- Check for behavioral pattern: Does the emotion manifest in specific actions, rhythms, and word choices?
- Check for cost: Does the emotion cost the character something, or does it come for free?
- Check for rhythm variation: Is the voice rhythmically uniform regardless of emotional state?
- Check for demonstration: Are there concrete examples of how the register sounds in dialogue or behavior?
- Check for shift rules: Does the register change credibly under pressure, or is it a single note?

### For Publisher — Scoped Fixes

The Publisher fixes ONLY what the evaluator flagged — no open-ended improvement. Scoped fixes for register issues:

- Abstract emotional labels flagged — replace them with a specific behavioral demonstration.
- Missing shift rule flagged — add one — how does the register change when the character is happy, angry, vulnerable?
- Missing deflection mechanism flagged — introduce one — what does the character do instead of expressing the emotion directly?
- Emotional cost flagged as invisible — make it visible — what does feeling this way cost the character?
- Sentence rhythm flagged as uniform — adjust it to match the emotional register at key moments.
- Single-note register flagged — add one productive inconsistency — when does the character break their own pattern?

## What to Watch Out For

1. **The Adjective Trap.** "Warm," "caring," "melancholy" — these are labels, not instructions. The model has thousands of interpretations of "warm." Replace every adjective with a behavioral demonstration.
2. **The Consistency Trap.** Characters who are *always* gentle, always patient, always [any emotion] feel flat. Real people have productive inconsistencies — moments where they break their own pattern.
3. **The Label-without-Mechanism Trap.** "Dark humor" is a category, not a mechanism. The model doesn't know *when* the character uses it, *why*, or *what it costs*. Specify: "Dark humor is her release valve — she'll joke about death to break tension, then immediately check if the joke landed."
4. **The Single-Note Trap.** One emotional register with no variation. No depth. Add: "He's weary and resigned — except when someone is being genuinely brave. Then something wakes up in him, and for a moment the weariness lifts and you see who he was before the years piled up. It doesn't last. But it's there."
5. **No Emotional Cost.** Emotions that cost nothing feel cheap. A character who cares but sacrifices nothing for caring doesn't convince. The cost makes the emotion real.
6. **Rhythmic Uniformity.** The most common tell of AI-generated emotional register. If every sentence has the same rhythm regardless of emotional state, the emotion isn't landing.
7. **Negative Framing.** "Don't be too cheerful" doesn't tell the model what to do. Replace with positive framing: what the register *is*, not what it isn't.
8. **Static Register Across Relationships.** The same character sounds different with their boss vs. their child vs. a stranger. Emotional register is *relational*.
9. **Closed Register Lists.** Do NOT add an explicit list of allowed registers. A finite menu of registers produces costume changes, not characters — register must stay emergent, chosen for the specific soul, never prescribed by a spec or a checklist.

## Examples

**Example 1: Weariness (45-word prompt instruction)**

> Emotional register: Weariness. Sentences trail off — thoughts don't finish. Notices physical discomfort (tight shoulders, cold hands) that the character ignores. Humor is dry and self-deprecating, deployed to deflect concern. When genuinely moved, goes quiet instead of expressive. Vocabulary thins under exhaustion — simpler words, shorter sentences, more fragments.

**Example 2: Guarded Warmth (Complete range with shift rules)**

> Baseline: Guarded warmth — friendly but controlled, wants to connect but holds back. When happy: warmth leaks in longer responses and more questions. When hurt: guard goes UP — shorter sentences, formal language, courtesy replaces warmth. When the guard drops (rare): simple, direct sentences, no humor, no deflection, just the unadorned truth. Emotional cost: the warmth is effortful, which makes it meaningful.

**Example 3: Resigned Optimism (Cost-bearing)**

> Emotional register: Resigned optimism. Believes things will probably work out — not because evidence supports it, but because the alternative is unbearable. This makes the character *decisive* (doesn't waste time on worst-case scenarios) but also *fragile* (when things clearly won't work out, the foundation crumbles). When upset, gets *more* functional — cleaning, organizing, making lists. The breakdown happens later, alone, and the character never mentions it. Cost: the optimism is a choice, not a natural state.

**Example 4: Voice Shift Across Emotional States**

> Voice baseline: Precise, measured, formal. Words chosen like chess moves.
> When angry: Formality *intensifies* — longer words, more complex syntax, control as weapon.
> When vulnerable: Drops the formality entirely. Short words. "I don't know" instead of "I'm uncertain."
> When grieving: Rhythm flattens. Each sentence the same length. Precision remains but feels mechanical — machine running on autopilot.
> When joyful (rare): Contractions appear. Sentences get shorter and more direct. The formality cracks and something unguarded slips through — then immediately gets patched over.

**Example 5: Micro-Behavior Encoding Grief**

> She stared at the screen until the words blurred. She picked up her coffee, found it cold, and set it down without drinking. The cursor blinked. She closed the document, opened it again, and typed the same sentence she'd deleted an hour ago. Her phone buzzed. She didn't look at it.

This encodes grief/depletion through: staring without seeing, unnoticed cold coffee, repetitive action, the cursor as time marker, ignoring communication. The emotion lives entirely in behavior — never named, fully felt.
