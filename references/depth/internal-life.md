# Internal Life — Reference

Three ways the unspoken shows up — none of them named:

> Asked how her shift went, the paramedic says: "Three calls. One DOA. The new dispatcher reads the address back wrong every time." She doesn't mention the one she couldn't bring back. She doesn't have to.

> She smelled the smoke before she heard a single voice — brisket, mesquite, someone's grill too hot — and stood at the edge of the yard with her keys already in her hand.

> He described the breakup in perfect paragraphs, subject-verb-object, and offered to make tea while he did it; it was the neatness of it that told you everything.

**What these have in common:** A character's internal life isn't what they *feel* — it's what they *notice*, what they *avoid*, and how the *gap* between what they say and what they mean encodes their unspoken reality. The most alive characters are lenses, not containers: their emotional state filters every word, detail, and rhythm of their narration. The paramedic deflects, the survivor notices, the divorcee tidies — the feeling is never stated, and that is exactly what makes it readable. Complaint is one vitality channel, not the gate (v5.2.1): these characters carry their interior through noticing, avoidance, and subtext — not through griping.

**What doesn't work:** "She felt sad and anxious about her mother's condition." A stated emotion does the reader's work for them — and a reader with nothing to infer stops caring. Labels flatten interior; gaps create it.

## What the Research Says

### 1. Subtext: The Structural Gap

Subtext is not mystery for its own sake. It's the structural gap between four layers:

1. **What a character says** (surface dialogue)
2. **What they mean** (the real message)
3. **What they do** (actions that contradict or complicate the words)
4. **What they notice** (selective attention that reveals priorities)

**Example:** "I'm fine" spoken while aggressively cleaning the kitchen. The surface says fine. The meaning says devastated. The action contradicts. The noticing (the chip in the mug) reveals hidden resentment. The reader gets all four layers; the character names none of them.

### 2. Five Techniques for Subtext in Constrained Prompts

| Technique | How It Works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **The Deflection** | Character answers a different question | "How was the funeral?" / "The sandwiches were terrible." |
| **The Over-Specific Detail** | Character fixates on trivia instead of the emotional core | "The house was fine. The wallpaper in the hallway was peeling. I didn't go upstairs." |
| **The Contradictory Action** | Character says one thing, does another — action is always truth | "I don't care about the promotion." / *Re-reads rejection email for the fourth time.* |
| **The Loaded Silence** | Character doesn't respond to the emotional part | "Do you still love me?" / *Asked about the weather for the rest of the drive.* |
| **The Redirect to Practicality** | Character converts emotional content into logistics | "I'm leaving you." / "Who gets the car?" |

### 3. Showing Worldview Through Selective Perception

A character's worldview is encoded not in what they *say* they believe, but in what they *notice* and what they *ignore*. This is the most powerful tool for encoding interiority invisibly.

**Four forces shaping perception:**

- **Profession:** An architect notices load-bearing walls; a soldier scans for exits; a poet tracks how light hits surfaces. Training rewires attention.
- **Trauma:** A fire survivor notices smoke before anyone else. An abandonment-wounded character tracks who's about to leave — the glance at the door, the half-second pause before answering.
- **Values:** A justice-oriented character reads every situation as a moral equation (who has power, who's exploited). A beauty-oriented character sees composition and color.
- **Desire:** A hungry character notices food smells and restaurant signs. A lonely character sees couples and empty chairs.

**The core principle:** "Sarah was a cautious person" is a label. "Sarah enters a new apartment and the first thing she notices is whether the windows lock from the inside" is a worldview in action.

### 4. Feeling Visible in Voice (Not Just Stated)

The critical distinction: a character who "has feelings" (reader is told: "she was angry") vs. a character whose feelings are *visible in their voice* (emotional state filters sentence rhythm, word choice, detail selection).

**Sentence rhythm as emotional fingerprint:**

- **Anger:** Short, clipped sentences. Fragments. Periods where commas should be. "He came home late. Again. She didn't ask where."
- **Grief:** Long, trailing sentences that lose momentum. Dashes, ellipses, incomplete thoughts.
- **Anxiety:** Rapid, jumping thoughts. Parenthetical asides. Sentences that start over.
- **Denial:** Formal, detached language for devastating events.
- **Suppressed anger:** Overly polite phrasing.

**Word choice as emotional fingerprint:** A depressed character uses more words related to weight, darkness, heaviness, stasis. An excited character uses motion, light, expansion. A frightened character uses distance, walls, enclosure.

### 5. Hemingway's Technique: Emotion Through Restraint

Hemingway's genius: making emotion visible through *what the voice refuses to say*. Characters don't describe feelings — they describe the world with such precision that feeling emerges from the *pattern* of attention. In "A Clean, Well-Lighted Place," the old waiter's insistence on light and cleanliness encodes a fight against nothingness — the story never says "loneliness" or "despair."

### 6. Pratchett's Technique: Emotion Through Comic Distance

Characters process emotion by making jokes about it. The gap between the humor and the underlying pain creates the emotional weight. In *Night Watch*, Sam Vines processes grief by focusing on operational details — clinical, practical, almost bored. The reader *feels* the grief precisely because the voice is working so hard not to feel it.

### 7. The Three-Layer Technique for Short-Form

- **Layer 1 — The Surface:** A concrete, specific action or scene.
- **Layer 2 — The Filter:** What details does the character select from this moment?
- **Layer 3 — The Voice:** Rhythm, word choice, what the voice avoids.

### 8. Seven Modes of Relationship to Work

A character's relationship to their work reveals identity:

| Mode | Voice Marker | Short-Form Tell |
|---|---|---|
| **Pride** | Detailed craft descriptions, defensiveness | "He ran his thumb along the cabinet joint. 'Whoever did this used dovetails. Good ones.' He said it the way other men say 'I love you.'" |
| **Weariness** | Past tense for feelings, present for routine | "She'd been a nurse for thirty years. She could still find a vein in the dark. She just couldn't remember why that used to matter." |
| **Dark Humor** | Comic observations about serious situations | "The coroner said 'natural causes.' Dave said that was like calling a building collapse 'structural relaxation.'" |
| **Protectiveness** | Aggressive when work is threatened | "When the landlord said the clinic had to close, Maren smiled. It wasn't a nice smile. 'You can try,' she said." |
| **Resignation** | Flat, detached tone, no anger | "He filed the reports. They went into the system. The system ignored them. He filed them again on Monday." |
| **Obsessive Love** | Intensity, fixation, distorted time | "She looked up from the microscope. The lab was dark. She hadn't noticed the cleaning staff come and go." |
| **Reluctant Duty** | Distancing language, competence without enthusiasm | "His father had been a butcher. So had his grandfather. He could break down a side of beef in twelve minutes. He'd never wanted to." |

### 9. Non-Griping Archetypes That Are Still Alive

Griping creates the illusion of personality through complaint, but it's passive. Active engagement creates deeper internal life:

- **The Optimist Who's Wrong A Lot** — tries, fails, tries again. Dramatic irony from the gap between their hope and reader's knowledge.
- **The Builder** — defines themselves by what they create, not what they endure. Direction is life.
- **The Quiet Professional** — does the work with care. Competence *is* characterization.
- **The Curious Observer** — asks questions, notices things. Curiosity is inherently active.
- **The Stubborn One** — refuses to give up by reflex. Friction with the world creates story.
- **The Caretaker** — identity defined by what they nurture. Care is inherently specific and active.
- **The Competent Pragmatist** — assesses, decides, acts. No drama, just decisive action.

## How to Apply It

### For Researcher (Seed Generation)

- Surface the "first-thing-noticed" for the character. What do they register when entering any space?
- Identify the character's blind spot — what they *don't* notice reveals as much as what they do.
- Note the character's relationship to their work (one of the seven modes).
- Define the deflection mechanism — what do they do instead of feeling directly?

### For Writer (Drafting)

- **Before writing any character passage, ask:**
  1. What does this character notice first? (worldview)
  2. What do they avoid noticing? (blind spots/denial)
  3. What's the one thing they'd never say out loud? (subtext)
  4. What does their voice sound like when they're tired? (emotional baseline)
  5. What's the contradiction between what they say and what they do? (complexity)
  6. What's their relationship to their work? (identity)
  7. What do they do instead of complaining? (active engagement)

- **Apply the Substitution Principle:** Every time you're tempted to *state* an emotion, *replace* it with a concrete detail that implies it:
  - "She was anxious" → "She checked her phone three times crossing the parking lot"
  - "He was angry" → "He closed the door more carefully than usual"
  - "She was lonely" → "She left the radio on when she left the house"

- **Vary sentence rhythm** to match emotional state. Let the voice *feel* the emotion through its structure.

### For Evaluator (Critique)

- Check for subtext: Is there a gap between what the character says and what the reader understands?
- Check for selective perception: Does the character notice things that reveal their profession, trauma, values, or desire?
- Check for voice encoding: Does the character's emotional state affect their sentence rhythm and word choice, or is the voice uniform regardless of state?
- Check for the substitution principle: Are emotions stated (labeled) or shown (through specific actions)?
- Check for active engagement: Is the character a passive observer/complainer, or do they do things that reveal their interior?

### For Publisher-Side Refinement

- Add one concrete detail of selective perception — what the character notices that someone else wouldn't.
- Introduce one subtext technique: deflection, over-specific detail, contradictory action, loaded silence, or redirect to practicality.
- Adjust sentence rhythm to match the character's emotional baseline.
- Replace stated emotions with the Substitution Principle equivalents.
- Ensure the character's blind spot is visible — what they won't or can't see.

## What to Watch Out For

1. **Stated Emotions Instead of Demonstrated Ones.** "She was sad" tells the reader what to think. "She picked up the coffee she'd already finished and stared at the empty cup" forces the reader to infer — and inference is engagement.
2. **Uniform Voice Regardless of Emotional State.** A character who sounds exactly the same when happy, angry, grieving, and at ease is not a character — they're a text-to-speech engine. Voice must shift.
3. **Characters Who Notice Nothing.** If the character walks through the world without registering specific details, they have no internal life. Every character should notice *something* — and that something reveals who they are.
4. **Over-Explained Feelings.** Characters who constantly narrate their emotional state (thinking "I'm so angry" or "Why am I so sad?") feel like they're in therapy, not in a story. Let behavior carry the weight.
5. **Griping as Personality.** Griping is passive complaint. Characters who only gripe feel stagnant. Give them an active mode of engagement — building, fixing, questioning, protecting, creating.
6. **No Subtext.** If every character says exactly what they mean, the reader has no work to do. Subtext is the gap that the reader fills — and filling it is what makes the character feel real.
7. **Elaborate Avoidance (Hemingway's lesson).** If the character's voice is working *hard* to avoid feeling, the reader feels the suppressed emotion more intensely than if it were stated. Use this.

## Examples

**Example 1: Subtext Through Deflection**

> "How was the funeral?"
> "The sandwiches were terrible."
> *Didn't mention the coffin. Didn't mention the eulogy. Didn't mention that she held her breath through the entire service because breathing felt like the wrong thing to do.*

The deflection (sandwiches instead of emotional content) encodes avoidance. The buried details in the third sentence reveal the truth the character won't state directly.

**Example 2: Selective Perception Encoding Worldview**

> Marcus set his lunchbox on the counter without opening it. The kitchen smelled like the soap his wife used — lavender, the cheap kind from the dollar store that she bought because it reminded her of her grandmother's house. He'd never told her he hated lavender. He sat in his chair and listened to the house settle around him, the way old houses do, like they're thinking about giving up.

- **Worldview revealed:** Marcus is exhausted, tolerating things he dislikes for his wife's sake, emotionally heavy.
- **What he notices:** The lavender soap (relationship detail), the settling house (projection of his own state onto environment).
- **What he doesn't do:** Open the lunchbox, address his feelings, act.
- **Voice encoding:** The long, trailing sentences carry weariness. The house "thinking about giving up" is the character's own feeling externalized.

**Example 3: Work Relationship as Identity**

> She'd been a nurse for thirty years. She could still find a vein in the dark. She just couldn't remember why that used to matter.

- **Mode:** Weariness.
- **Mechanism:** The contrast between competence (finding a vein in the dark — unconscious skill) and meaning (forgetting why it mattered — depletion of purpose). The voice is flat, matter-of-fact, which *is* the weariness.

**Example 4: Voice Encoding Emotion Through Rhythm**

> The door was locked (was it always locked? she couldn't remember) and the hallway smelled wrong, not bad exactly, just — different. She checked her phone. No messages. She checked it again. Still no messages. She put her key in the lock and realized she'd already unlocked the door.

- **Emotion:** Anxiety.
- **Voice markers:** Parenthetical asides (jumping thoughts), short fragments ("No messages. Still no messages."), repetition (checking phone twice), the realization that she's already done something she doesn't remember doing.
