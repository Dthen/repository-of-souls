# Name Memorability: Cognitive Science of Name Recall

*Research compiled for application to fictional character naming*

---

## 1. The Baker-Baker Paradox: Why Names Are Inherently Hard

The most fundamental finding in name memory research is the **Baker/baker paradox** (Cohen, 1990). If told someone's occupation is "baker," you'll remember it — you can picture them kneading dough, smell the bread, link to other bakers you know. But if told their *name* is "Baker," it's arbitrary — there's nothing to connect it to.

Proper names are **arbitrary labels** with no semantic content tied to the person. They resist the deep encoding strategies (imagery, association, elaboration) that make other information sticky. This is the core challenge of name memorability.

**Key implication for fiction:** Give character names *something to latch onto* — a sound, a feeling, a visual — to partially overcome this semantic void.

> Sources: Cohen (1990); Valentine, Bredart, Lawson & Ward (1991); [Brédart, 2017, "The cognitive psychology and neuroscience of naming people"](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/215164/1/NBBR2017.pdf)

## 2. The Distinctiveness Effect

Research consistently shows that **distinctive names are better remembered** than common or typical names.

- Studies on forename recognition memory found that distinctive (unusual) names are recognized significantly better than typical (common) names. This is part of the broader **von Restorff effect** — items that stand out from their context receive enhanced memory encoding. (Hunt & Worthen, 2006; Gerhardstein & Shyi, 2012)

- Distinctiveness affects not just the name itself but the **strength of the face-name association** — distinctive names make it easier to remember who belongs to which name. (Shirahatti et al., 2012)

**Key implication for fiction:** Unique/unusual character names will be more memorable than common ones — *Katniss* sticks better than *Katherine*. But there's a tradeoff: too exotic and readers can't pronounce or process them.

> Sources: Valentine & Moore (2005), "The distinctiveness effect in forenames"; Hunt & Worthen (2006), *Distinctiveness and Memory*, Oxford University Press; Gerhardstein & Shyi (2012), PubMed

## 3. Information Theory & Name Structure

Dye, Johns, Jones & Ramscar (2016) demonstrated that **name memorability follows information-theoretic principles**:

- **Uniform entropy rate** is optimal: when identifying information is distributed evenly across name elements, recall is best. A name where each part contributes roughly equal "uniqueness" is easier to remember than one where all the information is front-loaded or back-loaded.

- **Deviations from uniform entropy impair memory** — even for visual stimuli (faces) paired with the names. This was shown in artificial grammar experiments where optimal-structure names were recalled significantly faster (`t(15) = -3.36, p = .002`) and more accurately (`t(15) = 3.91, p < .001`).

- When the structure of a name system becomes chaotic (too many similar-sounding names, no semantic cues), people fall back on **frequency-based retrieval** — a simpler, less efficient strategy. This is why groups with more diverse name pools sometimes struggle with recall tasks.

**Key implication for fiction:** Spread distinctive information across the whole name. "Sherlock Holmes" works because both parts are distinctive. "John Smith" fails because "John" and "Smith" are both generic — the entropy is too low. A first-last combination where each element is somewhat unusual creates better memorability.

> Source: Dye, Johns, Jones & Ramscar (2016), "The Structure of Names in Memory: Deviations from Uniform Entropy Impair Memory," *Proceedings of the Cognitive Science Society*

## 4. Sound Symbolism & Phonological Iconicity

Names aren't just arbitrary — their **sounds carry meaning associations**:

### Sound-meaning mapping in fiction
Papantoniou & Konstantopoulos (2016) analyzed 409 films and found that phonological features alone could predict whether a character was a hero or villain with **82% accuracy** (F-score 0.823). The single most predictive feature category was **phonology** (plosives, fricatives, vowel patterns).

Key patterns:
- **Plosives** (p, b, t, d, k, g): Associated with hardness, power, directness
- **Fricatives** (f, v, s, z, sh): Associated with sneakiness, danger, ambiguity
- **Nasals** (m, n, ng): Associated with softness, warmth
- **Front vowels** (i, e): Associated with smallness, lightness, positivity
- **Back/low vowels** (a, o, u): Associated with largeness, darkness

### The "Kiki-Bouba" effect
Cross-culturally, people associate sharp-sounding names (with plosives and front vowels) with angular shapes and hard objects, and round-sounding names (with nasals and back vowels) with curves and softness. This extends to personality attributions.

### Valence sound symbolism
Recent research (Journal of Cognition, 2024) found that names with /i/ sounds are perceived as suiting positive faces, while other vowel qualities map to negative or neutral faces. Sound symbolism in names is real and measurable.

**Key implication for fiction:** Choose name sounds that match character traits. Villains benefit from harsher consonants (K, hard G, sharp S). Heroes benefit from more flowing sounds. This is a subtle but powerful tool.

> Sources: Papantoniou & Konstantopoulos (2016), "Unravelling Names of Fictional Characters," ACL Anthology; Ramachandran & Hubbard (2001), kiki-bouba effect; Journal of Cognition (2024), valence sound symbolism

## 5. Practical Principles for Fictional Character Names

Drawing from the cognitive science research:

### 5.1 Differentiation is paramount
- **Use different first letters** for main characters (Anne R. Allen, writing craft advice). Same-letter names (Sam/Sarah/Steve) cause reader confusion.
- **Vary syllable count and stress patterns.** A cast of three-syllable names with stress on the first syllable will blur together.

### 5.2 Pronounceability matters
- If readers can't pronounce a name, they'll skip it — and skipped names aren't encoded into memory. Audiobook format makes this critical. (Allen, 2021)

### 5.3 Semantic anchoring helps overcome the Baker paradox
- Names that evoke a *feeling*, *image*, or *association* are easier to remember than purely arbitrary ones. "Katniss" sounds like a plant (and is one). "Hermione" sounds erudite. "Gandalf" has Norse associations.
- This partially solves the Baker/baker problem — give the name an anchor.

### 5.4 Avoid the generic pool
- Agents and readers report fatigue with overused names (Jake, Jack, Catherine, Kate). Generic names have low distinctiveness and poor memorability. (Allen, 2021)

### 5.5 Fit name to setting and era
- A modern teenager named "Mildred" or a Roman named "Jamesus" breaks immersion and actually *impedes* memorability because the mismatch creates processing interference. (Allen, 2021)

### 5.6 The "distinctiveness sweet spot"
- Too common = forgettable (low entropy)
- Too exotic = unpronounceable, skipped over (overloaded)
- The sweet spot: **unusual enough to stand out, familiar enough to process fluently.** This aligns with the processing fluency theory — stimuli that are moderately easy to process are rated most positively and remembered best.

### 5.7 Named characters = significant characters
- Naming a walk-on character (cab driver, barista) signals to readers that they're important. Naming too many characters dilutes the memorability of each one. Only name characters who matter. (Allen, 2021)

## 6. Summary of Cognitive Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Effect on Memorability | Application |
|---|---|---|
| **Semantic encoding** (Baker/baker) | Names lack built-in meaning → hard to remember | Give names phonetic personality; use distinctive sounds |
| **Distinctiveness** (von Restorff) | Unique items get enhanced encoding | Avoid common names; aim for the "sweet spot" |
| **Uniform entropy** | Evenly-spread info = easier recall | Both first and last name should be distinctive |
| **Sound symbolism** | Sounds carry unconscious meaning | Match phonetics to character personality |
| **Processing fluency** | Easy-to-process = more liked + remembered | Keep names pronounceable |
| **Differentiation** | Similar items interfere in memory | Vary first letters, syllable counts, stress patterns |
