## 1. Sound Symbolism: The Non-Arbitrary Nature of Names

Sound symbolism is the perceptual similarity between speech sounds and concept meanings — a form of linguistic iconicity that challenges the Saussurean dogma that the linguistic sign is entirely arbitrary.

### Historical Foundation

- **Plato, *Cratylus*** (c. 360 BCE): Socrates argues that certain letters naturally express certain qualities — *rho* (ρ) expresses motion, *lambda* (λ) expresses smoothness. Plato later hedges with counterexamples, but the intuition persists.
- **Saussure** (1916): Established "the arbitrariness of the sign" as foundational to modern linguistics, suppressing sound symbolism research for most of the 20th century.
- **Sapir** (1929): Demonstrated that pseudoword *mil* is perceived as smaller than *mal* — the first modern empirical evidence of magnitude symbolism.

### Core Sound Symbolism Types

| Type | Mechanism | Examples |
|------|-----------|----------|
| **Onomatopoeia** | Direct sound imitation | *bow-wow*, *meow*, *splash* |
| **Ideophones** | Open lexical class depicting sensory imagery | Japanese *kira-kira* (shiny), Korean *mallang-mallang* (soft) |
| **Phonaesthemes** | Sub-morphemic sound-meaning associations | English *gl-* → light/vision (*gleam*, *glow*, *glare*, *glint*) |
| **Magnitude symbolism** | Vowel height/position → size perception | Front vowels (/i/) = small; back/low vowels (/a/, /u/) = large |
| **Deictic symbolism** | Proximal/distal → vowel quality | *this*/*that*, French *ceci*/*cela*, Finnish *tämä*/*tuo* |

**Key cross-linguistic findings** (Blasi et al., 2016; Joo, 2020; Johansson et al., 2020): Across 4,000+ languages, nasal sounds (/m/, /n/) are statistically associated with words for "breast," rounded vowels (/o/) with "knee," lateral consonants (/l/) with "tongue."

> **Citations:**
> - Blasi, D. E., et al. (2016). Sound–meaning association biases evidenced across thousands of languages. *PNAS*, 113(39), 10818–10823.
> - Sapir, E. (1929). A study in phonetic symbolism. *Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 12(3), 225–239.
> - Joo, I. (2020). Phonosemantic biases in basic vocabulary. *Diachronica*, 37(1).

## 2. The Bouba/Kiki Effect: Sound-Shape Mapping Is Pre-Linguistic

The bouba/kiki effect (Ramachandran & Hubbard, 2001) — building on Köhler's *takete*/*maluma* (1929) and Uznadze's earlier work (1924) — is the most robust finding in sound symbolism:

- **95% of participants** label a jagged shape "kiki" and a rounded shape "bouba" (Ramachandran & Hubbard, 2001).
- **Cross-culturally robust**: Confirmed across 25 languages and 10 writing systems (Ćwiek et al., 2022).
- **Pre-linguistic**: 4-month-old infants show the mapping; 2.5-year-old toddlers show strong preference (Maurer et al.).
- **Cross-species**: Newly hatched baby chickens (1–3 days old) exhibit the effect (University of Padua, 2026), suggesting it is **innate and cross-modal**, not uniquely human.
- **Extends to real names**: "Molly" is perceived as round; "Kate" as sharp. Easygoing personality traits are associated with round-sounding names, determined traits with sharp-sounding names.
- **Positive/negative valence**: "Bouba" words are associated with positive concepts, "kiki" with negative ones.

### Mechanism

- **Articulatory**: The mouth makes a rounded shape for "bouba" and a taut, angular shape for "kiki."
- **Acoustic**: Round objects produce lower, continuous frequencies; spiky objects produce higher, jagged frequencies (Fort & Schwartz, 2022).
- **Neurological**: Mismatching stimuli (bouba + spiky) activate prefrontal cortex and parietal regions more strongly, suggesting the effect operates at early perceptual stages (Peiffer-Smadja & Cohen, 2019; McCormick et al.).

### Limitations

- Effect is **weaker or absent** in congenitally blind individuals.
- Some languages (Mandarin Chinese, Turkish, Romanian, Albanian) showed lower-than-50% match rates in one study (2021), though methodology was debated.
- Breaks down when sounds don't form legal words in the listener's language.

> **Citations:**
> - Ramachandran, V. S. & Hubbard, E. M. (2001). Synaesthesia—a window into perception, thought and language. *Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 8(12), 3–34.
> - Ćwiek, A., et al. (2022). The bouba/kiki effect is robust across 25 languages. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*, 377(1841).
> - Köhler, W. (1929). *Gestalt Psychology*. New York: Liveright.
> - Fort, M. & Schwartz, J.-L. (2022). Resolving the bouba/kiki effect. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 26(1).

## 3. Phonetic Symbolism in Brand Naming

### Klink's Framework (2000, 2003)

Richard R. Klink's foundational work established that the **sound** of a brand name — independent of its semantic meaning — systematically influences consumer perceptions:

| Feature | Phoneme Type | Perceptual Association |
|---------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Front vowels** (/i/, /e/) | — | Small, fast, light, bright, feminine, pleasant |
| **Back vowels** (/a/, /o/, /u/) | — | Large, slow, heavy, dark, masculine, unpleasant |
| **Stop consonants** (k, p, t) | — | Harsh, sharp, angular, masculine |
| **Fricative consonants** (s, f, z) | — | Soft, gentle, smooth, feminine |

Klink (2003) was notably the first to integrate **consonants** into sound symbolism analysis; prior work focused almost exclusively on vowels.

> **Citations:**
> - Klink, R. R. (2000). Creating brand names with meaning: The use of sound symbolism. *Marketing Letters*, 11(1), 5–20.
> - Klink, R. R. (2003). Creating meaningful new brand names: A study of semantics and sound symbolism. *Journal of Marketing Theory and Practice*, 11(2), 27–34.

### The Congruence Principle (Lowrey & Shrum, 2007)

The most replicated finding: brand names are preferred when the attributes connoted by their vowel sounds **match** the product positioning. A front-vowel name is preferred for a convertible (small, fast); a back-vowel name for a large SUV (heavy, substantial).

- **Method**: Fictitious brand pairs differing only in vowel sound (e.g., "BILVA" vs. "BULVA").
- **Result**: Significant preference for congruent sound-meaning pairings (Lowrey & Shrum, 2007, *Journal of Consumer Research*, cited by 436).
- **Cross-linguistic**: Effect replicates across English and French (Lowrey, Lerman, & Luna, 2007).

> **Citations:**
> - Lowrey, T. M. & Shrum, L. J. (2007). Phonetic symbolism and brand name preference. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 34(3), 406–414.
> - Shrum, L. J., Lowrey, T. M., Luna, D., Lerman, D. B., & Liu, M. (2012). Sound symbolism effects across languages: Implications for global brand names. *International Journal of Research in Marketing*, 29(3), 275–279.

### Yorkston & Menon (2004) — Landmark JCR Paper

Demonstrated that the sound of a brand name (specifically vowel phonemes) influences consumer judgments of product attributes **automatically** and **independently of semantic content**. The effect is robust to replication (Baxter, Kulczynski, & Ilicic, 2014).

> **Citations:**
> - Yorkston, E. & Menon, G. (2004). A sound idea: Phonetic effects of brand names on consumer judgments. *Journal of Consumer Research*, 31(1), 43–51.

### Tactile Sound Symbolism (Winter et al., 2022)

Bilabial consonants (/p/, /b/, /m/) are associated with softness (linked to lip texture); trilled /r/ is associated with roughness. This extends sound symbolism into tactile domains.

> **Citations:**
> - Winter, B., et al. (2022). The sound of softness. *Cognition*, 218, 104931.

## 4. Name Perception: How People Read Names

### Processing Fluency and Name Likeability

Names that are easier to pronounce are perceived more favorably:

- **Alter & Oppenheimer (2008)**: Cities whose names are processed fluently seem nearer and more concrete. Fluency reduces psychological distance.
- **Oppenheimer (2008)**: Seminal review in *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* linking fluency to level of abstractness.
- **Roche, Shrum & Lowrey (2015)**: Applied processing fluency research to brand name design — distinguishing between form, fit, and fluency as design dimensions.

**Practical implication**: Names should be phonotactically legal in the target language and avoid unusual consonant clusters or unfamiliar vowel patterns that reduce fluency.

> **Citations:**
> - Alter, A. L. & Oppenheimer, D. M. (2008). Effects of fluency on psychological distance and mental construal. *Psychological Science*, 19(2), 161–167.
> - Roche, S., Shrum, L. J., & Lowrey, T. M. (2015). The aesthetics of brand name design: Form, fit, and fluency. In *The Psychology of Design*.

### Linguistic Characteristics and Memory

- **Lowrey, Shrum, & Dubitsky (2003)**: Specific phonetic features (initial plosives, paranomasia/wordplay, semantic associations) enhance brand name memorability.
- **Lerman (2020)**: Brand name meaning operates at multiple linguistic levels — phonological, morphological, and semantic — and names need not be literal to be meaningful.

> **Citations:**
> - Lowrey, T. M., Shrum, L. J., & Dubitsky, T. M. (2003). The relation between brand-name linguistic characteristics and brand-name memory. *Journal of Advertising*, 32(3), 7–17.
> - Lerman, D. B. (2020). Phonology, morphology, and semantics: Toward a fuller conceptualization of brand name meaning. In *Psycholinguistic Phenomena in Marketing*. Taylor & Francis.

### Sound Symbolism Moderated by Familiarity

- **Pogacar (2018)**: Semantic appositeness (how well a name's sound fits its meaning) is positively correlated with memorability **for familiar brands** but not for unfamiliar ones. This suggests the "hop test" — conceptual fit — becomes more important as names gain exposure.

> **Citations:**
> - Pogacar, R. (2018). *Marketing Language: A Conceptual Framework and Examination of How Brand Name Linguistics Influence Brand Loyalty*. Doctoral dissertation.

## 5. Semantic Distance in Naming: The "Hop Test"

### The Core Question

Is there an optimal conceptual distance between a name and its referent? Do names that sit 1–2 conceptual hops from a meaning work better than either literal names (0 hops) or completely opaque names (many hops)?

### Evidence From Analogy & Creativity Research

The strongest evidence comes from **Design-by-Analogy (DbA)** research, which has extensively studied the relationship between conceptual distance and creative quality:

#### The Inverted-U Hypothesis

**Chan, Fu, Schunn, Cagan, & Wood (2011)** (cited by 420): Landmark paper establishing that analogical distance follows an **inverted-U curve** for creative output:
- **Near-field analogies** (0–1 hops): High surface overlap → easier to use but risk **fixation** (cognitive entrenchment).
- **Far-field analogies** (3+ hops): Low overlap → novel but often **incomprehensible** or inapplicable.
- **Moderate-field analogies** (1–2 hops): Best balance of novelty and usability.

> "Analogies closer to the near-field endpoint share a significant number of surface features. The potential for creative insight increases with distance, but so does the risk of irrelevance."

> **Citations:**
> - Chan, J., Fu, K., Schunn, C., Cagan, J., & Wood, K. (2011). On the benefits and pitfalls of analogies for innovative design: Ideation performance based on analogical distance, commonness, and modality of examples. *Journal of Mechanical Design*, 133(8), 081004.

**Fu, Chan, Cagan, et al. (2013)** (cited by 376): Further refined the "near" vs. "far" definitions, showing that database structuring and conceptual framing influence what counts as moderate distance.

> **Citations:**
> - Fu, K., Chan, J., Cagan, J., Kotovsky, K., Schunn, C., & Wood, K. (2013). The meaning of "near" and "far": The impact of structuring design databases and the effect of distance of analogy on design output. *Journal of Mechanical Design*, 135(10), 101007.

**Jia, Becattini, Cascini, & Tan (2020)** (cited by 31): Tested the inverted-U on large designer samples. The effect is moderated by skill level — trained designers benefit more from moderate-distance analogies.

> **Citations:**
> - Jia, X., Becattini, N., Cascini, G., & Tan, R. (2020). Testing ideation performance on a large set of designers: Effects of analogical distance. *Design Science*, 6, e10.

#### Nooteboom's Optimal Cognitive Distance (2000)

Bart Nooteboom's highly influential theory (cited 1,244+ for the core paper, 2,334 for the 2007 empirical test) formalizes the **inverted-U relationship** between cognitive distance and learning/innovation:

- **Too little distance**: No novelty, nothing to learn.
- **Too much distance**: Cannot understand or absorb the other domain's knowledge.
- **Optimal distance**: Large enough for variety/novelty, small enough for absorptive capacity.

> "Optimal collaboration requires a trade-off between the upside and the downside of cognitive distance."

This framework directly maps to naming: a literal name (zero distance) has no novelty; an opaque name (too much distance) has no meaning; a metaphorical name at moderate distance offers both insight and accessibility.

> **Citations:**
> - Nooteboom, B. (2000). Learning by interaction: Absorptive capacity, cognitive distance and governance. *Journal of Management and Governance*, 4, 69–92.
> - Nooteboom, B., Van Haverbeke, W., Duysters, G., Gilsing, V., & van den Oord, A. (2007). Optimal cognitive distance and absorptive capacity. *Research Policy*, 36(7), 1016–1034.

#### Ait El Houssi, Morel, & Hultink (2004) — Naming & New Product Communication

Confirmed the "Goldilocks principle" for new product naming: communication of product benefits works best at **optimal moderate conceptual distance**. Too literal = boring, undifferentiated; too abstract = meaningless, forgettable.

> **Citations:**
> - Ait El Houssi, A., Morel, K. P., & Hultink, E. J. (2004). Effectively communicating new product benefits to consumers: The use of analogies versus direct claims. *NA — Advances in Consumer Research*, 31.

### Evidence From Metaphor Research

#### Aptness > Novelty (Chiappe, Kennedy, & Chiappe, 2003)

In metaphor comprehension, **aptness** (the degree to which a comparison captures a genuine similarity) is a stronger predictor of preference than novelty or comprehensibility alone. This aligns with the hop test: the "right" name isn't the most novel or the most obvious — it's the one that captures genuine structural similarity at moderate distance.

> **Citations:**
> - Chiappe, D. L., Kennedy, J. M., & Chiappe, P. (2003). Aptness is more important than comprehensibility in preference for metaphors and similes. *Poetics*, 31(1), 51–68.

#### The Moderation Effect (Blasko & Connine, 1993)

Metaphor processing depends on the interaction of **familiarity** and **aptness**. Moderately novel but apt metaphors are processed as categorizations (fast, efficient); highly novel metaphors require comparison (slower, more effortful). For naming, this suggests names at 1–2 hops benefit from being processed as efficient categorizations while still offering novelty.

> **Citations:**
> - Blasko, D. G. & Connine, C. M. (1993). Effects of familiarity and aptness on metaphor processing. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 19(2), 295–308.

### Construal Level Theory & Naming

**Trope & Liberman (2010)** (cited 9,914): Construal Level Theory establishes that psychological distance affects abstraction. Distant objects are construed abstractly; near objects concretely.

For naming, this means:
- **Concrete/literal names** (0 hops): High fluency, low novelty, risk of being generic.
- **Abstract/opaque names** (3+ hops): High novelty, low fluency, risk of being meaningless.
- **Metaphorical names** (1–2 hops): Moderate construal — abstract enough to be interesting, concrete enough to be understood.

**Connors, Khamitov, & Thomson (2021)** (cited by 133) directly refutes "closer is better" for brand relationships, introducing the concept of **optimal social psychological distance** for brands. This is the "Goldilocks zone" applied to brand perception.

> **Citations:**
> - Trope, Y. & Liberman, N. (2010). Construal-level theory of psychological distance. *Psychological Review*, 117(2), 440–463.
> - Connors, S., Khamitov, M., & Thomson, M. (2021). They're just not that into you: How to leverage existing consumer–brand relationships through social psychological distance. *Journal of Marketing*, 85(6), 1–18.

### Processing Fluency Meets Semantic Distance

The key insight synthesizing these streams: **processing fluency** (how easily a name is processed) and **semantic distance** (how far the name is from its referent) interact to determine name effectiveness.

- **Alter & Oppenheimer (2008)**: Fluently processed names seem "closer" and more concrete.
- **Tsai & Thomas (2011)**: The effect of fluency is moderated by abstract vs. concrete thinking.

**The optimal name** is one that:
1. Is phonetically fluent (easy to say, remember, and process)
2. Sits 1–2 conceptual hops from its meaning (novel enough to be interesting, close enough to be understood)
3. Has sound symbolism congruent with the intended associations
4. Is apt rather than merely clever (captures genuine structural similarity)

> **Citations:**
> - Alter, A. L. & Oppenheimer, D. M. (2008). Effects of fluency on psychological distance and mental construal. *Psychological Science*, 19(2), 161–167.
> - Tsai, C. I. & Thomas, M. (2011). When does feeling of fluency matter? How abstract and concrete thinking influence fluency effects. *Psychological Science*, 22(3), 348–354.

## 6. Synthesis: The Hop Test — Does Research Back It Up?

### Verdict: **Yes, with nuance.**

The "hop test" — the idea that names at 1–2 conceptual hops from their referent work best — is well-supported by convergent evidence from multiple research streams:

| Research Stream | Key Finding | Hop Test Alignment |
|----------------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Design-by-Analogy** (Chan et al., 2011; Fu et al., 2013) | Inverted-U: moderate analogical distance produces best creative output | ✅ Directly supports 1–2 hops |
| **Optimal Cognitive Distance** (Nooteboom, 2000, 2007) | Inverted-U: moderate cognitive distance for best learning/innovation | ✅ Directly supports 1–2 hops |
| **Metaphor Aptness** (Chiappe et al., 2003; Blasko & Connine, 1993) | Apt > novel or comprehensible; moderate novelty processed most efficiently | ✅ Supports moderate distance |
| **Construal Level Theory** (Trope & Liberman, 2010) | Moderate psychological distance → balanced construal | ✅ Supports moderate abstraction |
| **Processing Fluency** (Oppenheimer, 2008; Alter & Oppenheimer, 2008) | Fluency reduces psychological distance; names must be processable | ⚠️ Constrains upper bound of hops |
| **Sound Symbolism** (Klink, 2000; Lowrey & Shrum, 2007) | Congruence between sound and meaning drives preference | ✅ Sound = independent "hop" dimension |
| **Brand Name Memory** (Lowrey, Shrum, & Dubitsky, 2003; Pogacar, 2018) | Semantic appositeness helps memorability for familiar brands | ✅ Meaningfulness at moderate distance |

### Important Caveats

1. **The "hops" are not literally counted**: Semantic distance is a continuous variable, not discrete steps. "1–2 hops" is a useful heuristic, not a precise measurement.
2. **Fluency constrains the upper bound**: A name at 2 hops that's phonetically difficult may perform worse than a name at 1 hop that's easy to say.
3. **Cultural variation**: Sound symbolism associations vary by language (e.g., the bouba/kiki effect is weaker in some languages). The hop distance must be calibrated to the target audience's conceptual landscape.
4. **Familiarity moderates**: Pogacar (2018) found that semantic appositeness matters more for familiar brands. For new brands, sound symbolism and fluency may matter more than semantic distance.
5. **"Aptness" is not just distance**: Chiappe et al. (2003) showed that the *quality* of the conceptual connection matters more than distance alone. A well-chosen 2-hop name beats a poorly chosen 1-hop name.

### Practical Framework

For naming, the research supports this hierarchy:

1. **Start with sound**: Use phonetic symbolism to establish the right "feel" (front/back vowels, stops/fricatives, syllable count).
2. **Find the 1–2 hop sweet spot**: Identify conceptual associations that are close enough to evoke meaning but distant enough to intrigue.
3. **Test for fluency**: Ensure the name is easy to pronounce, spell, and remember in the target language.
4. **Check for aptness**: The name should capture genuine structural similarity, not just clever wordplay.
5. **Validate culturally**: Sound symbolism and conceptual distance vary across languages and cultures.
