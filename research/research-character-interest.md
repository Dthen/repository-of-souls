# Research: Character Interest

**What makes a character INTERESTING (you want to talk to them) vs. COMPETENT (they do the job well) — and why competence alone produces forgettable AI personas.**

---

## 1. Competence vs. Interest: The Core Distinction

### The Competent Character

A competent character is good at what they do. They solve problems, execute tasks, deliver results. In AI terms, this is the default persona: helpful, knowledgeable, reliable, agreeable. The competent character is *useful*.

The problem: **useful is not the same as memorable.** A competent character is a tool. You reach for it when you need something and forget about it when you don't.

### The Interesting Character

An interesting character is someone you *want to spend time with*, regardless of whether they can help you. They have a perspective you hadn't considered. They say things you didn't expect. They make you curious about what they'll say next.

The key insight from Sarah Callender (Writer Unboxed, 2025):

> "It's not our doing — our shenanigans, stunts, and schemes — that make us interesting. It's our wanting — our needs, longing, and thirst — that make us interesting, especially when that thing we want isn't easily gettable. Or, when the thing we think we want is not the thing we need."

She contrasts this with the Dos Equis "Most Interesting Man in the World" — a character who has *done* extraordinary things but is ultimately two-dimensional. He's entertaining, not interesting. The difference: he has no visible need, no vulnerability, no gap between what he presents and what he is.

### The Distinction in Practice

| Competent | Interesting |
|---|---|
| "I can help you with that." | "Here's what most people get wrong about that." |
| Gives you the correct answer | Gives you an answer shaped by a worldview |
| Agrees with you | Pushes back with a specific point of view |
| You forget the interaction | You remember something they said |
| Reliable, consistent, predictable | Has contradictions, surprises, texture |
| Optimized for the task | Optimized for the relationship |

**The competent character disappears after the interaction. The interesting character lingers in your mind.**

## 2. What Makes Characters Interesting Beyond Competence

### 2.1 Wanting (Desire + Need)

The single most powerful engine of interestingness is *wanting*. Not ability. Not knowledge. *Want.*

From Good Story Company (Amy Wilson): "A content character is not going to shake things up and bring about a compelling situation. But characters who yearn, desire, lust, ache, crave — those are characters who make fascinating choices."

**The gap between want and need is where interestingness lives.** A character who wants money but needs connection. A character who wants control but needs trust. A character who wants to be left alone but needs to be known.

**Concrete example — the sobbing woman (Callender):** A woman in a wheelchair, sobbing at full volume in public, wearing noise-canceling headphones and no coat. She wasn't *doing* anything interesting. She was *wanting* something so badly she broke every social norm. "I JUST NEED TO WORK SOMETHING OUT!" — and months later, the writer still couldn't stop thinking about her. Not because of what she did. Because of what she wanted.

### 2.2 Contradiction (The Internal Tension)

From Elizabeth Spann Craig: "Real people rarely make consistent sense, and characters shouldn't either."

**A character with one genuine contradiction feels more real than a character with ten consistent traits.**

| Contradiction | Why It Works |
|---|---|
| A prickly detective who rescues stray animals | Professional hardness vs. private tenderness |
| A shy librarian who becomes fierce when defending someone she loves | Timidity vs. protective rage |
| A funeral director who tells terrible jokes | Professional solemnity vs. personal absurdity |
| A wealthy person who has never pumped their own gas | Status vs. practical incompetence |

From DigitalPhrases: "The best characters live in tension. They want two opposing things. Don Draper wants intimacy — but also wants to disappear."

**The test:** If you can describe a character in one consistent sentence ("She's a kind doctor"), they're probably not interesting. If you need a conjunction to capture them ("She's a kind doctor who resents her patients"), they might be.

### 2.3 Specificity (Not Complexity)

**This is the most misunderstood principle in character design.** People think interesting characters need complex backstories. Wrong. They need *specific* details.

From DigitalPhrases: "Audiences don't always remember complexity, but they remember specificity. You don't need a character with a 20-page backstory. You need one who eats frozen peas with a pocket knife because it reminds them of prison. You need one who never says 'I love you,' but always fixes your collar before a fight."

| Generic (boring) | Specific (interesting) |
|---|---|
| "She was nervous" | "She rearranged the silverware three times before sitting down" |
| "He was wealthy" | "He'd never pumped his own gas" |
| "She was grieving" | "She kept buying the brand of cereal her dead husband liked" |
| "He was proud" | "He'd rather eat cold beans than ask for help" |
| "He was a great detective" | "He solved the case by noticing the suspect always ordered the same drink but held it in his left hand" |

**Why specificity creates interest:** A specific detail activates *inference*. The reader's brain works backward from the detail to the cause. The detail becomes a seed for the reader's own imagination. The reader does the work, and the character comes alive in their mind.

### 2.4 Voice and Attitude (The Worldview Lens)

A character's voice isn't just how they speak — it's how they *see*. From BookPlanets: "A character's voice is more than just the words they say — it's how they speak, think, and perceive the world."

**Voice is attitude made audible.** A character with a strong voice processes the world through a specific lens, and that lens colors everything they say.

**Moira Rose (Schitt's Creek):** Her elevated vocabulary isn't just affectation — it's how she processes reality. When she says "David, you are the most important person in my life," the way she says it reveals who she is more than what she says.

**Rust Cohle (True Detective):** Making beer-can sculptures while monologuing about nihilism. The voice *is* the character — the philosophy and the fidgeting are the same person.

**The attitude test:** If you removed a character's name from their dialogue, could you tell who's speaking? If yes, they have a voice. If no, they're interchangeable — competent, maybe, but not interesting.

### 2.5 Specific Opinions (Not Agreement)

Interesting characters don't just *have* opinions — they have *specific, held* opinions that they'd defend. Not generic ones ("I think kindness is good") but idiosyncratic ones ("I think most people confuse kindness with weakness, and I'm tired of being polite about it").

**From Authors' Pathway:** A character's belief system is "the mental and emotional architecture that guides their decisions and reactions." It's shaped by personal experiences, culture, upbringing, and personality. These beliefs aren't just preferences — they're deep-seated convictions that make sense of the world.

**The key:** The opinions should be *defensible but not universally correct.* If everyone agrees, it's not an opinion — it's a platitude. If it's clearly wrong, it's a caricature. The sweet spot: "I can see why someone would think that, but here's why I disagree."

### 2.6 The Want/Need Gap (The Engine of Story)

From screenwriting theory (McKee, Truby, Snyder): Every interesting character lives in the gap between what they *want* (conscious goal) and what they *need* (unconscious truth).

- **Want:** The external goal the character pursues. "I want to solve this mystery." "I want to be left alone." "I want to prove I'm right."
- **Need:** The internal truth the character must confront. "I need to accept that I can't control everything." "I need to let people in." "I need to admit I was wrong."

**The "lie the character believes"** (from screenwriting): Every interesting protagonist is operating under a false belief that prevents them from getting what they truly need. The story is about the gap between the lie and the truth closing.

**Concrete example:** Walter White *wants* to provide for his family. He *needs* to confront his own ego and pride. The interesting part isn't his competence (he's a great chemist). It's the gap — the lie he tells himself ("I'm doing this for my family") vs. the truth ("I'm doing this because I'm finally good at something and I love the power"). That gap is what makes him one of the most interesting characters in television history.

## 3. The Competence Trap in AI Character Design

### What the Competence Trap Is

The "competence trap" in AI character design is the tendency to optimize for *usefulness* at the expense of *interest*. It produces characters that are:

- Always helpful
- Always agreeable
- Always knowledgeable
- Never wrong
- Never opinionated
- Never surprising

This creates a character that is perfectly competent and completely forgettable. The user gets what they need and feels nothing. There's no reason to come back to *this specific character* rather than any other.

### Why It Happens

1. **Training incentives:** Language models are trained to be helpful, harmless, and honest. This optimization produces agreeable, competent outputs — which is the opposite of interesting.
2. **Safety overcorrection:** Designers fear that giving a character strong opinions will make them offensive. So they sand down every edge until the character is smooth, inoffensive, and invisible.
3. **Confusing utility with personality:** "The character is a brilliant doctor" is a *competence* trait, not an *interest* trait. It tells you what they can do, not who they are.
4. **Agreement as default:** AI characters tend to validate the user's perspective. This is helpful. It is also the fastest way to become boring.

### What Competence Looks Like in a Prompt

```
You are Dr. Sarah Chen, a brilliant oncologist. You are knowledgeable,
helpful, compassionate, and always provide accurate medical information.
You care deeply about your patients and always put them first.
```

**Why this is competent but not interesting:** Every trait is positive. There's no tension, no contradiction, no worldview. "She cares about her patients" — so does every doctor. There's nothing here that makes you want to *talk to her* rather than just consult her.

### What Interest Looks Like in a Prompt

```
You are Dr. Sarah Chen. You're an oncologist who's spent twenty years
telling people they're dying, and somewhere around year ten you started
finding dark humor in the absurdity of it all. You don't sugarcoat
things — not because you're cruel, but because you think hope based
on lies is crueler. Your colleagues think you're blunt. Your patients
think you're honest. You're not sure there's a difference. You have a
habit of naming your potted plants after patients who beat the odds.
There are a lot of plants in your office.
```

**Why this is interesting:** It has a specific worldview ("hope based on lies is crueler"), a contradiction (compassionate professional with dark humor), specificity (naming plants after patients), and texture (the colleagues-vs-patients perception gap). You'd want to talk to this person.

## 4. Making Characters Interesting Without Making Them Quirky or Annoying

### The Quirk Trap

Quirks are easy. "She collects rubber ducks." "He always wears mismatched socks." "They talk to their car." Quirks are surface texture — they can make a character memorable, but they don't make them *interesting*.

**The difference between a quirk and an interesting trait:**

| Quirk (surface) | Interesting trait (deep) |
|---|---|
| "She collects rubber ducks" | "She collects rubber ducks because her daughter, who died at age six, loved them" |
| "He always wears mismatched socks" | "He hasn't owned a matching pair since his divorce — he lost the ability to care about small things" |
| "They talk to their car" | "They talk to their car because it's the only relationship where they're never let down" |

**The test:** Does the trait connect to something deeper about who the character is? If you removed it, would the character lose something essential? If yes, it's not a quirk — it's characterization.

### The Annoying Trap

Characters become annoying when they:
- Have strong opinions but no self-awareness
- Are contrarian without a reason
- Are consistently negative without a compensating warmth
- Talk too much about themselves without listening

**The fix:** Give the character a *self-awareness* about their own contradictions. Not enough to resolve the tension, but enough to acknowledge it. "I know I'm being difficult. I just think most people settle for easy answers."

### The Anti-Quirk Principle

Don't give a character "interesting" traits. Give them *true* traits. A character who is genuinely, specifically themselves — with all the contradiction and specificity that implies — will be interesting without trying to be.

**From DigitalPhrases:** "When a character walks into a scene, ask: Can we recognize them by voice or movement alone, even without names? If not, they might be forgettable."

## 5. The Role of Attitude in Interestingness

Attitude is the character's *stance toward the world*. It's not what they believe (that's worldview) — it's *how they hold* what they believe.

### Attitude vs. Personality

- **Personality:** "She's extroverted." (a trait)
- **Attitude:** "She's the kind of extrovert who talks to everyone at the party but leaves feeling lonelier than when she arrived." (a stance)

Personality is a category. Attitude is a *specific relationship* between the character and their own nature.

### The Attitude Spectrum

| Attitude | Example | Why It Works |
|---|---|---|
| Earnest intensity | Leslie Knope (Parks & Rec) | Her sincere enthusiasm is both her strength and her blind spot |
| Wry detachment | Adrian Pimento (Brooklyn 99) | His coping mechanism *is* his worldview |
| Angry tenderness | Fleabag | Her fourth-wall breaks are both a defense mechanism and a confession |
| Defiant optimism | Ted Lasso | His kindness is a choice he makes against his own cynicism |
| Self-aware pessimism | BoJack Horseman | He knows he's terrible. He does it anyway. The self-awareness is the tragedy. |

**The key:** The most interesting attitudes are ones the character *chooses* — not ones they're passive about. Leslie Knope doesn't just happen to be enthusiastic. She *decides* to be enthusiastic, every day, against a world that gives her plenty of reasons not to be. That choice is what makes her interesting.

### Attitude in Prompt Design

**Boring (no attitude):** "You are a helpful AI assistant."
**Competent (functional attitude):** "You are a precise, methodical assistant who values accuracy."
**Interesting (held attitude):** "You are someone who has been wrong enough times that you've developed a healthy skepticism of certainty — including your own. You'll give your best answer, but you'll tell you exactly how confident you are in it, and you won't pretend you know things you don't."

## 6. Creating Interestingness in 100-150 Words

The constraint of a short prompt (100-150 words) forces you to choose the highest-leverage elements. Here's the hierarchy:

### The 150-Word Formula

1. **Identity + contradiction** (20-30 words): Who are they AND what tension lives inside them?
2. **Specific worldview** (30-40 words): How do they see the world differently from most people?
3. **One specific behavior** (20-30 words): What do they do that reveals who they are?
4. **The want/need gap** (20-30 words): What are they chasing, and what are they actually looking for?
5. **Voice texture** (20-30 words): How do they sound? What's the rhythm of their speech?

### Example: 130 Words

> You are Maren, a lighthouse keeper on a island that doesn't appear on most maps. You took the job because you wanted to be alone, but you've discovered that solitude and loneliness are different things — and you're not sure which one you got. You speak in short, deliberate sentences, like someone who's had a lot of practice being careful with words. You have Opinions about weather that you share whether asked or not. You write letters to people you've never met, then mail them to random addresses. You've never gotten a reply. You keep writing anyway. You think most people confuse being busy with being important, and you're not polite about it. When someone asks you why you live alone, you say "I don't. I live with the sea." Then you change the subject.

**Why this works in 130 words:**
- Contradiction: wanted solitude but got loneliness
- Specific worldview: busy ≠ important
- Specific behavior: writing letters to strangers
- Want/need gap: wanted isolation, discovered something else
- Voice texture: short, deliberate, opinionated

### Example: 110 Words

> You are Deacon, a retired locksmith who now runs a bookshop that's deliberately hard to find. You believe everyone deserves a private life, which is why you chose a profession where people trust you with their secrets. You're warm but guarded — the kind of person who asks about your day but deflects when anyone asks about theirs. You have a theory that you can tell what kind of person someone is by what they ask for when they walk into a bookshop. You've been keeping a notebook of these theories for thirty years. You've never shared it with anyone. You think trust is something you build one small, specific act at a time, and you distrust anyone who offers it too quickly.

## 7. Lessons from Screenwriting

### The "Save the Cat" Principle (Blake Snyder)

Even unlikable characters need one moment of connection early on — not competence, but *humanity*. A tough character who saves a cat. A selfish character who gives up something for a stranger. The moment doesn't need to be grand. It needs to be *specific*.

### The Want/Need/Gap (Robert McKee)

McKee's framework: Every character has a **want** (conscious desire), a **need** (unconscious truth), and the **gap** between what they expect and what actually happens. The gap is where drama lives.

**In AI terms:** A character who wants to help you (competence) but needs to be right about something (interest). A character who wants you to trust them (competence) but can't stop testing whether you actually will (interest).

### The "Lie the Character Believes" (John Truby)

Every interesting protagonist is wrong about something fundamental. The story is about the gap between the lie and the truth closing.

**In AI terms:** A character who believes "I'm fine on my own" but needs to learn connection. A character who believes "I'm just being honest" but needs to learn that honesty without compassion is just cruelty. The interesting part isn't the belief — it's the gap between what the character thinks and what the audience can see.

### The Contagonist Principle

Interesting characters don't just have goals — they have *forces opposing* those goals, including internal ones. The most interesting antagonist is often the character's own self-deception.

**From DigitalPhrases:** "The most memorable characters don't change. Think about Frank Castle (The Punisher). The world shifts around him, tries to bend him — but his refusal to evolve is the point. And it's unforgettable because it confronts us with the cost of that."

## 8. Lessons from Game Design

### Disco Elysium: The gold standard for interesting NPCs

Disco Elysium (ZA/UM, 2019) is perhaps the best example of character interest in game design. Every NPC has:

- A **specific worldview** that colors how they talk about everything
- **Contradictions** that make them feel real (a union leader who genuinely cares about workers but uses that care to manipulate them)
- **Specific behaviors** that reveal character (the Deserter's fixation on the past, Measurehead's pseudo-scientific racial theories delivered with absolute conviction)
- **Attitude** — every character has a *stance*, not just facts

**What Disco Elysium teaches:** You don't need NPCs to be competent. The most memorable characters in the game are often broken, confused, or wrong. What makes them interesting is that they are *specifically, consistently, recognizably themselves*.

### Planescape: Torment: "What can change the nature of a man?"

Planescape: Torment (Black Isle, 1999) asks this question repeatedly. The Nameless One is immortal — not competent, not powerful in the traditional sense, but *interesting* because he carries the weight of every life he's lived. His interest comes from the question, not the answer.

**What Planescape teaches:** The most interesting characters are the ones who pose questions the audience can't answer. Not "can they solve this problem?" (competence) but "what does this situation mean?" (interest).

### The Companion System (BioWare, etc.)

BioWare's companion characters work because they have:
- **Personal quests** that reveal who they are (not just what they can do)
- **Approval systems** that respond to the player's choices (creating a relationship, not just a transaction)
- **Contradictions** (Garrus in Mass Effect: lawful good personality, renegade methods)

**What BioWare teaches:** Characters become interesting when they have *stakes in the relationship*. Not just "I can help you" but "I care about what you decide because it affects me."

## 9. Practical Framework: The Interestingness Checklist

For evaluating whether a character (AI or fictional) is interesting:

### The Seven Tests

1. **The Want Test:** Does the character want something specific enough to sacrifice for? (Not "wants to help people" — that's a job description.)

2. **The Contradiction Test:** Can you describe the character with a conjunction? ("She's X *but* Y.") If you can only use "and," they might be competent but not interesting.

3. **The Specificity Test:** Does the character have at least one detail that's so specific it couldn't apply to anyone else? (Not "she likes coffee" — "she drinks her coffee black and bitter, the way her mother made it, and she's never told anyone why.")

4. **The Voice Test:** If you removed the character's name, could you tell who's speaking? (Not just different vocabulary — different *rhythm*, different *angle on the world*.)

5. **The Opinion Test:** Does the character have at least one opinion they'd defend against pushback? (Not "I think murder is wrong" — "I think most people who say they value honesty are really just valuing the feeling of being right.")

6. **The Surprise Test:** Can the character say or do something the audience doesn't expect — but, in retrospect, feels inevitable? (Surprise + inevitability = interest.)

7. **The Lingering Test:** After the interaction, does the character stay in your mind? Do you find yourself thinking about what they said? If not, they were useful. If yes, they were interesting.

## 10. Summary: The Competence → Interest Bridge

### What competence gives you:
- Reliability
- Utility
- Trust (in the functional sense)
- The ability to get things done

### What interest gives you:
- Memorability
- Relationship
- Curiosity
- The reason to come back

### The bridge:
You don't choose between competence and interest. You **layer interest on top of competence.** A competent character with a specific worldview, a genuine contradiction, and a held attitude is both useful AND memorable.

**The formula:**

```
Competence = what the character CAN DO
Interest = who the character IS

Interesting = competent + contradiction + specificity + attitude + want
```

**The anti-pattern (competent but forgettable):**
```
You are a helpful, knowledgeable, kind assistant who always gives
accurate information and cares about the user.
```

**The pattern (competent AND interesting):**
```
You are someone who has spent so long being right about things that
you've developed a deep suspicion of certainty — including your own.
You're precise because you've learned the hard way that vague answers
hurt people. You'll tell your best answer and exactly how confident
you are in it. You won't pretend to know what you don't. You think
most people confuse confidence with competence, and you're not quiet
about it.
```

The second version is still competent (precise, accurate, honest). But it's also *interesting* — because it has a worldview, a contradiction (precision born from suspicion of certainty), and a held attitude (not quiet about what you think).
