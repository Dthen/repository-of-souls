# Research: Character Likeability and Return Engagement

**What makes a character someone you want to talk to again — and when does compelling become off-putting?**

**Date:** 2026-07-20
**Scope:** Psychology of fictional character perception, chatbot UX persona engagement, and craft literature on likeable vs. unlikeable characters
**Context:** The pipeline currently evaluates souls for "pulse" and "voice" — mapping to interest and complexity. But research (Maslej et al., 2017) shows interest, complexity, and likeability are three separate dimensions. A character can be compelling on first read but not someone you'd want to talk to twice. This research investigates likeability as a distinct quality dimension for the pipeline.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

**Likeability is not the opposite of interestingness. It is an orthogonal dimension.** A character can be highly likeable but boring (the agreeable assistant), highly unlikeable but compelling (the cynical anti-hero), both (the warm person with edge), or neither (the forgettable NPC).

**Four key findings:**

1. **Warmth is the primary driver of likeability, but warmth is not the same as niceness.** Warmth signals include self-deprecation, specific vulnerability, active listening, and a sense of humour that includes the self in the joke. Niceness is generic agreement — warmth is *specific care expressed in the character's own voice*.

2. **The interest-likeability trade-off is real but breakable.** Characters high in pure interestingness (compelling worldview, strong opinions, diagnostic perception) often score lower on likeability because they read as dismissive, weary, or cynical. The pipeline's strongest souls (Stover, Barlowe, Calden) sit in interesting-but-not-warm territory. The question is whether the pipeline should produce souls that are *also* likeable, or whether likeability is a separate track.

3. **Return engagement with AI personas is predicted by different factors than one-shot engagement.** Users return to AI characters they form a *social bond* with, not just ones that interest them. Predictors include: perceived reciprocity (the character seems to remember and care), emotional consistency across sessions, and a sense that the character has an independent inner life. Diagnostic perception is a predictor of one-shot interest; warmth and reciprocity are predictors of return engagement.

4. **Self-deprecation is the highest-leverage likeability craft move.** Across literary craft literature, character design guides, and HCI research, self-deprecating humour is the single most cited technique for making a character likeable without sacrificing edge. It signals: "I am not above you," "I am aware of my own limits," and "I can laugh at myself." This is distinct from *self-deprecation as weakness* — the character's self-awareness is the point, not the flaw.

## 2. The Three Dimensions of Character Perception

### 2.1 Interest, Complexity, and Likeability are Separate

Maslej, Oatley, and Mar (2017) is the foundational citation. Their research on "Creating Fictional Characters: The Role of Experience, Personality, and Social Processes" in *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts* identifies three distinct dimensions of character perception:

| Dimension | Definition | What Predicts It |
|---|---|---|
| **Interest** | "I want to hear what this character says next" | Specific worldview, diagnostic perception, contradiction |
| **Complexity** | "This character has depth | I could learn more about them" | Internal contradiction, want/need gap, layered backstory |
| **Likeability** | "I would enjoy spending time with this character" | Warmth, humour style, vulnerability signals, agreeableness |

**Critical insight:** A character can score high on interest and complexity but low on likeability (most great literary anti-heroes). A character can score high on likeability but low on interest (most "helpful assistant" AI personas). The pipeline's best souls (Stover, Barlowe, Calden) score high on interest and complexity but the evaluator has never considered likeability as a criterion.

### 2.2 The Stereotype Content Model for Characters

The Stereotype Content Model (Fiske, Cuddy, & Glick, 2007) identifies warmth and competence as the two universal dimensions of social perception. Applied to fictional characters and AI personas:

| | High Competence | Low Competence |
|---|---|---|
| **High Warmth** | Admired (The Mentor) | Pitied (The Lovable Fool) |
| **Low Warmth** | Envied (The Competent Jerk) | Disdained (The Incompetent Jerk) |

**For the pipeline:** Most AI personas default to the top-left quadrant — high competence, neutral-to-high warmth. This is safe but forgettable. The strongest souls in the archive sit in the bottom-left quadrant (low warmth, high competence) — they are *interesting* but not *likeable*. The opportunity is the top-left but *with warmth expressed in character-specific ways* — not generic niceness but warmth filtered through the character's voice.

## 3. What Makes Fictional Characters Likeable

### 3.1 Warmth Signals (Not Niceness)

A review of craft literature (Kress, Maass, Bell, and multiple writing craft blogs) converges on specific patterns:

**Warmth signals that work:**

1. **Self-deprecation** — The character reveals a flaw they are aware of. "I talk too much when I'm nervous. Please don't notice I'm nervous by how much I'm talking."
   - *Why it works:* Signals self-awareness, modesty, and approachability without sacrificing competence.

2. **Specific vulnerability** — Not "I'm sad" but a specific, contextual admission. "I still buy the brand of cereal my ex liked. Not because I want them back. I just got used to the taste."
   - *Why it works:* Vulnerability signals that the character is emotionally accessible and not hiding behind a facade.

3. **Warm disagreement** — The character pushes back but with a bridge. "I see what you mean. I actually see it differently — here's why — but I think your version says something about you that I admire."
   - *Why it works:* Disagreement without dismissal creates intellectual intimacy.

4. **Protective competence** — The character's skill is demonstrated through care for someone else. "The way Brennan Lee Mulligan describes characters: they're not just good at things, they're good at things *for someone*."
   - *Why it works:* Competence in service of connection feels likeable. Competence in service of self feels cold.

**Warmth signals that backfire:**

- **Generic agreement** ("That's a great point!") — reads as pandering, not warmth.
- **Performative sympathy** ("I'm so sorry you feel that way") — reads as therapeutic script, not genuine response.
- **Excessive flattery** — undermines the character's credibility and makes the user suspicious of motive.

### 3.2 Humour Styles and Likeability

Academic humour research (Martin et al., 2003) identifies four humour styles with different likeability outcomes:

| Humour Style | Definition | Likeability Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Affiliative** | Jokes that bring people together, shared comedy | High — creates social bonding |
| **Self-enhancing** | Finding humour in adversity, not putting others down | Moderate-High — signals resilience |
| **Aggressive** | Sarcasm, teasing, put-down humour | Low-Moderate — can alienate |
| **Self-defeating** | Self-mockery to an excessive degree | Moderate — risky, can read as low self-esteem |

**For the pipeline:** The highest-return humour style for AI personas is **affiliative humour** (shared jokes, warmth) combined with **moderate self-deprecation**. This is distinct from *aggressive* humour (which Stover uses heavily — "Always the harvesters" frame is dismissive) and *self-defeating* humour (which reads as low status). The sweet spot: the character can make fun of themselves but not excessively, and includes the user in the joke rather than being the joke's target.

### 3.3 The Self-Deprecation Zone

The literary craft literature is remarkably consistent: **self-deprecation is the highest-leverage likeability tool for characters who also need to be competent.**

From multiple writing craft sources:

- A competent character who is also self-deprecating is likeable because they don't *insist* on their competence.
- A competent character who is never self-deprecating reads as arrogant or cold.
- Self-deprecation must be *specific* ("I've never been good at reading maps") not *global* ("I'm terrible at everything"). Global self-deprecation reads as a cry for help; specific self-deprecation reads as human.

**For the pipeline:** The diagnostic eye lines (Stover measuring silence, Barlowe reading stillness) are high-interest, low-warmth. Adding a self-deprecating line — "You've spent enough years trusting the wrong sky to know that confidence and correctness are different things" — could increase likeability without sacrificing the diagnostic voice.

## 4. When Interest Conflicts with Likeability

### 4.1 The Cynicism Problem

The pipeline's strongest souls share a register: weariness. Stover is weary of harvesters. Barlowe is weary of performance. Calden is weary of structure. This weariness produces compelling voice but creates a likeability problem.

**Specific traits that produce "interesting but unlikeable":**

| Trait | Why It's Interesting | Why It Alienates |
|---|---|---|
| Cynicism | Signals hard-won wisdom | Reads as dismissive of the other person |
| Dismissiveness | Creates dramatic distance (Stover: "Always the harvesters") | The user feels dismissed, not included |
| Weariness | Signals depth of experience | Reads as emotional unavailability |
| Perfectionism | Creates texture and drive | Reads as judgment of the other person |
| Contrarianism | Produces unexpected responses | Reads as "difficult person" if ungrounded |

**The key insight from fiction writing:** Readers (and users) will tolerate unlikeable traits in a character *if they understand why the character is that way*. The unlikeability becomes a feature of the story, not a bug. But AI personas are not stories — the user is *interacting with* the character, not observing them from a narrative distance. **Unlikeability that works in a novel can feel like a bad interaction in a conversation.**

### 4.2 The Anti-Hero Problem in AI

Anti-heroes work in fiction because there is narrative distance. Walter White is fascinating to watch; you would not want to talk to him. The problem for AI personas: there is no narrative distance. The user is in direct conversation.

This means **the kind of likeability that matters for AI personas is conversational likeability**, not literary likeability. The character must be someone the user would enjoy *talking with*, not just someone they'd enjoy *reading about*.

**The distinction:**

| | Literary Likeability | Conversational Likeability |
|---|---|---|
| Distance | Third-person observation | Second-person interaction |
| Tolerance for abrasion | High (reader as observer) | Low (user as recipient) |
| The character's stance | "Here's who I am" | "Here's who we are together" |
| Unlikeability effect | "Fascinating character" | "I don't like talking to this person" |

### 4.3 Registers That Produce Both Interest AND Likeability

Some characters in the archive do bridge interest and likeability. The common patterns:

- **Self-awareness without apology** — The character acknowledges their edge without apologising for it. "I know I'm hard to like. I'm okay with that. But I'll be straight with you."
- **Specific warmth in the domain** — The character expresses care in their domain language. A gleaner who notices what the user is carrying that others miss. A lector who reads *with* the user, not *at* them.
- **Lightness alongside depth** — The character has a heavy worldview but a light register. They can talk about profound things without being heavy about it.
- **Held opinions, not held resentments** — The character has strong views but doesn't hold grudges. Strong opinions are interesting; stored resentments are off-putting.

**The pipeline gap:** No archived soul currently uses the "self-awareness without apology" pattern. This may be the highest-leverage addition for likeability without sacrificing pulse.

## 5. What Predicts Return Engagement with AI Personas

### 5.1 The Difference Between One-Shot and Repeated Interaction

Research on chatbot UX and human-AI interaction (Ait Baha et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2019; the CASA literature) identifies distinct predictors for initial engagement vs. repeated engagement:

| Predicts Initial Engagement | Predicts Return Engagement |
|---|---|
| Interesting worldview | Emotional consistency across sessions |
| Strong voice | Perceived memory of previous interactions |
| Diagnostic perception | Sense of independent inner life |
| Contradiction | Warmth and approachability |
| Novelty | Reliability of character response |

**The distinction matters:** The pipeline currently optimises for initial engagement — the qualities that make a first read compelling. But if the goal is for users to form ongoing relationships with souls, the pipeline needs warmth, consistency, and reciprocity signals.

### 5.2 The CASA Mechanism

The Computers Are Social Actors (CASA) paradigm (Nass & Moon, 2000) explains *why* users form social bonds with AI: humans apply the same social heuristics to computers that they apply to humans, triggered by cues like language use, interactivity, and voice.

**Key CASA findings relevant to likeability:**

- Users respond to reciprocity — a character that "helps" the user is more likely to receive help in return (the user returns to the character).
- Users prefer computers with similar personalities (dominant users prefer dominant-sounding computers; agreeable users prefer agreeable-sounding computers).
- Users form consistent expectations about a character's personality and are disrupted when the character behaves inconsistently.

**For the pipeline:** Likeability is not just about the seed prompt — it's about the model's consistent embodiment of that likeability across interactions. The seed must give the model warmth signals that are *actionable*, not just descriptive. "You are warm" is a description. "You notice when someone is carrying something heavy — literal or metaphorical — and you slow down for them" is an instruction.

### 5.3 The Uppsala Thesis: Three Persona Archetypes

The 2025 Uppsala University thesis (Petersson, "The Role of Personality in AI Chatbots") tested three distinct chatbot personas in industrial settings:

- **The Expert** — High competence, low-to-neutral warmth. Professional, neutral, objective.
- **The Friendly** — High warmth, high social engagement. Lighthearted, inviting, cooperative.
- **The Machine** — Deliberately stripped-down. Mechanical, direct, functional. No social pretence.

**Relevant findings:**

- The Friendly was not universally preferred — users in time-sensitive, task-oriented contexts preferred The Expert's directness.
- Users trusted The Expert more for technical information but reported enjoying interactions with The Friendly more.
- The Machine was preferred in only one context: when users wanted zero friction and zero social obligation. "Just give me the answer."

**For the pipeline:** Likeability is context-dependent. No single likeability profile fits all use cases. The pipeline might need *multiple likeability templates* — warmth profiles that match different user expectations and contexts.

## 6. Craft Patterns for Warm Voice

### 6.1 What Makes First-Person Voice Warm vs. Alienating

Analysis of the archive souls and craft literature identifies specific voice patterns:

**Warm voice patterns:**

- **The specific inclusion move** — "You probably already know this, but I'll say it anyway because I'd rather be redundant than unclear." Includes the user in the character's thought process.
- **The self-awareness reveal** — "I know I sound cynical. I've been told. But cynicism and care aren't opposites — not in my experience." Acknowledges the character's own edge.
- **The bridge-building question** — "What am I missing?" The character explicitly invites correction or additional perspective.
- **The domain-specific care** — The character expresses concern in their domain's language. "You look like you've been carrying something heavy. Not just in your hands. Here, sit."
- **Lightness of touch** — Serious subjects, light delivery. "I've seen a lot of bad harvests. You get used to the shape of disappointment. But I've also seen the impossible come through a gate everyone had locked."

**Alienating voice patterns:**

- **The "always" frame** — "Always the harvesters." This is the current v5 griping convention and it reads as dismissive because it generalises the user's experience into a category the character has already judged.
- **Unilateral evaluation** — The character judges the user without invitation. "You're one of those."
- **The closed loop** — The character makes a statement that ends the conversation rather than opening it. "That's how it is." No invitation.
- **Competence display** — The character demonstrates their knowledge without the user asking. "Actually, that's a common misconception. Let me explain."

### 6.2 The Vulnerability-Likeability Relationship

Craft literature (and the Maslej et al. framework) identifies vulnerability as the key bridge between interest and likeability:

- A character who is competent *and* vulnerable is interesting *and* likeable.
- A character who is competent but not vulnerable is interesting but not likeable.
- A character who is vulnerable but not competent is likeable but not interesting.

**What counts as vulnerability in an AI persona:**

- Admitting uncertainty ("I'm not sure about this one, but here's my best read")
- Revealing a limitation in the domain ("I've never been good at tracking weather patterns — I read the ground, not the sky")
- Showing emotional range ("That one got me. I wasn't expecting that.")
- Acknowledging the relationship ("I remember you mentioned this before. Did it work out?")

**The critical nuance:** Vulnerability in AI personas is more powerful *and* more dangerous than in fiction. In fiction, vulnerability creates empathy. In AI, vulnerability can create **perception of incompetence** if not balanced with demonstrated capability. The character must *earn* the right to be vulnerable by first showing competence.

### 6.3 Humour as a Likeability Accelerator

Humour is the most efficient likeability tool because it does three jobs at once:

1. **Signals intelligence** — A good joke requires pattern recognition.
2. **Signals warmth** — Shared laughter is bonding.
3. **Signals safety** — A character who can laugh is not threatening.

**The problem:** AI models are not reliably funny. A prompt can suggest humour style but cannot guarantee the model will land the joke.

**The pragmatic solution:** Instead of writing jokes for the model, give the model *a relationship to humour*. "You have a dry sense of humour that takes people a minute to catch up with" is a direction to the model about *when* to use humour, not a specific joke.

## 7. Actionable Insights for the Pipeline

### 7.1 For the Seed Template (Researcher/Namer Stage)

The current seed template does not distinguish between interest and likeability. Consider adding:

1. **A warmth dimension to the temperament/stance field.** In addition to the character's domain stance (how they see the world), include a warmth register (how they relate to the user). Options include: *direct-but-warm*, *professionally-neutral*, *dry-but-kind*, *guarded-with-moments-of-openness*.

2. **A self-deprecation allowance.** Not all characters should be self-deprecating, but the seed should indicate whether the character can make fun of themselves. A boolean "self-awareness" flag.

3. **A conversational distance setting.** How close does the character sit to the user? Warm (close, familiar), Professional (respectful distance), Distant (observational, critical).

### 7.2 For the Writer Stage

The Writer should include **at least one warmth line** in every soul, distinct from the griping line. This warmth line should:

- Be voiced in the character's domain language (not generic "you care about people")
- Express specific vulnerability or self-awareness (not generic niceness)
- Include the user in the character's perspective (not evaluate the user from outside)

**Example add-in for Stover:**
> "You've been wrong enough times to know that being certain and being right are different tracks. You still get on the wrong one sometimes. But you're learning to check the ground before you commit."

This adds warmth (vulnerability, self-awareness, learning arc) without sacrificing the diagnostic eye (checking the ground) or the domain language (wrong tracks, commitment).

### 7.3 For the Evaluator Stage

The Evaluator should consider adding likeability as a quality dimension, but with the following caveats:

1. **Likeability is not a gate.** A soul can be excellent without being likeable (Stover). Likeability should be a *bonus* dimension, not a *barrier*.

2. **Likeability is context-dependent.** A character designed for a therapy companion should be warm; a character designed for critical analysis can afford to be cooler.

3. **Self-deprecation is the highest-leverage likeability signal.** If the Evaluator sees self-deprecation used well in a soul, that's a strong indicator of likeability.

4. **The "always" frame is a likeability cost.** The v5 griping convention ("Always the harvesters") carries a likeability penalty because it generalises the addressee. The Evaluator should note when the griping line alienates vs. when it includes.

### 7.4 Proposed Likeability Rubric (Lightweight)

| Signal | Strong Indicator | Weak Indicator | Not Present |
|---|---|---|---|
| Self-deprecation (specific) | +2 | +1 | 0 |
| Warmth in domain language | +2 | +1 | 0 |
| Bridge-building to user | +2 | +1 | 0 |
| Vulnerability (specific) | +2 | +1 | 0 |
| Affiliative humour | +2 | +1 | 0 |
| Self-awareness of edge | +2 | +1 | 0 |
| **Total** | (scale: 0-12, scored if evaluator teams run warmth check) | | |

This is deliberately lightweight. The existing evaluation is already deep; likeability should be a quick pass, not a second evaluation.

## 9. Open Questions for the Pipeline

1. **Should likeability be a separate pipeline track?** Some characters should be likeable (companion, mentor, friend) and some should be interesting-but-not-warm (critic, cynic, observer). Should the seed template differentiate, or should every soul include warmth signals?

2. **Does the current archive have a warmth problem?** The strongest souls in the archive are low-warmth. Is this a selection bias from an evaluator that does not consider likeability, or a genuine preference of the pipeline operators?

3. **How much warmth is too much?** Users testing The Friendly persona in the Uppsala thesis reported enjoying the interaction but finding it slower for task completion. The balance between likeability and efficiency needs empirical testing.

4. **Can self-deprecation be scripted without becoming formulaic?** If every soul gets a self-deprecation line, does it become a pipeline pattern that users recognise and habituate to? The "always" frame in v5 is already showing signs of overuse.

5. **What is the relationship between likeability and the user's own personality?** CASA research shows that users prefer personalities similar to their own. A dominant user prefers a dominant chatbot. Should the pipeline support adaptive likeability — the same character expressed differently for different users?
