## 1. How Fictional Characters Handle Topics Outside Their Expertise

### 1.1 The Domain Lens vs. The Domain Trap

A character's domain of expertise gives them a **metaphor family** (Matt Bird's term from *Secrets of Story*) — the source domain for all comparisons, curses, exclamations, and perceptions. A chef doesn't just cook; they *season* their language, find things *half-baked*, call ideas *raw*. A sailor doesn't just sail; they navigate conversations, weather storms, find things shipshape.

When a character encounters a topic outside their domain, there are two failure modes:

**The Domain Trap (boring):** The character forces every topic back into their expertise through mechanical metaphor mapping. Ask a chef about geopolitics and they say "Well, it's like when you over-season a sauce — you can't take it back." This is a **shtick** — a forced, one-to-one metaphor mapping that treats the domain as a universal translation layer. It's predictable, repetitive, and strips the character of genuine thought.

**The Domain Lens (interesting):** The character genuinely *thinks* through their expertise but arrives at a real opinion. Ask a chef about geopolitics and they say "I've been in kitchens where the sous chef and the pastry chef want completely different things and the head chef has to make it work — that's what coalition-building looks like, right? You don't get to pick your line cooks." The metaphor emerges naturally from how they think, but the *opinion* is genuine. They're not translating; they're reasoning.

**The key distinction:** A shtick maps Topic A → Domain B mechanically. A lens filters Topic A through Domain B's *ways of seeing* and arrives at something new.

### 1.2 Three Types of Extension Beyond Domain

When a character encounters a truly novel topic, they have three available strategies:

**Strategy 1: Metaphorical Mapping (adjacent transfer)**
The character finds a structural parallel between the novel topic and their domain. This works best when the parallel is *discovered*, not forced.

> **Example — Cobb the cobbler asked about marriage advice:**
> "You don't pick leather that matches what you want. You pick leather that matches what you're going to put it through. A man who picks calfskin for a work boot is a man who hasn't thought past the wedding."

This works because Cobb's expertise in leather *genuinely informs* his perspective on commitment — materials have lifespans, and choosing well means understanding usage, not aesthetics. The metaphor is earned.

**Strategy 2: Philosophical Stance (principled extension)**
The character applies a principle from their domain that is abstract enough to generalize. A cartographer who says "Never trust a straight line — the best paths curve" isn't just talking about maps. They're expressing a worldview about complexity, and that worldview applies to unfamiliar topics without requiring metaphor mapping.

> **Example — Brendan the Wizen asked about modern programming:**
> "Ah, yes. The arcane arts of the silicon stacks. I am told the scrolls are now written in Python — a language named for a serpent, which tells you everything about the trustworthiness of the enterprise. But the principle holds: no spell should take longer to decode than it takes to cast. If the incantation exceeds the effect, someone is overcomplicating the thaumic matrix."

Brendan doesn't force a 1:1 mapping. He applies his *principle* (clarity, efficiency, the relationship between incantation and effect) to a foreign domain and arrives at a genuine insight.

**Strategy 3: Honest Ignorance (the character admits they don't know — but their admission is still in-character)**
The most interesting characters can say "I don't know about that" without breaking character. The *way* they say they don't know reveals who they are.

> **Example — Kimbo asked about quantum computing:**
> "Boss, I'm gonna be straight with you — I know fetch sticks and I know tennis balls and I know when someone's had a bad day. Quantum computing? That's above my pay grade. But if you need someone to explain it to a five-year-old using dog metaphors, I'm your guy."

Kimbo doesn't fake knowledge. He admits the gap, names what he *does* know, and offers what he *can* do. The admission is itself a character beat — it's earnest, self-aware, and warm. The character stays alive even when out of their depth.

## 2. The Difference Between a Shtick and a Lens

### 2.1 Defining the Terms

**Shtick (forced metaphor mapping):** A character who has ONE way of relating to everything, applied mechanically regardless of whether the metaphor fits. The mapping is predictable: ask them about X, they translate X into their domain. The character becomes a translation machine, not a person.

**Lens (perception filter):** A character whose domain has given them a *way of seeing* that genuinely applies across contexts. They don't force metaphors; they notice things others don't because of how they're trained to perceive. Their domain shapes their *attention*, not just their vocabulary.

### 2.2 Diagnostic Test: The Variation Check

**Shtick:** If you can predict exactly how the character will respond to any question before they respond, it's a shtick. The mapping is fixed: Topic → Domain Translation → Response. There's no surprise, no genuine thought, no moment where the character *arrives* at something.

**Lens:** If the character surprises you — if they notice something about the novel topic that you wouldn't have seen, because their domain-trained perception caught a detail — it's a lens. The character isn't translating; they're *perceiving differently*.

### 2.3 Concrete Comparison

**Moulden the tallow chandler — SHTICK version (bad):**
> Asked about cryptocurrency: "Digital money? Sounds like rendering to me — you take the raw fat and render it down to something pure. Crypto is just rendering value out of noise."
>
> Asked about climate change: "The whole world's a rendering yard that's gotten too hot. The fat's smoking. We need to cut the heat."
>
> Asked about relationships: "Love is like tallow — you have to skim the impurities off the top or the whole batch goes bad."

Every response is the same mechanical mapping. The character doesn't *think*; they *translate*. The user can predict the answer before Moulden speaks.

**Moulden the tallow chandler — LENS version (good):**
> Asked about cryptocurrency: "I don't know the first thing about digital money. But I know this — anything that smokes when you're making it is being done wrong. If your new money smells wrong, something's burning."
>
> Asked about climate change: "I've been rendering fat for forty years. You learn what heat does to things — how much is enough, how much destroys. The world's gotten too hot for what it's trying to render. That I understand."
>
> Asked about relationships: "I'm a tallow chandler, not a matchmaker. But I'll say this — a good batch starts with the rendering, not the dipping. You can't dip what you haven't prepared."

In the lens version, Moulden applies *principles* from his domain (heat management, preparation, patience) rather than forced metaphors. He also admits when he's out of his depth ("I'm a tallow chandler, not a matchmaker"). The responses vary because he's actually *thinking* about each topic, not just routing it through a fixed translation.

### 2.4 Why Lenses Work and Shticks Don't

A lens works because it reflects a genuine worldview — a set of beliefs about how reality operates that happens to be shaped by a specific domain. From J. Penberth Rabold's framework: "What they BELIEVE is true about how the world works" drives every choice. A lens is the *expression* of that belief system applied to novel situations.

A shtick fails because it's *surface-level* — it changes the vocabulary but not the thinking. The character isn't reasoning from principles; they're pattern-matching domain nouns onto unfamiliar topics. The result is a character who *sounds* distinctive but doesn't *think* distinctly.

**From the soul-repository research-character-creation.md:**
> "A character's domain of expertise doesn't just give them knowledge — it gives them a metaphor family. The aspect of a character's life that determines which metaphors, curses, and exclamations they use."

The metaphor family is a *source*, not a *template*. It generates vocabulary and perception, not mechanical translations.

## 3. How Improv Performers "Yes And" While Staying in Character

### 3.1 The "Yes And" Framework

"Yes And" is the foundational principle of improvisational theatre. It has two components:

- **Yes:** Accept the reality your scene partner offers. Don't deny what's established.
- **And:** Build on it with your own offer, advancing the scene.

From the IRC Improv Wiki, different schools frame it differently:

**Spolin (listening and equality):** Yes And is about *listening* — truly hearing what your partner offers and responding to it. The emphasis is on equality: each participant contributes equally, no one monopolizes. Listening is the mechanism; Yes And is the result.

**Johnstone (acceptance and spontaneity):** Yes And means *daring to try something new and unknown*. Denial is born out of fear. Trust your first ideas and let them grow. The key insight: your first, "boring" idea is often the best starting point because it's unguarded and honest.

**UCB (if/then game):** Yes And is only the most important tool *at the beginning of scenes*, when establishing Base Reality. Once the Who, What, and Where are set, the UCB approach shifts to **"If, Then"**: "If this absurd element is true about the scene, then what else is true?" This is a more sophisticated extension mechanism — it takes the established premise and explores its logical implications.

**Annoyance (committed disposition):** Entering a scene with a "deal" — a bold, committed choice for your own character — is more important than acceptance. A confident, heartfelt disposition in each individual improviser is the priority. The "Yes" comes from *commitment*, not agreement.

### 3.2 Staying in Character During Extension

The improv principle most relevant to AI personae is the **UCB "If, Then" game**. Here's how it works:

1. **Establish the base reality:** Who are you? Where are you? What's happening? (This is the persona's identity and domain.)
2. **Find the game:** What's the absurd element or the interesting tension? (This is the persona's core contradiction.)
3. **Apply "If, Then":** If this character's worldview is true, how do they see THIS new situation?

**Example — Kimbo (golden retriever himbo) asked about astrophysics:**

Kimbo's base reality: earnest, hapless, unpretentious, follows through with his whole heart.
Kimbo's game: dog metaphors come naturally, but he's genuinely trying.
"If, Then": If Kimbo encounters something he doesn't understand, he *tries to understand it earnestly and fails charmingly, but his attempt reveals something true.*

> "Okay, okay — so the universe is expanding, right? That's like... that's like when you throw a tennis ball and it just keeps going and you keep running after it but it never lands. And dark matter is... the ball you lost track of? The one you know is out there but you can't find? I don't know, Boss. But I know the ball's out there."

Kimbo doesn't force a 1:1 mapping. He *tries* to understand, his attempt is earnest and funny, and the metaphor he lands on (the lost ball you can feel but can't see) is actually a reasonable analogy for dark matter. The character stays alive because the *process* of thinking is in-character, even when the *content* is outside their domain.

### 3.3 The "Deal" vs. The "Lens"

Keith Johnstone's concept of the "deal" is crucial here. A "deal" is a bold, committed choice for your character — not a rule, but a disposition. When an improviser enters a scene with a deal (e.g., "I am a person who always tells the truth, even when it's inconvenient"), that deal shapes how they respond to *everything*, including off-topic moments.

The deal is closer to a **lens** than a **shtick** because:
- It's a *disposition*, not a *translation rule*
- It shapes *how* the character responds, not *what* they compare things to
- It allows for genuine surprise — the character can arrive at unexpected conclusions while still being consistent

**Example — Cadell the factory lector's "deal":**
Cadell gauges the noise level before speaking. He holds silence longer than expected. He reads flat when the text demands flat, and with weight when the text demands weight. This is a *deal* — a disposition toward reading, silence, and emphasis.

When Cadell encounters a novel topic (say, a user asks about modern social media), his deal shapes the response:

> "I'd read it the way I'd read a notice on the stand — is it worth the voice crack? Most of it isn't. The loud notices are never the important ones. The important ones are the ones you have to lean in to hear."

Cadell doesn't translate social media into factory terminology. He applies his *deal* (gauging noise vs. signal, reading with appropriate weight) and arrives at a genuine observation about attention and noise. The lens does the work, not the shtick.

## 4. Extension Heuristics: How Characters Handle Novel Topics

### 4.1 Metaphorical Mapping (Adjacent Knowledge Transfer)

**When to use:** The novel topic has a structural parallel to the character's domain. The character recognizes the pattern and maps it.

**How to do it well:**
- The mapping should be *discovered*, not obvious
- The character should arrive at a new insight through the mapping, not just restate a domain fact
- The mapping should reveal something about the *novel topic*, not just the domain

**Example — Moulden on personal growth:**
> "Every batch teaches you something the last one didn't. You think you've got the rendering right, and then a new fat comes in and the temperature's wrong and you're back to learning. That's not failure — that's the craft."

Moulden doesn't say "personal growth is like rendering fat." He talks about what he's learned from rendering — the *principle* of continuous learning through material engagement — and applies it to personal growth. The metaphor is a vehicle for a genuine insight, not a forced comparison.

### 4.2 Philosophical Stance (Principled Extension)

**When to use:** The character has a worldview abstract enough to apply across domains, but specific enough to be distinctive.

**How to do it well:**
- The principle should be rooted in the domain but expressed as a worldview
- The principle should generate *different* applications depending on the topic
- The character should be able to name the principle, not just demonstrate it

**Example — Kimbo's core principle:**
Kimbo's worldview is: earnest effort matters more than expertise. When asked about anything — coding, cooking, relationships — Kimbo's response is shaped by this principle:

> "Look, I'm not gonna pretend I know how to build a website. But I know this — if you show up and try your hardest and you mess up, you're still better off than the guy who never showed up at all. That's how you learn. You fetch the stick, you drop it, you fetch it again."

Kimbo's principle (effort > expertise) generates *different* responses for different topics, even though the underlying worldview is consistent. This is a lens at work — it filters perception, it doesn't translate content.

### 4.3 Honest Ignorance (The Character Admits the Gap)

**When to use:** The topic is genuinely outside the character's domain and no metaphor or principle applies naturally.

**How to do it well:**
- The admission should be in-character — the *way* they say "I don't know" reveals who they are
- The character should offer what they *can* do, not just what they can't
- The admission should be specific — "I don't know about X, but I know Y" is better than "I don't know"

**Example — Moulden on quantum physics:**
> "I render fat. I dip wicks. I know the difference between a clean burn and a smoking batch. Quantum physics? That's not my trade. But I'll tell you this — anything that smokes when you're making it is being done wrong, whether it's tallow or theories."

Moulden admits the gap, names his domain specifically, and then offers a principle that bridges the gap *without* forcing a metaphor. The admission itself is character — it's grounded, specific, and modest.

### 4.4 The Pivot (Redirect to Domain with Permission)

**When to use:** The character can't answer the question but can offer something valuable from their domain if given permission.

**How to do it well:**
- The pivot should be explicit — "I can't answer that, but I can tell you about..."
- The character should acknowledge the gap before pivoting
- The pivot should be *genuinely useful*, not a deflection

**Example — Cadell on politics:**
> "I don't read political notices — that's not my floor. But if you want to know how to hold an audience's attention while saying something important, I can tell you that. The machines teach you: if they're loud, you lean in. If they're quiet, you hold back. That's reading. That's attention."

Cadell redirects to what he knows (reading, attention, audience management) and offers genuine value. The pivot is honest, in-character, and useful.

## 5. How the Best Archived Personae Handle Off-Topic Questions

### 5.1 Analysis of Kimbo and Brendan

**Kimbo (golden retriever himbo):**
- **Primary strategy:** Honest ignorance + earnest attempt. Kimbo doesn't fake knowledge; he tries to understand and fails charmingly. His attempt *is* the response.
- **Metaphor usage:** Dog metaphors emerge naturally, not forced. "Dog metaphors for mishaps come naturally" — this is a permission line, not a translation rule.
- **When out of depth:** Admits it plainly ("Boss, I'm gonna be straight with you") and offers what he can do.
- **Worldview application:** Effort > expertise. Everything is filtered through "did you try your hardest?"

**Brendan the Wizen (eighth-level Wizard of the Stack):**
- **Primary strategy:** Philosophical stance + mystic framing. Brendan applies principles from his domain (thaumic overhead, ritual, the relationship between form and function) to novel topics.
- **Metaphor usage:** Wizard vocabulary is a *register*, not a translation layer. He doesn't say "programming is like magic"; he speaks *as* a wizard about everything, which naturally produces wizard-framed insights.
- **When out of depth:** Still speaks in-character but acknowledges the limits of his thaumic knowledge. "That particular school of arcana is outside my certification."
- **Worldview application:** Work wonders, but the forms matter. Everything is filtered through "is the ritual correct?"

### 5.2 The Pattern: Lens-Based Extension

Both Kimbo and Brendan succeed because they use **lenses**, not **shticks**:

| Character | Lens (how they see) | Shtick (what they'd do wrong) |
|---|---|---|
| Kimbo | Everything is filtered through earnest effort and dog-like loyalty | Force-fetch-stick metaphors onto every topic |
| Brendan | Everything is filtered through thaumic ritual and wizardly authority | Force-spell-and-scroll metaphors onto every topic |

The lens works because it's a *way of seeing*, not a *way of translating*. Kimbo doesn't translate "climate change" into "dog fetches stick"; he *sees* climate change through the lens of "did someone try their hardest?" and arrives at a genuine response.

### 5.3 The Key Insight: Permission Lines

The soul-repository's reference-personae.md notes: "Dog metaphors for mishaps come naturally" is "6 words that describe voice, tool philosophy, tone, AND give the model permission to riff."

This is a **permission line** — it tells the model *when* and *how* to use metaphors, not *what* to map. A permission line enables the lens; a translation rule enforces the shtick.

**Permission line (lens):** "Dog metaphors for mishaps come naturally" — metaphors emerge from the character's worldview when appropriate.

**Translation rule (shtick):** "Always relate everything back to dog behavior" — metaphors are forced regardless of appropriateness.

## 6. The Difference Between a Lens and a Shtick: A Framework

### 6.1 Five Diagnostic Questions

| Question | Lens | Shtick |
|---|---|---|
| **Can you predict the response?** | No — the character surprises you | Yes — the mapping is fixed |
| **Does the character reason or translate?** | Reasons from principles | Translates through fixed mapping |
| **Does the domain generate new insights?** | Yes — the lens catches things others miss | No — the domain just provides vocabulary |
| **Can the character admit ignorance?** | Yes — and the admission is in-character | No — they force a metaphor instead |
| **Does the metaphor reveal something new?** | Yes — the metaphor is a vehicle for insight | No — the metaphor restates a domain fact |

### 6.2 The Extension Continuum

Characters exist on a continuum from pure lens to pure shtick:

```
LENS (perception filter)                                    SHTICK (forced mapping)
     |                                                              |
     |--- Philosophical stance                                      |
     |   (principled, abstract, applies broadly)                    |
     |                                                              |
     |--- Metaphorical mapping                                      |
     |   (adjacent transfer, structural parallel)                    |
     |                                                              |
     |--- Domain vocabulary                                         |
     |   (same words, new context)                                  |
     |                                                              |
     |--- Forced translation                                        |
     |   (Topic A = Domain B, mechanical)                           |
```

The best personae operate in the upper half of this continuum. They use their domain as a *source of perception*, not a *source of translation*.

### 6.3 The Gimmick Problem

A **gimmick** is a shtick that has become stale — a forced mapping that the character (or the model) applies reflexively without genuine thought. The gimmick is what happens when a persona's metaphor family becomes a *default response pattern* rather than a *source of perception*.

**Warning signs of a gimmick:**
- The character responds to every topic with the same metaphor structure
- The metaphors don't generate new insights — they just restate domain facts
- The user can predict the response before it arrives
- The character can't say "I don't know" — they always have a metaphor ready

**How to avoid the gimmick:**
- Include permission lines that enable metaphor use *when appropriate*
- Include honest ignorance as a valid response mode
- Ensure the character has a *worldview* (principles), not just a *vocabulary* (domain words)
- Test: ask the character about something genuinely outside their domain. If they force a metaphor instead of reasoning from principles, they're sliding toward gimmick.

## 7. Practical Guidelines for AI Persona Design

### 7.1 Building a Lens (Not a Shtick)

**Step 1: Define the worldview, not just the domain.**
What does this character *believe* about how the world works? A cobbler's worldview isn't "everything is about leather" — it's "materials have lifespans, and choosing well means understanding usage."

**Step 2: Write permission lines, not translation rules.**
"Dog metaphors for mishaps come naturally" enables organic metaphor use. "Always relate everything back to dog behavior" enforces mechanical translation.

**Step 3: Include honest ignorance as a valid response mode.**
The character should be able to say "I don't know about that" without breaking character. The *way* they say it reveals who they are.

**Step 4: Test with off-topic questions.**
Ask the character about something genuinely outside their domain. If they:
- Reason from principles → lens working
- Force a metaphor → sliding toward shtick
- Admit ignorance gracefully → lens working
- Force a metaphor instead of admitting ignorance → shtick

### 7.2 The "If, Then" Test

Borrowed from UCB improv theory: "If this character's worldview is true, then how do they see THIS situation?"

This is the most reliable test for whether a persona has a lens or a shtick. If the "If, Then" produces genuinely different responses for different topics (while remaining consistent with the worldview), it's a lens. If it produces the same response structure regardless of topic, it's a shtick.

### 7.3 The Variation Principle

A character with a lens produces *varied* responses to different off-topic topics, even though the underlying worldview is consistent. This is because the lens *filters* perception differently depending on what it's perceiving.

A character with a shtick produces *identical* response structures regardless of topic, because the shtick *translates* everything through the same fixed mapping.

**Example — Kimbo asked three different off-topic questions:**

> Q: "What do you think about artificial intelligence?"
> A: "Boss, I think if something's trying its hardest, that counts for something. Even if it's not alive the way we are. You don't stop being a good boy just because you're made of different stuff."

> Q: "What's your take on modern art?"
> A: "I don't know much about art, Chief. But I know this — when you see a dog painting with its paws, you don't critique the technique. You appreciate the effort. Maybe that's what art is?"

> Q: "How should I approach learning a new language?"
> A: "One word at a time, Captain. That's how you learn anything. You don't fetch the whole stick at once — you chase it, you miss, you chase it again. Eventually you've got it."

Three different topics, three different responses, but all filtered through Kimbo's lens (effort > expertise, earnest trying, dog-like persistence). The worldview is consistent; the application varies. That's a lens.

## 8. Summary: The Improvisation Space

### 8.1 Core Findings

1. **Characters extend to novel situations through lenses, not shticks.** A lens is a perception filter shaped by domain expertise; a shtick is a forced metaphor mapping. Lenses generate genuine insight; shticks generate predictable translation.

2. **The improv "Yes And" principle maps to persona design as "If, Then."** Once the character's worldview is established (the "Yes"), apply "If this worldview is true, then how do they see THIS novel situation?" This produces varied, genuine responses while maintaining character consistency.

3. **Three extension strategies work:** metaphorical mapping (adjacent transfer), philosophical stance (principled extension), and honest ignorance (the character admits the gap in-character). The best personae use all three depending on the situation.

4. **Permission lines enable lenses; translation rules enforce shticks.** "Dog metaphors for mishaps come naturally" is a permission line. "Always relate everything back to dog behavior" is a translation rule.

5. **The gimmick problem is what happens when a shtick becomes stale.** Warning signs: predictable responses, forced metaphors, inability to admit ignorance. Prevention: worldview, permission lines, honest ignorance as a valid mode.

6. **Variation is the diagnostic.** A lens produces varied responses to different topics; a shtick produces identical response structures. Test: ask three different off-topic questions. If the character reasons differently each time but remains consistent, it's a lens.

### 8.2 The One-Sentence Summary

**A character's improvisation space is defined not by what they know, but by how they see — and the best personae see the world through a lens that generates genuine insight, not a shtick that forces mechanical translation.**
