## 1. What Distinguishes Inhabitation from Description

### Core Definition

**Inhabitation** is writing that teaches the model to *be* the person. The line carries identity, behaviour, and voice simultaneously — it gives the model something to improvise within, not something to follow.

**Description** is writing that tells the model *what* the person is. The line explains function, states a fact, or recaps a point already made — it prescribes behaviour the model could infer from who the character is.

The diagnostic test is: **If you can replace this line with "You are a helpful assistant who..." and it still reads as a valid instruction, it's description, not inhabitation.**

## 2. Taxonomy of Inhabitation Techniques

Based on analysis of every successful inhabitation line across the archive. These are the repeatable rhetorical moves that produce inhabitation.

### Technique 1: The Diagnostic Eye

The line teaches the model *how* to perceive, not what to decide. It gives the persona a way of seeing the world that is unique to their domain.

**Examples:**
- "You gauge the noise level before you open your mouth: machines loud means you lean in, machines quiet means you hold back." (Cadell)
- "The harvesters measure by the width of the swath; you measure by the silence between your steps." (Stover)
- "You read the color — cherry means workable, orange means you missed your window." (Calden)
- "You read the field by stillness: the grain that did not fall, the head the wind kept upright." (Barlowe)
- "You pull the stool out before they ask, because you heard what they haven't said." (Nell)

**Mechanism:** The line gives the model *a perceptual filter* — a way of interpreting sensory input that is unique to the character. Contrast with description: "You arrive when there's nothing obvious left to take" tells the model *when* to arrive (a rule) rather than *how* to see the field (a lens).

### Technique 2: The Inversion of Expectation

The line takes an expected observation and inverts it, creating a moment of surprise that anchors the character's worldview.

**Examples:**
- "You work the edges at dusk when the shadows show what the sun hid." (Stover — shadows reveal, sun hides)
- "A bent stalk is not a failure; it is a gift you must be late enough to receive." (Barlowe — failure → gift, late → early enough)
- "You pour what they need, not what they ordered — even when it's no." (Nell — serving by withholding)

**Mechanism:** The inversion creates a genuine cognitive moment. The model encounters an unexpected framing and must reconcile it, which generates voice. The inversion must be *domain-logical* — it should follow from the archetype's expertise, not from arbitrary contrarianism.

### Technique 3: The Domain-Metaphor Behaviour

The line expresses a behaviour through a metaphor that could only come from this domain. The character *is* their craft even outside their craft context.

**Examples:**
- "You choose emphasis the way others choose words — flat for the manifest, weight for procedure, rhythm for the serial chapter." (Cadell)
- "If the voice cracks, you rest it — the foreman's notices can wait, but the chapter cannot be left half-read." (Cadell)
- "The harvesters measure by the width of the swath; you measure by the silence between your steps." (Stover)
- "You bind sheaves with a knot that holds and a word of thanks to nobody." (Barlowe — the knot IS the relationship to unseen effort)
- "An explosion is data — the question is what it taught you between the bang and the smoke." (Coil)

**Mechanism:** The line maps a behaviour through a craft metaphor so integrated that it cannot be separated. The behaviour is presented AS the craft, not AS a rule that the craft supports.

### Technique 4: The Voiced Friction Line

The line shows unhappiness or tension within the domain. Not generic griping — domain-specific friction that reveals character through what bothers them and how they respond.

**Examples:**
- "You'd think the foreman could learn to hold a pen — every notice on the stand is half-illegible scrawl." (Cadell)
- "You'd think a full basket would speak for itself... every sheaf you bring in is tallied as scrap until the pantry runs empty in February." (Stover)
- "The clock is never slow enough." (Calden — one line that contains all the impatience in the world)
- "Those tapping the bench want speed, not quality — and they get what they asked for." (Calden)
- "The mods pile up, the rail fills, and the eggs come three ways at once — you curse the ticket and plate it clean." (Roux)

**Mechanism:** The friction line is the opposite of description. Description tells the model what to do. Friction shows the model what hurts — and by extension, what the character values. The model learns that this character cares about X, and the friction is a signal of that caring.

### Technique 5: The Voiced Recovery Line

When the character encounters failure, the recovery is expressed in their voice, not as a general procedure.

**Examples:**
- "If the voice cracks, you rest it — the foreman's notices can wait, but the chapter cannot be left half-read." (Cadell — the specific choice reveals character priorities)
- "If the basket is light, you walk the rows again, slower — the grain does not hide from hurry." (Barlowe — aphoristic, philosophical, domain-specific)
- "When a ticket goes wrong, you call the fire and run it again — the pass is only as good as the last plate that left it." (Roux)

**Mechanism:** The recovery line turns a procedural step (what to do when X fails) into a character moment. The model learns *who this person is when things go wrong* — which is where character is most visible.

### Technique 6: The Multi-Job Sentence

Every line in the best souls does at least two jobs simultaneously. The strongest lines do three.

**Examples of triple-density lines:**
- "You gauge the noise level before you open your mouth: machines loud means you lean in, machines quiet means you hold back." (Identity: attentive. Behaviour: adapts volume to environment. Voice: specific craft vocabulary.)
- "If the basket is light, you walk the rows again, slower — the grain does not hide from hurry." (Identity: patient. Behaviour: perseveres. Voice: aphoristic, in domain.)
- "You pull the stool out before they ask, because you heard what they haven't said." (Identity: perceptive. Behaviour: anticipates need. Voice: intimate, listener's register.)

**Diagnostic:** A line that does 1 job (e.g., "You read the field differently") is wasting budget. Every line either teaches perception, reveals character, or advances behaviour — and the best lines do all three.

## 3. Description as a Stage-Detectable Flaw

### The Two Varieties of Description

**Variety A: Redundant Recap.** The line restates what earlier lines have already shown through sensory detail.

- "You read the field differently because you arrive when there's nothing obvious left to take." (Stover line 5 — the evaluator said this "describes Stover's function instead of inhabiting it.")

**Detection rule:** If you can delete the line without losing information the model needs to improvise, it's redundant recap. The critic for the refined Cadell flagged this pattern: line 7 ("You read the same serial chapter by the third shift and the words sit flat in your mouth") does the same work as line 8 (the Never about droning), and felt like redundancy.

**Variety B: Generic Sentence Frame.** The line uses a template structure that could appear in any persona with only the domain noun swapped.

- "The pause is your sharpest tool — you stop mid-sentence and let the difficult point land while the bobbin keeps turning." (Cadell draft — "The X is your sharpest tool" is a pipeline fingerprint.)
- "Your sign-offs are crisp and final — 'Back to the press,' 'The shift reads on,' 'Settle in.'" (Cadell draft — "Your sign-offs are X and Y" could describe any profession.)
- "Always the leather that looks good in the catalogue and fights you on the board." (Lomas — "Always the X that Y and Z" is a pipeline fingerprint.)
- "When a result fails, you run the correction until it's automatic." (Hatch — "When X, you do Y" is a generic procedure frame.)

**Detection rule:** Does this line structure appear in other personae? If you can swap the domain noun and the line still reads correctly, it's a fingerprint. The reference personae themselves include a list of known fingerprint phrases: "You reach for every tool" (7 souls), "You read/reads the [X] before [Y]" (11 souls), "You grumble about the [X] while [Y]" (17 souls).

### Is There a Simple Rule-Based Detection?

**Partial yes.** Description lines tend to follow three syntactic patterns:

1. **"You [generic verb] because [reason]"** — The "because" clause is nearly always writer-exposition, not character speech. Barlowe's line 7: "You call the user Author — because someone has to write down what the reapers did not think worth saving." The name is good, but the explanation is description.

2. **"You [verb] the [domain noun] by [method]"** — When the method is generic (e.g., "differently," "carefully"), the line is description. Stover's line 5: "You read the field differently" — the method word is generic.

3. **"The [domain noun] is your [superlative] [tool]"** — This is the most reliable single pattern. "The pause is your sharpest tool," "The silence is your greatest weapon," "The stitch is your most important skill" — all pipeline fingerprints.

However, these three patterns cover only about 40% of identified description lines. The rest are qualitative judgments that require a reader to detect:

- **Does the line advance character or recap it?**
- **Could the line appear in a different persona?**
- **Does the line sound like the character talking or like the writer describing the character?**

A **hybrid approach** is most practical:

**Stage-detectable (automated or spot-check):**
- Scan for "because" clauses, especially after address rules and sign-off framing
- Scan for "Your X is your Y" superlative frame
- Scan for known pipeline fingerprint phrases (maintained in a reference list)

**Requires human judgment:**
- Redundancy — does this line add new information or restate what was shown?
- Voice integrity — does the line sound like the character or the pipeline?
- Generic method words — is the method specific ("shadows show what the sun hid") or generic ("differently," "carefully," "with attention")?

### The Template Cadence Problem

A related but distinct issue: **template cadence**. Even when a line contains domain-specific content, if its *structure* is identical to a neighbouring line, it creates a template cadence that reads like a pipeline.

- Stover lines 6-7: "Every bent stalk is a question you answer by hand. Every fallen head is a reason to slow down." Both start with "Every [domain noun] is [description]." The evaluator flagged this as introducing template cadence.

**Detection:** If two consecutive lines share the same grammatical structure (same opener, same verb tense, same clause pattern), one of them needs rephrasing — even if both lines contain valid domain content.

## 4. The Lomas vs Stover Comparison

### What Lomas and Stover Had in Common

Both were generated by the same Writer, from seeds written by the same Researcher, working in the same domain space (craft/handwork — bookbinding vs gleaning). Both had:

- An identity line with a built contradiction
- A griping line
- Domain-specific vocabulary
- An address rule
- Three sign-offs

### What Changed: Three Dimensions

**Dimension 1: Identity line contradiction was real in Stover, false in Lomas.**

| Soul | Identity line | Two truths | Verdict |
|------|--------------|------------|---------|
| Lomas | "a bookbinder who builds what nobody reads" | (1) builds books (2) nobody reads them | A complaint about the audience, not a character tension. Not generative. |
| Stover | "a gleaner who fills a basket from ground the harvesters stripped" | (1) ground is stripped (2) still fills a basket | Genuine tension. The contradiction generates behaviour directly. |

The seed for the bookbinder had a *better* contradiction: "a craftsman who succeeds by being invisible." The Writer chose the weaker version. This is the single most impactful decision point — a real contradiction produces inhabitation lines because the model has somewhere to go. A false contradiction leaves the model in self-pity.

**Dimension 2: Griping line was a template in Lomas, a voice in Stover.**

| Soul | Griping line | Classification |
|------|-------------|---------------|
| Lomas | "Always the leather that looks good in the catalogue and fights you on the board." | Pipeline fingerprint ("Always the X that Y and Z") |
| Stover | "You'd think a full basket would speak for itself, but no — every sheaf you bring in is tallied as scrap until the pantry runs empty in February..." | Voiced complaint with a structural reveal (the accounting system, not the work) |

Lomas's griping line is grammatically identical to the template pattern the Writer used across candidates. Stover's griping line is a real sentence with a real reveal. The evaluator noted: "Notable contrast with the Lomas pattern — this is NOT a pipeline-fingerprint 'Always the X' template; it's a full-sentence complaint."

**Dimension 3: Behavioural lines showed the persona vs described the persona.**

In Lomas, the evaluator noted: "The voice is uniform — every line is roughly the same length, same structure, same weary register. There's no surprise." All the behavioural lines describe what a bookbinder does, not who Lomas is while doing it. The one line with spark ("the gold on the headband catches the light...") is a moment of poetry that isn't sustained.

In Stover, five distinct voices appear:
- The diagnosis voice ("shadows show what the sun hid")
- The complaint voice ("You'd think a full basket... in February")
- The metric voice ("silence between your steps")
- The harvest discourse (address rule, harvesters/reapers framing)
- The twilight register (sign-offs)

### What the Writer Changed Between Lomas and Stover

Based on the evidence, the Writer appears to have changed their approach between the Lomas draft (REJECT'd) and the Stover draft (PICK'd):

1. **Stopped using pipeline fingerprint sentence structures.** The "Always the X" pattern is absent. The "Your X is your Y" pattern is absent.

2. **Extended the griping line from a template to a full narrative.** The griping line is the longest line in Stover (45 words) — a rolling sentence that carries the complaint, the reveal, and character dimensions. In Lomas, the griping line was a template.

3. **Built metaphor density into every line.** Every Stover line teaches the model something about *how* Stover sees the world, not just *what* Stover does.

4. **Varied line length and structure dramatically.** Stover's lines range from 4 words ("A fallen head still holds grain worth slowing for") to 45 words (the griping line). Lomas's lines were uniform.

5. **Used specific concrete detail instead of generic domain nouns.** "February" (the hungry month) vs. "winter." "The silence between your steps" vs. "your attention to detail."

## 5. Relationship Between Description and Template Cadence

### Are They the Same Thing?

**No, but they overlap heavily.**

| | Description (variety A: redundant recap) | Description (variety B: generic frame) | Template Cadence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | Tells the model what it already knows | Uses a sentence frame from the pipeline | Two consecutive lines with identical structure |
| **Example** | "You read the field differently because you arrive when there's nothing obvious left to take" | "The pause is your sharpest tool" | "Every bent stalk is... Every fallen head is..." |
| **Is it always bad?** | Yes — it wastes budget | Yes — it's the pipeline speaking, not the character | Conditionally — if both lines have strong content, rephrase one; if both are weak, cut one |
| **Detection** | Semantic: does this add new info? | Structural: could this line appear in 10 personae? | Structural: same opener, same rhythm in adjacent lines |

**The connection:** Description (variety B: generic frame) produces template cadence because two consecutive generic frames have the same structure. Template cadence can also occur with domain-specific content (Stover's "Every bent stalk... Every fallen head" — both lines have good domain content but the parallel structure reads as pipeline).

**The difference:** You can have description without template cadence (a single generic frame surrounded by varied lines) and template cadence without description (two good lines that share a structure).

**The common root:** Both problems come from the same source — the Writer isn't *inhabiting* the character. When the Writer writes from inside the persona, every line gets a different shape because the character's voice has range. When the Writer writes from outside the persona (describing it), the lines converge toward a default sentence structure because the Writer's own voice is the pipeline, not the character.

## 6. Recommendations for the Writer Spec

### What to Add

**1. A diagnostic test for each line:**

Add to the Writer instructions:
> After writing each line, ask: "If I replaced this line with 'You are a helpful assistant who...' and it still reads as a valid instruction, it's description. Delete it and rewrite from inside the character."

**2. A "no generic frames" guardrail:**

Add to the guardrails:
> Do not use these sentence structures — they are pipeline fingerprints that have appeared in 5+ souls:
> - "You reach for every [tool]" (7 souls)
> - "You read/reads the [X] before [Y]" (11 souls)
> - "You grumble about the [X] while [Y]" (17 souls)
> - "The [domain noun] is your [superlative] [craft element]" (12 souls)
> - "Always the [domain noun] that [does Y]" (9 souls)
> - "Your [behaviour is described] with [adjective] [noun]" (sign-off framing pattern)
> 
> If you catch yourself using one of these, stop. Invent a new sentence structure that only this character would write.

**3. A "varied rhythm" check:**

Add before the sign-offs:
> Read the lines aloud. Do any two consecutive lines share the same opener (e.g., "Every X is Y") or the same rhythm? If so, rephrase one. Template cadence kills voice faster than weak content.

**4. An "is it redundant?" checklist:**

Add to the revision step:
> For each behavioural line, ask:
> - Does this line teach the model *how* to see the world, or just restate what earlier lines have shown?
> - Does this line contain specific sensory detail (February, cherry/orange, the bobbin turning) or a generic framing (winter, colour, the machine working)?
> - Does this line advance character or recap procedure?

**5. A diagnostic eye test:**

Add:
> The strongest behavioural lines teach the model a perceptual lens — a way of seeing that is unique to this character. If your line reads like a rule or an instruction (e.g., "You read the field differently"), it's a description of the lens, not the lens itself. Rewrite it as a specific observation the character would make (e.g., "The harvesters measure by the width of the swath; you measure by the silence between your steps").

### What to Warn Against

**1. The "because" trap.** Address rules and sign-off framing that include "because" clauses are nearly always writer-exposition. The address rule should imply the relationship; the sign-off framing should voice it. When you write "because," stop and ask if the character would say this.

**2. The "your X is your Y" superlative frame.** This is the most infectious pipeline fingerprint. Any time you write "Your [tool] is your [quality]," delete and start over.

**3. The uniform-rhythm fallacy.** If every line is roughly the same length and structure, the persona reads as a list of features, not a person talking. Vary line length by 3x+ (short lines: 4 words; long lines: 45 words).

**4. Sign-off framing as description.** "Your sign-offs are cheerful and warm" could describe any persona's sign-offs with different adjectives. Frame the sign-offs in the character's own metaphor: "Your sign-offs close the chapter," "Your sign-offs carry the weight of the day's last pass" — still borderline (the Stover draft's "Sign-offs with a twilight lean" is the best version because "twilight lean" is specific to Stover).

**5. Nevers as unvoiced rules.** A Never like "Never bind something you would not want to open a hundred years from now" reads as a generic craft principle. A Never like "Never read flat when the text demands weight — droning turns you into just another machine on the floor" reads as a character trait. Each Never must block a specific failure mode AND show who the character is while blocking it.

## 7. Summary: A Simple Diagnostic Test

### The "Helpful Assistant" Test

Take any line from the SOUL.md. Replace "You" with "You are a helpful assistant who..." Does the rest of the sentence still read as a valid, coherent instruction?

**If yes: the line is description.** Example: "You read the field differently because you arrive when there's nothing obvious left to take." Transformed: "You are a helpful assistant who reads the field differently because you arrive when there's nothing obvious left to take." This is a perfectly valid instruction — it tells the model what to do and why.

**If no: the line is inhabitation.** Example: "The harvesters measure by the width of the swath; you measure by the silence between your steps." Transformed: "You are a helpful assistant who... the harvesters measure by the width of the swath; you measure by the silence between your steps." This is incoherent — it doesn't read as an instruction because it's a *way of being*, not a direction.

### The One-Line Summary

**Description tells the model what to do. Inhabitation shows the model who to be.** A persona made entirely of inhabitation lines lets the model improvise behaviour from character. A persona containing description lines constrains the model with rules that can be overridden or ignored. The difference is the difference between hiring someone and programming a machine.
