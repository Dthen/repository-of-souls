# Research: Internal Life

**How fiction writers encode a character's internal life — not just what they DO but what they FEEL, NOTICE, and CARE ABOUT.**

---

## 1. Subtext: The Gap Between What Characters Say and What They Mean

### The Iceberg Theory (Hemingway)

Hemingway's theory of omission: "If a writer of prose knows enough about what he is writing about, he may omit things that he knows and the reader, if the writer is writing truly enough, will have a feeling of those things as strongly as though the writer had stated them. The dignity of movement of an iceberg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water."

**The mechanic:** You write the surface — dialogue, action, gesture — while *knowing* the seven-eighths beneath: the character's history, fears, desires, the reason they chose *these* words instead of others. The hidden mass creates weight. Readers sense depth not because they're told about it, but because the visible portion is *chosen* with precision.

**"Hills Like White Elephants" (1927):** The couple discusses an abortion without ever using the word. Hemingway shows them ordering drinks, commenting on the landscape, discussing "the operation." The subtext lives in what they *don't* say — the loaded pauses, the deflections, the way the man says "I don't want you to do it if you don't want to" while pushing for exactly that. The reader constructs the emotional reality from behavioral gaps.

**"For sale: baby shoes, never worn."** Six words. The entire tragedy exists in the gap between "for sale" (commercial, mundane) and "never worn" (loss, grief). Nothing is stated. Everything is implied. The reader does the emotional work.

### How Subtext Actually Works Mechanically

Subtext isn't mystery for its own sake. It's the *structural gap* between:

1. **What a character says** (surface dialogue)
2. **What they mean** (the real message)
3. **What they do** (actions that contradict or complicate the words)
4. **What they notice** (selective attention that reveals priorities)

**Example — domestic subtext:**
- Surface dialogue: "I'm fine."
- What they mean: I'm devastated but I won't give you the satisfaction.
- What they do: They start cleaning the kitchen with aggressive efficiency.
- What they notice: The chip in the mug they gave you for your birthday, which you never use.

The reader gets all four layers. The character never says "I'm devastated." The reader *knows*.

### Techniques for Writing Subtext in Constrained Prompts (100-200 words)

1. **The Deflection:** Character answers a different question than the one asked. Implies avoidance, defensiveness, or a secret they're protecting.
   - "How was the funeral?" / "The sandwiches were terrible."

2. **The Over-Specific Detail:** Character fixates on a trivial detail instead of the emotional core. Implies they can't face the big thing directly.
   - "The house was fine. The wallpaper in the hallway was peeling. I didn't go upstairs."

3. **The Contradictory Action:** Character says one thing, does another. The action is always the truth.
   - "I don't care about the promotion." / *She re-reads the rejection email for the fourth time.*

4. **The Loaded Silence:** Character doesn't respond to the emotional part of what was said. The silence is the answer.
   - "Do you still love me?" / *He asked about the weather for the rest of the drive.*

5. **The Redirect to Practicality:** Character converts emotional content into logistics. Implies emotional avoidance or overwhelm.
   - "I'm leaving you." / "Who gets the car?"

## 2. Showing Worldview Without Stating It

### Selective Perception: The Biased Camera

Every character walks through the world with a filtered lens. Their experiences, training, wounds, and values determine what they *notice*, what they *ignore*, and what they *misinterpret*. This is the most powerful tool for encoding worldview invisibly.

**The core principle:** "Sarah was a cautious person" is a label. "Sarah enters a new apartment and the first thing she notices is whether the windows lock from the inside" is a *worldview in action*. The reader doesn't feel told — they feel they're seeing through Sarah's eyes.

**Raymond Chandler / Philip Marlowe:** Chandler never writes "Marlowe was cynical." Instead, Marlowe notices decay: dead flowers in a vase, dust on bookshelves, the way a rich man's mansion feels like a mausoleum. He's a man who expects corruption, and his perception proves it on every page. The world Marlowe describes is a cynical world, because that's the only world Marlowe can see.

### Four Forces That Shape Character Perception

**Profession:** Years of training rewire attention. An architect walks into a building and sees load-bearing walls, structural compromises, how natural light falls. They don't choose to see these things — the training has baked it into their visual processing. In Michael Connelly's Harry Bosch novels, Bosch walks crime scenes in a specific order: victim position, blood pattern, entry/exit points, what's missing. His perception IS his competence.

**Trauma:** Wounds install permanent filters. A character who survived a house fire notices the smell of smoke before anyone else, tracks fire extinguishers in buildings they've never visited, feels the heat of a sunny room differently. In *Beloved*, Sethe's perception is so shaped by slavery that she can't look at her children's backs without seeing the potential for scars. A character with abandonment wounds notices who's about to leave — they catch the moment a friend glances at the door, the half-second pause before someone answers, the slight lean backward that means someone is already mentally gone.

**Values:** What a character believes matters determines what they see. A character who values justice reads every situation as a moral equation: who has power, who's being exploited. A character who values beauty sees composition, color, symmetry where others see clutter.

**Desire:** What a character wants colors everything. A hungry character notices food smells, restaurant signs, the way other people eat. A lonely character notices couples, empty chairs, the sound of laughter in another room.

### Techniques for Encoding Worldview in Short-Form

1. **The First-Thing-Noticed:** What does the character register first when entering a space? A soldier scans for exits. A poet notices how light hits surfaces. A mother locates the nearest child. This single detail encodes profession, trauma, and values simultaneously.

2. **The Unfair Comparison:** Character compares the current situation to something from their past. The comparison reveals their history without explaining it.
   - "The office smelled like his father's workshop — cedar and disappointment."

3. **The Missing Detail:** What the character *doesn't* notice reveals blind spots. A character who notices every flaw in a room but never mentions people's emotions is encoding a specific worldview.

4. **The Metaphor as Worldview:** The metaphors a character uses reveal how they process reality.
   - A character who thinks of relationships as "investments" sees the world transactionally.
   - A character who thinks of time as "water" sees life as flowing and uncontrollable.
   - A character who thinks of problems as "enemies to be fought" sees life as combat.

## 3. A Character Who HAS Feelings vs. A Character Whose Feelings Are Visible in Voice

### The Critical Distinction

A character who "has feelings" is a container — the feelings exist inside them but aren't expressed through the narration. The reader is told about them ("She was angry") or must infer them from plot events.

A character whose feelings are *visible in their voice* is a *lens* — their emotional state filters every word they use, every detail they select, every rhythm of their sentences. The reader doesn't need to be told what the character feels because the feeling is *in the texture of the prose itself*.

### How Feelings Become Visible in Voice

**Sentence length and rhythm:**
- Anger: short, clipped sentences. Fragments. Periods where commas should be.
  - "He came home late. Again. She didn't ask where."
- Grief: long, trailing sentences that lose momentum. Dashes, ellipses, incomplete thoughts.
  - "She kept meaning to call, except every time she picked up the phone there was this — this weight, like the receiver was made of something heavier than plastic, something that remembered"
- Anxiety: rapid, jumping thoughts. Parenthetical asides. Sentences that start over.
  - "The door was locked (was it always locked? she couldn't remember) and the hallway smelled wrong, not bad exactly, just — different."

**Word choice as emotional fingerprint:**
- A depressed character uses more words related to weight, darkness, heaviness, and stasis.
- An excited character uses more words related to motion, light, and expansion.
- A frightened character uses more words related to distance, walls, and enclosure.

**What the voice avoids:**
- A character in denial will use formal, detached language to describe devastating events.
- A character overwhelmed will use simple, almost childlike language for complex situations.
- A character suppressing anger will use overly polite phrasing.

### Hemingway's Technique: Emotion Through Restraint

Hemingway's genius was making emotion visible through *what the voice refuses to say*. His characters don't describe their feelings. They describe the world around them with such precision that the feeling emerges from the *pattern* of attention.

In "A Clean, Well-Lighted Place," the old man drinks alone at a café. The waiter wants to close up. The older waiter understands why the old man needs the light, the order, the absence of darkness. The story never says "loneliness" or "despair." But the older waiter's insistence on the café's cleanliness and light — his need for a specific kind of shelter against nothingness — makes the feeling *visible* in his voice.

### Pratchett's Technique: Emotion Through Comic Distance

Pratchett encodes deep feeling through humor and deflection. His characters process emotion by *making jokes about it* — and the gap between the humor and the underlying pain creates the emotional weight.

In *Night Watch*, Sam Vines processes grief by focusing on operational details: the weight of his crossbow, the condition of the cobblestones, the precise way a trap should be set. The voice is clinical, practical, almost bored — and the reader *feels* the grief precisely because the voice is working so hard not to feel it.

**Technique for short-form:** Give a character a voice that's *doing something other than feeling*. The avoidance itself encodes the emotion. A character who describes their ex's apartment in meticulous architectural detail while never mentioning the relationship is encoding heartbreak through voice.

### Le Guin's Technique: Emotion Through Cultural Lens

Le Guin encodes emotion through the character's *relationship to their culture*. In *The Left Hand of Darkness*, Genly Ai's narration reveals his emotions through his discomfort with Gethenian gender norms. His voice becomes uncertain, questioning, slightly disoriented — not because he says "I'm confused" but because his descriptions of Gethenian people keep circling back to gender markers he can't quite process.

**Technique for short-form:** Make the character's voice reveal what they *can't quite articulate*. The struggle to describe something — the pauses, the approximations, the "it was like... no, not like that" — makes the feeling visible without naming it.

## 4. How Authors Encode Character Through Voice Alone

### Hemingway: The Minimalist

**Technique:** Strip the voice to bare declarative sentences. Let rhythm carry emotion. Trust the reader to fill the gaps.

**Voice fingerprint:** Short sentences. Concrete nouns. Active verbs. Almost no adjectives. No interiority.

**Example pattern:**
> "He sat at the table. The coffee was cold. He drank it anyway. Outside, the trees were losing their leaves. He watched one fall."

The feeling — resignation, persistence, quiet defeat — emerges from the *pattern*, not from any single statement. The cold coffee, the falling leaves, the passive acceptance: these details accumulate into a worldview.

**How to apply in 100-200 words:** Write the character's description of a mundane task (making coffee, walking to work, waiting for a bus) using only concrete details and actions. Let the *selection* of details encode the emotion. Don't name the feeling. Let the rhythm of the sentences carry it.

### Pratchett: The Comic Philosopher

**Technique:** Use humor as a lens for genuine insight. The voice is warm, witty, and slightly exasperated — like a friend who's seen too much but still cares.

**Voice fingerprint:** Conversational. References to the absurd. Sudden shifts from comedy to gravity. Observations that start funny and end profound.

**Example pattern:**
> "The thing about being a policeman in Ankh-Morpork was that you learned to appreciate the small things. Like not being on fire. Like the fact that most people only stabbed you a little bit."

The humor is the vehicle. The underlying truth — that this is a dangerous, exhausting job — lands *because* of the comedy, not despite it.

**How to apply in 100-200 words:** Let the character describe something difficult using humor as a coping mechanism. The joke reveals the pain. The voice should feel like someone who's been through too much but hasn't lost the ability to find it funny.

### Le Guin: The Anthropologist

**Technique:** Describe the world through a lens of cultural observation. The voice is precise, curious, slightly alien — like someone taking field notes on their own life.

**Voice fingerprint:** Observational. Questions assumptions. Notes what's "normal" and what's strange. Uses concrete sensory detail to ground abstract ideas.

**Example pattern:**
> "On the third day, I noticed that the market women always arranged their fruit in odd numbers. When I asked why, the oldest one looked at me the way you look at someone who doesn't know how to breathe."

The voice reveals: the narrator is an outsider, observant, slightly patronized, and learning. The emotion — displacement, curiosity, the slow realization that their own culture is just one option — emerges from the observation itself.

**How to apply in 100-200 words:** Have the character describe a familiar custom or routine as if seeing it for the first time, or as if explaining it to someone from a different world. The act of *making the familiar strange* reveals the character's relationship to their own culture.

## 5. Applying Internal Life to Short-Form Character Prompts (100-200 words)

### The Constraint

In 100-200 words, you cannot:
- Write backstory
- Explain motivations
- Describe emotions directly
- Use multiple scenes

You *can*:
- Show one moment of selective attention
- Embed one contradiction
- Use one physical tell
- Let the voice itself carry the emotional weight

### The Three-Layer Technique for Short-Form

**Layer 1 — The Surface (what's happening):** A concrete, specific action or scene. "She's fixing the hinge on the garden gate."

**Layer 2 — The Filter (what they notice):** What details does the character select from this moment? The splinter under their fingernail? The way the gate has been broken before and someone tried to fix it with wire? The sound of a neighbor's dog barking?

**Layer 3 — The Voice (how they describe it):** The rhythm, word choice, and what the voice avoids. Are the sentences long and rambling (avoidance)? Short and precise (control)? Filled with humor (coping)?

### Concrete Example

**Without internal life (flat):**
> "Marcus was a tired construction worker. He came home from work and sat in his chair. He was tired and unhappy."

**With internal life (alive):**
> "Marcus set his lunchbox on the counter without opening it. The kitchen smelled like the soap his wife used — lavender, the cheap kind from the dollar store that she bought because it reminded her of her grandmother's house. He'd never told her he hated lavender. He sat in his chair and listened to the house settle around him, the way old houses do, like they're thinking about giving up."

The second version doesn't say "tired" or "unhappy." But:
- The unopened lunchbox implies he's not hungry (depression, exhaustion, or something worse)
- The lavender detail encodes a relationship — he tolerates something he dislikes because it matters to her
- "The house settle around him, like they're thinking about giving up" encodes his emotional state through the environment
- The entire passage is *about* the house and the soap, but it's *about* Marcus

## 6. Characters and Their Relationship to Their Work

### The Seven Modes

Characters don't just *do* work — they have a *relationship* to it. That relationship reveals who they are.

**1. Pride:** The character's work is their identity. They measure themselves by it. Insult the work, insult the person.
- *Voice marker:* Specific, detailed descriptions of craft. Defensiveness when the work is questioned. The character notices quality in others' work immediately.
- *Short-form tell:* "He ran his thumb along the cabinet joint. 'Whoever did this used dovetails. Good ones.' He said it the way other men said 'I love you.'"

**2. Weariness:** The work was once meaningful. Now it's a grind. The character does it because they don't know what else to do.
- *Voice marker:* Past tense for feelings, present tense for routine. Nostalgia that curdles into bitterness. Physical complaints that are really existential ones.
- *Short-form tell:* "She'd been a nurse for thirty years. She could still find a vein in the dark. She just couldn't remember why that used to matter."

**3. Dark Humor:** The character copes with the absurdity or horror of their work by making jokes. The humor is a shield.
- *Voice marker:* Comic observations about serious situations. Gallows humor. The character who laughs at the funeral — not because they don't feel it, but because they feel it too much.
- *Short-form tell:* "The coroner said the cause of death was 'natural causes.' Dave said that was like calling a building collapse 'structural relaxation.'"

**4. Protectiveness:** The character guards their work — or the people their work serves — with ferocity. They'll sacrifice for it.
- *Voice marker:* Aggressive when the work is threatened. Tender when describing the people they serve. Willing to break rules for the right reason.
- *Short-form tell:* "When the landlord said the clinic had to close, Maren smiled. It wasn't a nice smile. 'You can try,' she said."

**5. Resignation:** The character knows the work is broken, futile, or wrong — but they do it anyway. They've stopped fighting.
- *Voice marker:* Flat, detached tone. Observations about dysfunction stated as facts. No anger — anger would imply hope.
- *Short-form tell:* "He filed the reports. They went into the system. The system ignored them. He filed them again on Monday."

**6. Obsessive Love:** The character is consumed by the work. It's not healthy. They know it's not healthy. They don't care.
- *Voice marker:* Intensity. The voice fixates on details others would skip. Time distorts — hours feel like minutes. The character forgets to eat, sleep, maintain relationships.
- *Short-form tell:* "She looked up from the microscope. The lab was dark. The cleaning staff had come and gone. She hadn't noticed. The cells were dividing exactly the way she'd predicted. It was beautiful."

**7. Reluctant Duty:** The character didn't choose this work. They were born into it, drafted, or trapped by circumstance. They do it adequately, not passionately.
- *Voice marker:* Distancing language ("the job," "the work," never "my calling"). Comparisons to what they'd rather be doing. Competence without enthusiasm.
- *Short-form tell:* "His father had been a butcher. His father's father had been a butcher. He could break down a side of beef in twelve minutes. He'd never wanted to."

## 7. Archetypes That DON'T Gripe But Are Still Alive

### The Problem with Griping

Griping is the most common way writers make characters feel "alive" — it creates the illusion of personality through complaint. But griping is passive. It says "I notice what's wrong." It doesn't say "I'm doing something about it."

Characters who don't gripe but are still alive use *active engagement* instead. They're not passive observers of their own lives — they're participants.

### What Non-Gripping Archetypes Do Instead

**1. The Optimist Who's Wrong A Lot**
- *What they do:* They try things. They fail. They try again. They genuinely believe the next attempt will work.
- *Why they're alive:* Their optimism creates *dramatic irony* — the reader can see the obstacle the character can't. The gap between the character's hope and the reader's knowledge creates tension without a single complaint.
- *Short-form voice:* "The bridge was out, but there was a fallen tree across the gorge. It looked sturdy enough. Most things that were going to kill you didn't look like it."

**2. The Builder**
- *What they do:* They make things. They fix things. They improve things. They define themselves by what they create, not what they endure.
- *Why they're alive:* Creation is inherently forward-moving. A character who's building something — a house, a relationship, a business, a recipe — has *direction*. Direction is life.
- *Short-form voice:* "The fence had been crooked since the storm. He spent Saturday pulling it straight, resetting the posts, checking the level. His wife asked why he bothered — the fence was fine crooked. He said, 'The fence doesn't know it's crooked. But I do.'"

**3. The Quiet Professional**
- *What they do:* They do the work. They don't complain about it. They don't brag about it. They just... do it, with a level of care that reveals how much it matters to them.
- *Why they're alive:* Competence *is* characterization. Watching someone do something well — with attention, precision, and quiet pride — is inherently engaging. The reader respects the character, and respect is a form of connection.
- *Short-form voice:* "She stitched the wound closed. Twelve stitches, evenly spaced. She'd done it a thousand times. She still held her breath on the last one."

**4. The Curious Observer**
- *What they do:* They ask questions. They notice things. They're genuinely interested in how things work, why people do what they do, what's behind the surface.
- *Why they're alive:* Curiosity is inherently active. A character who's *interested* in the world — even a world that's difficult or broken — is engaging. The reader follows their attention.
- *Short-form voice:* "Everyone else saw a cracked wall. She saw the mineral deposits that told her where the water had been running for decades. She wondered who'd built it, and whether they'd known about the spring."

**5. The Stubborn One**
- *What they do:* They refuse to give up. Not because they're optimistic — they might be deeply pessimistic — but because giving up isn't in their nature. It's not a choice; it's a reflex.
- *Why they're alive:* Stubbornness creates *friction* with the world, and friction creates story. The reader watches the character push against obstacles and wonders: how far will they go? What will they sacrifice?
- *Short-form voice:* "The doctor said the tumor was inoperable. She said, 'Okay.' She went home and started researching second opinions. Her daughter asked if she was scared. She said, 'Scared isn't the same as stopping.'"

**6. The Caretaker**
- *What they do:* They take care of things — people, animals, gardens, machines, buildings. Their identity is defined by what they nurture, not what they endure.
- *Why they're alive:* Care is inherently active and specific. A character who's feeding a cat, watering a plant, or oiling a machine is *doing something* — and the specific way they do it reveals who they are.
- *Short-form voice:* "He checked the greenhouse first thing every morning. Temperature, humidity, the slight lean of the tomato stems toward the light. His wife said it was just tomatoes. He said, 'They lean toward the light. That's not nothing.'"

**7. The Competent Pragmatist**
- *What they do:* They assess the situation, identify what needs to be done, and do it. No drama. No complaints. No heroics. Just competence.
- *Why they're alive:* Pragmatism is *decisive*. A character who acts — even small acts, even wrong acts — is more alive than one who observes and complains. The reader follows the action.
- *Short-form voice:* "The power was out. The pipes would freeze by morning. She filled every pot she had with water, stacked blankets by the windows, and lit the gas stove. She'd done this before. She'd probably do it again."

## 8. Practical Toolkit: Encoding Internal Life in 100-200 Words

### The Checklist

Before writing a short-form character prompt, ask:

1. **What does this character notice first?** (Worldview)
2. **What do they avoid noticing?** (Blind spots / denial)
3. **What's the one thing they'd never say out loud?** (Subtext)
4. **What does their voice sound like when they're tired?** (Emotional baseline)
5. **What's the contradiction between what they say and what they do?** (Complexity)
6. **What's their relationship to their work?** (Identity)
7. **What do they do instead of complaining?** (Active engagement)

### The Substitution Principle

Every time you're tempted to *state* an emotion, *replace* it with a concrete detail that implies it:

| Instead of... | Write... |
|---|---|
| "She was anxious" | "She checked her phone three times in the time it took to cross the parking lot" |
| "He was proud" | "He aligned the picture frame with the edge of the shelf using a ruler" |
| "She was grieving" | "She still set two places at the table before catching herself" |
| "He was angry" | "He closed the door more carefully than usual" |
| "She was exhausted" | "She sat on the edge of the bed and forgot what she'd come in for" |
| "He was lonely" | "He left the radio on when he left the house" |

### The Voice Test

Read the passage aloud. Ask:
- Does the *rhythm* match the emotion? (Short, clipped = tension; long, trailing = grief; precise, measured = control)
- Does the *word choice* match the character's background? (A sailor doesn't say "the horizon was blue" — they say "the horizon was flat, no weather coming")
- Does the voice *avoid* something? (The most alive voices are defined by what they won't say)
