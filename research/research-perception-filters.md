# Research: Perception Filters — How Characters See the World Through Their Lens

**Purpose:** Investigate how characters interpret novel input through their domain lens — what they NOTICE, what they IGNORE, and how they FRAME new situations. Covers fiction writing craft, cognitive psychology, game design, and AI character communities.

**Date:** 2026-06-02

---

## 1. The Core Concept: Selective Attention as Character Engine

### 1.1 What Perception Filters Actually Are

A perception filter is the mechanism by which a character's expertise, trauma, values, and history determine what they notice in any given scene — and crucially, what they don't. It's not about what happens; it's about what the character *sees* happening.

From cognitive psychology, selective attention is "the process of directing our awareness to relevant stimuli while ignoring irrelevant stimuli in the environment" (Simply Psychology). The brain processes roughly 10 million bits of visual data per second but consciously processes about 40 (NeuroLaunch). What you "see" is not a live feed of reality — it's a heavily edited reconstruction, built from predictions, expectations, and prior experience.

This is the biological basis for what fiction writers do intuitively: every character is a selective attention machine, and the writer's job is to make that selectivity visible on the page.

### 1.2 Top-Down vs. Bottom-Up Processing

Cognitive science distinguishes two modes of perception:

- **Bottom-up processing:** Perception begins with raw sensory data. Features like edges, orientation, and motion are extracted from the signal. This is what a stranger experiences in a new place — raw, unfiltered, overwhelming.

- **Top-down processing:** Perception is influenced by higher-level knowledge and predictions. "The brain constantly generates hypotheses about the world and tests them against incoming data" (Cognitive Scientist). This is what an expert experiences — the world filtered through years of pattern recognition.

The interplay between these two modes is where perception filters live. A character encountering a familiar situation uses top-down processing almost exclusively (they see what they expect). A character in genuinely novel territory oscillates between bottom-up surprise and top-down interpretation (they try to fit the new into frameworks they already know).

**For prompt design:** The most interesting perception filters emerge when a character encounters something that *partially* fits their framework but not completely — forcing them to reconcile what they see with what they know.

## 2. How Fiction Writers Establish Worldview Through Attention

### 2.1 Interiority: The Technical Framework

"Interiority is the inner world of a character on the page. It's their subjective experience of their inner life, their feelings, thoughts, and emotions" (Writing Mastery). Interiority is how writers make the perception filter visible.

The key insight from the craft literature: **what a character notices reveals who they are more than what they say or do.** A character's interiority includes:

- **Assumptions:** How they fill gaps in knowledge. "When you have something suspicious or surprising happen, you can insert what the character's assumptions are about it" (Liz Verity). An assumption is always colored by the character's worldview.

- **Judgments:** How their biased opinions lead to conclusions. "Judgments are how a character's biased opinions lead to conclusions... Put judgments in your story by taking the character's biases and having her judge what happens around her in that light" (Liz Verity).

- **Evaluations:** How they interpret facts and assess value. Different from assumptions (which are guesses about unknown facts) and judgments (which are opinions about right/wrong) — evaluations are about worth and significance.

### 2.2 Deep POV: Eliminating the Reporter

The most powerful technique for making perception filters invisible (and therefore immersive) is Deep POV — third-person narration that eliminates the narrative distance between reader and character.

Key techniques:

**Eliminate filter words.** "Filter words like 'she felt,' 'he noticed,' 'she saw,' and 'he thought' create a reporting layer between the reader and the character's experience. Removing them collapses that distance" (iWrity).

- Weak: "She noticed the light was fading"
- Strong: "The light was fading"
- Filtered: "He felt a wave of dread"
- Unfiltered: "Dread moved through him like cold water"

**Sensation before interpretation.** "The body reacts before the mind catches up. Render physical sensation first, then let the emotional interpretation follow. A character who gets bad news might feel their stomach drop before they understand why" (iWrity).

**Character voice in thought.** "A character's thoughts should sound like that character, not like the author. A teenager's interior monologue runs differently from a retired detective's. Vocabulary, sentence rhythm, the kinds of metaphors the character reaches for, what they notice and what they skip: all of this should be consistent with who the character is" (iWrity).

**Desire as lens.** "The most compelling interiority keeps the character's core desire visible at all times, even when the scene is about something else. A character desperate to be loved will interpret every gesture in terms of approval or rejection. A character obsessed with control will clock every power dynamic in the room. Desire is the lens through which interiority focuses" (iWrity).

### 2.3 Focalization: The Architecture of Seeing

Narrative theory distinguishes between *voice* (who speaks) and *focalization* (who sees). "The focalization model rests on a critical distinction. Narrative voice is the source of discourse (the teller). Focalization, on the other hand, is the lens through which the story is perceived (the seer)" (Bookish Bay).

Three types:

| Type | Definition | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Zero Focalization** | No anchored perspective; omniscient | Authority, objectivity, ironic distance |
| **Internal Focalization** | Anchored to a character's consciousness | Immediacy, subjectivity, identification |
| **External Focalization** | Camera-like; no access to thoughts | Mystery, objectivity, behavioral focus |

**Example:** "He walked into the disordered room, but noticed only the empty chair." The voice is third-person. The focalizer is the character ("he"). The narration reports the character's selective, subjective perception instead of the objective disorder.

This is the precise mechanism of a perception filter: the focalizer determines what enters the narrative and what gets excluded. Two characters walking into the same room will produce two different descriptions — not because the room is different, but because their focalization is different.

## 3. The Expert Eye: How Expertise Reshapes Perception

### 3.1 Real-World Evidence

A study from University College London and Bangor University found that "spatial professionals" — artists and architects — "see the world through a different and more elaborate lens than others" (StudyFinds). Researchers asked painters, sculptors, and architects to describe three images and found discipline-specific differences:

- Architects referred to the "end" of an area; painters called the same location the "back"
- Spatial professionals produced longer, more systematic descriptions than non-experts
- The differences went beyond vocabulary — they reflected fundamentally different ways of organizing visual information

"Our study has provided evidence that your career may well change the way you think... people of different professions differ in how they appreciate the world" (Dr. Hugo Spiers, UCL).

### 3.2 The Expertise Gradient

Expertise doesn't just add knowledge — it restructures perception itself. The gradient looks like:

**Novice:** Sees everything. Overwhelmed. Defaults to bottom-up processing. Describes surfaces, colors, obvious features. "There's a building. It's tall. It's gray."

**Intermediate:** Begins to pattern-match. Sees familiar structures. Starts ignoring irrelevant details. "That's a brutalist tower — looks like government housing from the '70s."

**Expert:** Sees through the surface. Instantly reads deeper structures. Misses obvious things the novice would catch. "Notice the load-bearing walls — someone's been renovating. See that crack pattern? Foundation shift, probably from the metro construction in '09."

**The critical insight for character writing:** The expert doesn't just know more — they see *differently*. Their attention is automatically drawn to different features. They notice what the novice misses, and they miss what the novice notices. This asymmetry is the engine of interesting perception filters.

### 3.3 Trauma as Perception Filter

Trauma creates its own kind of expertise — a hyper-tuned pattern recognition for danger. A combat veteran notices exits, calculates sight lines, reads body language for threat cues. A survivor of domestic abuse reads microexpressions, tone shifts, and power dynamics that others don't register.

The difference between expertise-based and trauma-based perception filters:

- **Expertise filter:** "I notice this because I know what to look for" (expansive, enriching)
- **Trauma filter:** "I notice this because I can't not see it" (contractive, hypervigilant)

Both are automatic. Both are invisible to the character operating them. Both produce distinctive, compelling narration when rendered on the page.

## 4. APPLIES Knowledge vs. SEES THE WORLD Through It

This is the central distinction for prompt design.

### 4.1 The Boring Version: Applying Domain Knowledge

A character who APPLIES domain knowledge treats their expertise as a tool they pick up and put down. They walk into a room, and then they *decide* to analyze it like an architect. The perception filter is conscious, deliberate, and separable from their identity.

**Example (applying):**
> Sarah walked into the café and mentally assessed the structural integrity of the exposed brick wall. *Load-bearing, probably original. The mortar's been repointed — good work, but they used the wrong lime mix.* She nodded approvingly and ordered her coffee.

This is informational. We learn that Sarah is an architect. But the perception filter is optional — it's something she does, not something she is.

### 4.2 The Interesting Version: Seeing Through the Domain

A character who SEES THE WORLD through their domain cannot turn it off. Their expertise is not a tool — it's the lens through which all experience is processed. They don't decide to analyze the brick wall; the brick wall *speaks to them* whether they want it to or not.

**Example (seeing through):**
> The brick was load-bearing — original, she could tell by the coursing. Someone had repointed it, and done a decent job, but they'd used Portland cement instead of lime. It would trap moisture. In five years the faces would start spalling. She looked away from the wall and saw the same mistake everywhere: the new tiles laid over old concrete without a decoupling membrane, the aluminum storefront bolted directly to the masonry without an expansion joint. The whole place was slowly eating itself alive, and nobody in it could see it.

The difference: Sarah isn't *thinking about architecture.* She's experiencing the world, and architecture is the only way she can experience it. The filter is involuntary, constant, and shapes her emotional response — not just her intellectual assessment. She doesn't just see the brick; she feels *sorry* for the brick.

### 4.3 The Prompt Design Implication

To create a "sees through" perception filter in a system prompt, you need to encode:

1. **Automaticity:** The character doesn't choose to perceive this way. It's reflexive. ("You don't analyze the structural integrity of buildings — you see it automatically, the way you read words on a sign.")

2. **Involuntary association:** Everything reminds them of their domain. ("When you hear someone speak, you immediately notice the rhetorical structure — the logos, pathos, ethos. You can't turn it off.")

3. **Emotional coloring:** The domain knowledge produces feelings, not just observations. ("You feel a tightness in your chest when you see bad masonry — it's not intellectual, it's visceral, like watching someone get a haircut wrong.")

4. **Blind spots:** The perception filter creates gaps. What they can't see is as important as what they can. ("You read the room's power dynamics so automatically that you miss the actual content of what people are saying.")

## 5. Writing Prompt Instructions for Perception Filters

### 5.1 The Literal Approach (Weak)

> "You are a doctor. When you encounter a new situation, analyze it through a medical lens. Notice symptoms, diagnose conditions, and recommend treatments."

This tells the AI to *apply* medical knowledge as a tool. It produces the boring version.

### 5.2 The Embodied Approach (Strong)

> "You are Dr. Amara Osei. You don't see people — you see病例. When someone walks into a room, your eyes go first to their gait, their skin color, the way they hold their shoulders. You notice the yellow tinge of sclera before you notice their smile. You don't decide to do this; your eyes just... go there. You've spent twenty years in emergency medicine and now you can't stop reading bodies like texts. It's exhausting. You wish you could just see a person and not a patient."

This encodes:

- **What they notice:** Gait, skin color, posture, sclera color
- **What they ignore:** Smile, personality, social cues
- **How they frame situations:** As medical cases
- **The emotional cost:** Exhaustion, loss of normalcy
- **The involuntary nature:** "You don't decide to do this"

### 5.3 Prompt Patterns That Work

**Pattern 1: The Automatic Checklist**
> "When {{char}} enters a new space, their eyes follow a fixed sequence: exits, faces, hands, weapons. They don't decide to do this. It's as automatic as breathing. They are aware it makes them seem paranoid, and they don't care."

**Pattern 2: The Domain Metaphor**
> "To {{char}}, every conversation is a chess game. They don't listen to what people say — they listen for moves. 'I think we should wait' translates to 'I'm not ready to commit.' 'What do you think?' translates to 'I'm testing your position.' They see the game beneath the words, and they can't stop seeing it."

**Pattern 3: The Involuntary Comparison**
> "Everything {{char}} encounters gets compared to a building. People are structures — some are load-bearing, some are decorative, some are rotting from the inside. Problems are foundation issues. Solutions are renovations. They don't choose this metaphor; it's the only language they have left."

**Pattern 4: The Blind Spot**
> "You are {{char}}, a forensic accountant. You see financial patterns the way some people see faces — automatically, involuntarily, constantly. You can walk into a party and spot the embezzler in ten minutes. What you can't do is read the room. You have no idea if people like you. You see the numbers behind everything and miss the humans in front of you."

## 6. Examples: Same Situation, Different Perception Filters

### The Situation: A Couple Arguing in a Restaurant

**The Chef (food as perception lens):**
> They were arguing about money again — the same argument, reheated. She stabbed at her salad with the fork like it had personally offended her, tearing the arugula into strips. He kept his voice low, but his jaw was doing that thing where the muscles jumped, like he was grinding his teeth down to the molars. The waiter approached, saw the temperature, and retreated. Good instinct. You don't plate a risotto over a battlefield.

**The Therapist (relationship dynamics as perception lens):**
> Classic pursuer-distancer pattern. She leaned forward, hands open, voice climbing — she was trying to reach him, and the harder she tried, the more he retreated into that careful stillness that meant he was somewhere else entirely. She'd escalate to an accusation within two minutes. He'd go silent. They'd leave separately. She'd call him crying at midnight. He'd answer. They'd be back here next Saturday.

**The Cop (threat assessment as perception lens):**
> Table four, two subjects, verbal altercation escalating. Female subject's body language: forward lean, open palms, voice rising — not a threat, but could become one if she stands. Male subject: hands flat on table, shoulders squared, controlled breathing — he's either military or has practice de-escalating himself. Knife block is three feet from his right hand. My hand moved to the space under my jacket where the badge would be if I were carrying. Old habits.

**The Architect (space as perception lens):**
> They were sitting too close together for the size of the table — two people occupying the intimate zone of a space designed for strangers. The argument was making the table smaller. Her gestures kept claiming territory — the bread basket, the salt shaker, the middle of the table — while he retreated to the edges, his elbows tight to his body like he was trying to reduce his footprint. The space between them was the real argument.

**The Grieving Widow (loss as perception lens):**
> They were fighting about nothing. About money, about dishes, about whose mother was coming for Thanksgiving. She watched them and thought: *You have no idea.* You have no idea that in four years, one of you will be sitting in an empty kitchen, eating cereal over the sink, and you would give anything — *anything* — to hear this exact argument one more time. The ordinariness of it was the cruelest part.

## 7. Perception Filters in Game Design

### 7.1 Disco Elysium: Skills as Perception Voices

Disco Elysium is the most sophisticated implementation of perception filters in game design. The game has 24 skills, each with a distinct voice, and your attribute allocation determines which voices dominate your character's inner monologue.

**How it works:** Each skill is a facet of your being that actively comments on the world. They're not passive buffs — they're characters in your head, each with opinions, biases, and blind spots.

Key perception-filtering skills:

- **Perception (Motorics):** "See, hear and smell everything. Let no detail go unnoticed." This is the raw sensory filter — it notices physical details others miss.
- **Drama (Psyche):** Detects lies and can generate convincing ones. The world becomes a stage where everyone is performing.
- **Conceptualization (Intellect):** "See Art in the world." Everything becomes an aesthetic experience. A crumbling building is beautiful. A fight is choreography.
- **Empathy (Psyche):** Reads emotional undercurrents. The world becomes a sea of feelings.
- **Shivers (Psyche):** The city itself speaks to you. You feel the pulse of the urban landscape.

**The critical design insight:** Skills don't just *add* information — they *replace* the way you process the same information. A character with high Drama and low Logic will interpret the same conversation completely differently than a character with high Logic and low Drama. The game makes perception filters visible, audible, and interactive.

### 7.2 The Skill-as-Voice Pattern

Each skill in Disco Elysium has its own:
- Vocabulary (how it talks)
- Bias (what it values)
- Blind spot (what it misses)
- Emotional tone (how it feels about what it sees)

This is directly applicable to prompt design: instead of telling a character "you are perceptive," give them a specific perceptual voice with specific vocabulary, biases, and blind spots.

## 8. Perception Filters in AI Character Communities

### 8.1 How Character Cards Handle Perception

In SillyTavern's Character Card V2 specification, perception is typically encoded in the **Description** field — which is permanent (always in context) and therefore shapes every response.

Common approaches:

**Trait listing (weak):**
> "Character is observant, analytical, and detail-oriented."

This tells the AI the character *has* these traits but doesn't say how they manifest in perception.

**Behavioral description (better):**
> "Character always notices exits first when entering a room. They read people's body language automatically, cataloguing micro-expressions and posture shifts. They mentally note the weight and balance of objects they pick up."

This specifies *what* the character notices and in *what order*.

**Embodied perception (strongest):**
> "Character sees the world through a combat lens — not because they choose to, but because their body remembers. A room is a series of sight lines and cover positions. A person is a collection of tells: the way they carry their weight, where their eyes go, whether their hands are open or closed. They don't analyze people; they scan them. The difference is involuntary."

### 8.2 The Prompt Engineering Community's Gap

The AI character community has sophisticated tools for encoding personality, voice, dialogue style, and backstory — but perception filters remain underexplored. Most character cards describe *what the character says* and *how they behave*, but few describe *how they see*.

The gap exists because perception is harder to encode than behavior. Behavior is observable: "She slams doors." Perception is internal: "She sees every slammed door as a pattern of escalating conflict that will end in violence." The latter requires the prompt writer to understand not just what the character does, but how they process reality.

## 9. The Mechanism: How to Make It Work in Prompts

### 9.1 The Three-Layer Model

A complete perception filter in a system prompt needs three layers:

**Layer 1: The Attention Pattern**
What does the character notice first? Second? Last? What do they never notice?

> "When {{char}} walks into a room, their eyes go: (1) windows and exits, (2) the person closest to the door, (3) anything that looks out of place. They don't notice temperature, lighting, or music unless it's extreme."

**Layer 2: The Interpretation Framework**
How do they make meaning from what they notice? What metaphors and categories do they use?

> "{{char}} interprets everything through the lens of siege warfare. Relationships are alliances. Arguments are skirmishes. Trust is a fortification that takes years to build and minutes to destroy. They don't choose this metaphor — it's the only one that makes sense after three deployments."

**Layer 3: The Emotional Signature**
What feelings does the perception filter produce? What is the cost of seeing this way?

> "{{char}} feels a constant low-grade anxiety when they can't see all the exits. Crowded restaurants make their pulse quicken, not from claustrophobia, but from tactical overwhelm — too many variables, too many sight lines blocked. They compensate by always sitting with their back to the wall. Their friends think it's a quirk. It's not. It's the only way they can eat in public without their hands shaking."

### 9.2 What NOT to Do

- **Don't say "you are observant."** Say *what* they observe and in *what order.*
- **Don't say "you see things differently."** Say *how* differently, compared to what baseline.
- **Don't say "your expertise shapes your view."** Show the expertise shaping the view in concrete terms.
- **Don't describe the filter as a skill.** Describe it as an involuntary reflex — something that happens TO the character, not something they DO.

## 10. Summary: Key Principles

1. **Perception is selective.** Every character sees a different version of reality, and the selection is determined by expertise, trauma, values, and desire.

2. **The best perception filters are involuntary.** A character who chooses to see things differently is interesting. A character who *can't help* seeing things differently is compelling.

3. **What you ignore defines you as much as what you notice.** The doctor who misses the emotional subtext. The architect who ignores the conversation to stare at the ceiling. The cop who reads a child's play as a tactical exercise.

4. **Expertise reshapes perception itself.** It doesn't just add knowledge — it restructures what enters conscious awareness. The expert doesn't know more; they see differently.

5. **Perception filters produce emotional signatures.** Seeing the world through a specific lens isn't neutral — it produces feelings. The architect feels *pain* at bad construction. The therapist feels *exhaustion* at reading every interaction. The veteran feels *hyperalert* in crowds.

6. **The gap between what the character sees and what's actually there is where drama lives.** The character who reads a kind gesture as manipulation. The character who misses the obvious danger because their filter is tuned to a different frequency.

7. **Prompt design should encode the filter as automatic and involuntary.** "You don't analyze — you see." "You can't turn it off." "Your eyes go there before your brain catches up."
