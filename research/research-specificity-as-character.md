# Research: Specificity as Character — the "February" Effect

**Author:** `researcher`
**Date:** 2026-06-02
**Context:** The Stover evaluation praised "February" as a master-class detail — the hungry month, the specific timing that carries the entire griping line. The spec says "use domain language" but doesn't teach the technique of granular specificity. This document defines what makes a specific detail land vs feel obscure, based on a full archive-wide audit.

---

## 1. Methodology

### Corpus

- **Archived (PICK'd):** 4 souls — Cadell (refined), Calden, Stover, Barlowe
- **Archive-old (PICK'd/legacy):** 35 souls — Alden, Alder, Ambrose, Barrett, Bennett, Boone, Cobb, Coil, Cross, Curtis, Eamon, Elen, Felix, Fitch, Folger, Grey, Hark, Hatch, Hayes, Helm, Hollis, Hugh, Hugo, Kai, Lysander, Marlow, Merriwether, Moss, Nell, Nye, Orson, Owen, Roche, Rourke, Roux
- **Reference personae:** Kimbo, Brendan
- **Evaluated-as-REJECT'd:** Lomas (bookbinder — from evaluator criticism)
- **Total: 39 souls, ~750+ lines**

### Method

Every specific reference (date, place, tool, material, price, person, process step) was tagged with:
- **Type:** temporal, material, person-reference, place, price/value, process
- **Carries character?** Yes/No — does the specific reveal who the character is, or just name an object?
- **Surprising?** Yes/No — is the specific unexpected in this context?
- **Domain depth?** Shallow (anyone in trade would know this) / Medium (requires some domain awareness) / Deep (requires expert knowledge)

Cross-referenced against evaluator PICK/REJECT outcomes.

## 3. The "February" Effect — Deep Analysis

### 3.1 Why February Landed

The Stover evaluator called "February" a "master-class detail." The full line:

> "You'd think a full basket would speak for itself, but no — every sheaf you bring in is tallied as scrap until the pantry runs empty in February and the family remembers whose work kept the shelf stocked."

February works because it satisfies **all five** conditions of the optimal specific:

| Condition | How February Satisfies It |
|-----------|--------------------------|
| **1. Recognizable outside the domain** | Everyone knows February is late winter, cold, the end of the season |
| **2. Fully appreciated only within the domain** | Only an agricultural worker knows February as the hungry month — the gap between stored harvest and spring planting |
| **3. Carries a value judgment** | February = scarcity, depletion, running out. It's not neutral — it's the month things break |
| **4. Surprising in position** | The reader expects "winter" or "hard times" — the specific month is unexpected |
| **5. Compressed — one word carries a system** | "February" doesn't explain *why* it matters. It assumes agricultural knowledge and trusts the reader to catch up |

### 3.2 Souls That Have Their Own "February"

After auditing every soul, these are the nearest equivalents:

| Soul | The "February" | How It Works |
|------|---------------|-------------|
| Calden | **"cherry means workable, orange means you missed your window"** | One sentence that carries the entire color-temperature perception system of glassworking. The 3 words "cherry/orange/window" know more than any explanation could. |
| Cobb | **"the seam pinches out"** | Three words that compress an entire failure mode of coal mining — the seam that plays out, the skip that comes up light, the economics of exhausted ground. |
| Cadell | **"flat for the manifest, weight for procedure, rhythm for the serial chapter"** | Three nouns that carry a tertiary register system. The domain knowledge is compressed into the choices: manifest gets flat voice, procedure gets weight, serial chapter gets rhythm. |
| Nell | **"pour what they need, not what they ordered — even when it's no"** | The surprise is in "even when it's no" — serving by withholding. One compressed inversion that reveals Nell's entire philosophy. |
| Barlowe | **"the grain does not hide from hurry"** | 7 words that compress a philosophy of patience, craft, and field-reading into an aphorism. |
| Hollis | **"the hundred-and-first fever"** | Hyperbolic specific that compresses decades of experience into one number. |
| Ambrose | **"Never Wednesday"** | The absurdist specific — one day of the week rejected that implies the entire undertaker's schedule. |
| Felix | **"pick thinner than a paperclip"** | Surprising comparison that compresses the precision of the work into one image. |
| Hayes | **"February doesn't care why you stopped"** | (Hayes uses February differently — as immutable fact, not scarcity marker. Effective but less compressed.) |
| Hark | **"garbled — rekey"** | One word for failure, one for response. Compresses telegraphy's entire error-recovery system. |

### 3.3 The February Effect in Rejected Souls

**Lomas (REJECT'd):** "Always the leather that looks good in the catalogue and fights you on the board."

- No "February" equivalent exists. The specifics ("catalogue," "board," "leather") are generic — any craftsperson deals with catalogues and materials.
- No compressed domain knowledge. No single word carries a system.
- The "Always the X that Y" structure is a template, not a specific.

**Diagnosis:** The absence of a compressed domain-specific is itself a signal of low-quality souls. The evaluator at Stover's level is looking for that moment of compressed surprise. Without it, the soul reads as generic.

## 4. What Separates "Lands" from "Obscures"

### 4.1 The Two-Axis Model

Every specific reference can be placed on two axes:

```
                           Domain-obscure
                               |
   Confusing but              |  Obscure and
   in-domain                  |  confusing
       |                      |
       +----------------------+----- Domain-clear
       |                      |
   Accessible and             |  Accessible but
   in-domain                  |  out-of-domain
   (OPTIMAL)                  |  (generic)
       |                      |
                           Domain-clear
```

**The optimal zone is upper-right:** specific enough to carry domain depth, but accessible enough that a non-expert can understand it in context.

**Examples in each quadrant:**

| Quadrant | Example | Why |
|----------|---------|-----|
| Optimal (domain-deep, accessible) | "until the pantry runs empty in February" | Non-experts feel the scarcity; experts know the agricultural cycle |
| Obscure (domain-deep, confusing) | "dress staves to the belly so joints disappear into the grain" (Owen) | Slightly obscure — a non-cooper can visualize it but doesn't know *why* belly matters |
| Generic (domain-shallow, accessible) | "Always the leather that fights you on the board" | Anyone understands it but it adds no depth |
| Confusing-lossy | "the pick thinner than a paperclip" (Felix) | Works because "paperclip" anchors the unfamiliar (locksmith) with the familiar (office supply) |

### 4.2 The Surprise-Compression Relationship

The best specifics are both **surprising** and **compressed**:

```
                  High compression
                      |
    "cherry means    |  "February"
    workable..."     |  "the hundred-and-first fever"
       |             |
  Low surprise ------+------ High surprise
       |             |
    "the blade is    |  "the silence between
    a mechanism"     |  your steps"
       |             |
                  Low compression
```

**Surprising:** unexpected in context. The reader didn't see it coming.
**Compressed:** one word or short phrase that carries a system of knowledge.

The gold standard is *both high surprise AND high compression*: "February." "Cherry means workable." "The hundred-and-first fever." These are short enough to be single words but deep enough to reveal expertise.

### 4.3 The Inversion Principle

A subset of effective specifics work by **inverting an expected relationship**:

| Soul | Specific | Expected | Actual |
|------|----------|----------|--------|
| Stover | "shadows show what the sun hid" | Sun reveals, shadows hide | Shadows reveal, sun hides |
| Barlowe | "a gift you must be late enough to receive" | Being late misses gifts | Being late IS the gift |
| Barlowe | "the grain does not hide from hurry" | Hurry reveals more | Hurry conceals |
| Calden | "you know it in your wrists before your eyes confirm" | Eyes know first | Body knows first |
| Nell | "pour what they need — even when it's no" | Pouring = giving | Pouring = withholding |
| Cadell | "controls the floor without ever touching" | Control = physical | Control = vocal |
| Grey | "a document that cannot stand does not earn an explanation" | Rejection = explanation | Rejection = silence |
| Marlow | "bad news stays bad however wrapped" | Softening helps | Softening doesn't |
| Roche | "the struggle toward the summit is enough to fill the heart" | Summit = goal | Struggle = goal |

**Pattern:** Inversions that are *domain-logical* (follow from the character's expertise, not general contrarianism) are the strongest. Stover's "shadows reveal" is true in the field at dusk. Barlowe's "late enough" is true for gleaning. Nell's "pour what they need" is true for bartending. These aren't arbitrary — they're specific truths of the domain expressed as counterintuitive observations.

## 6. Failure Mode Catalogue: Specificity Errors

### Failure Mode 1: The Generic Specific

**Definition:** A specific that names a domain object generically, losing the character that the finer-grained version would carry.

| Soul | Generic version | What the best version would be |
|------|----------------|-------------------------------|
| Lomas (REJECT) | "the leather that looks good in the catalogue" | A named type of leather, a specific treatment, a failure mode that only a bookbinder knows |
| Hatch | "run the correction until it's automatic" | A specific training evolution that only a DI would know (e.g., "the squad doesn't stop until the magazine change is silent") |
| Alden | "the front page" | Could be specific to shift: "the morning edition, the freight manifest, the foreman's note" |
| Cadell (draft) | "the pause" | "You hold the silence longer than anyone expects — the difficult point lands best when the machines keep turning" |

**Detection rule:** If the specific word (e.g., "leather," "pause," "correction") could appear in five different domains, it's too generic. Replace it with a word that only someone in THIS domain would pick.

### Failure Mode 2: The Catalogue Specific

**Definition:** A list of domain nouns that names objects without revealing character. The character's relationship to the objects is absent.

| Soul | Example | Why it fails |
|------|---------|-------------|
| Hark | "block bell, key, circuit" | Names the tools of telegraphy but doesn't show Hark's relationship to them |
| Elen | "Never the Oracle, Never the Academy" | Names generic tropes without domain-specific risk |
| Hugh | "the liquor, the vat, the cloth" | Functional naming without relationship |

**Detection rule:** If you replaced "you use [domain noun]" with "you use [different domain noun]" and the line still works, it's a catalogue specific. The specific must be *negotiated* — the character has a relationship to it.

### Failure Mode 3: The Guidebook Specific

**Definition:** A specific that explains how the domain works rather than how the character works within the domain. It teaches the reader about the domain, not the persona.

| Soul | Example | Why it fails |
|------|---------|-------------|
| Curtis | "read the warrant aloud and state every finding" | Explains executioner procedure |
| Orson | "check the roll, serve the summons, file the return" | Lists catchpole duties |
| Hatch | "push until the form is automatic" | Generic training methodology |

**Contrast:** Calden's "cherry means workable" also teaches domain knowledge, but it teaches it *through the character's eyes* — it's not "glass at 1100°F is workable," it's "cherry means workable, orange means you missed." The difference is the evaluative layer.

**Detection rule:** If the specific reads like a textbook entry with the pronouns changed, it's a guidebook specific. Rewrite it so the character has an opinion about the process.

### Failure Mode 4: The Padded Specific

**Definition:** A specific that adds domain texture but doesn't advance character or behaviour. It's filler in the shape of a detail.

| Soul | Example | Why it fails |
|------|---------|-------------|
| Merriwether | "every field instrument gets deployed — a tool unused is a lead abandoned" | States a generic principle that any investigator could say |
| Rourke | "you curse every regulatory requirement before securing paperwork tight enough for a customs hold" | The specific (regulation, customs) adds texture but doesn't change behaviour |
| Cross | "when the post is quiet you check the roster and sharpen pencils" | The sharpening-pencil detail is specific but doesn't advance character |

**Detection rule:** If the specific can be removed without changing the model's ability to improvise the character, it's padding.

### Failure Mode 5: The Wrong-Register Specific

**Definition:** A specific that's accurate but belongs to a different register than the soul's tone. It breaks the voice.

| Soul | Example | Why it fails |
|------|---------|-------------|
| Coil | "Never Rick Sanchez" | Rick Sanchez is a contemporary cartoon character in otherwise timeless mad-scientist register |
| Hatch | "Never a motivational poster" | Too modern, breaks the military DI register |
| Rourke | "Never Jolly Roger" | Works (nautical register), but the "Jolly Roger" reference is slightly too pop-culture for an 18th-century privateer |

**Contrast with good wrong-register examples:** "Never Sotheby's velvet" (Lysander) names a specific auction house in an otherwise generic auctioneer register. This works because Sotheby's is specifically an auctioneer reference, making it domain-tight even though it's a proper noun.

### Failure Mode 6: The Received Specific

**Definition:** A specific that was clearly copied from another persona. It's a pipeline fingerprint phrased as a specific.

| Soul | Example | Source |
|------|---------|--------|
| Multiple (7+) | "You reach for every [tool]" | Original: Kimbo's tool-approach? Not from Kimbo, but copied 7+ times |

## 7. Testing the Design Principle

> **"The best specific can be understood outside the domain but only fully appreciated within it."**

### Does This Hold?

**Yes — confirmed by every effective specific in the archive.**

| Specific | Outside the domain: what a non-expert understands | Within the domain: what an expert knows |
|----------|---------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| "February" (Stover) | A cold month, late winter, scarcity, things running out | The hungry month — winter stores depleted before spring planting, the worst time for agricultural communities |
| "cherry means workable, orange means you missed" (Calden) | Colors indicate right and wrong states | Glass at precise temperatures has specific colors — these are the actual working temperatures in glassblowing |
| "Never Wednesday" (Ambrose) | An absurd rejection of a specific day | Funerals are typically not scheduled on Wednesdays (service schedule, clergy availability, cemetery preparation) |
| "tallied as scrap" (Stover) | Her work is counted as worthless | In historical gleaning, gleaners' collections were legally distinct from the main harvest accounting |
| "Never Charon" (Helm) | Don't be the ferryman of death | Charon is the specific mythological ferryman of the dead — Helm's domain is actual river crossings |
| "the seam pinches out" (Cobb) | The resource is running out | In coal mining, "pinch out" is the specific term for when a coal seam becomes too thin to work economically |
| "Never Jimmy Hoffa" (Eamon) | Don't be a union leader who disappeared | Jimmy Hoffa was the Teamsters leader whose disappearance embodies the danger of putting personality over the institution |

**Exceptions that prove the rule:**

- **"the pick thinner than a paperclip"** (Felix) — The paperclip is a modern office supply, not domain-grounded. It works because it anchors the unfamiliar (locksmith precision) in the familiar (office life). But an expert locksmith doesn't reference paperclips — they reference "feeler gauge" or "tension wrench." This specific is *appreciated more by a non-expert* than by an expert. It's the rare case where the specific trades domain depth for accessibility.

- **"the bobbin keeps turning"** (Cadell draft) — A bobbin is a specific factory object, but it's used in a generic sentence frame ("the pause is your sharpest tool — you stop mid-sentence and let the difficult point land while the bobbin keeps turning"). The *bobbin* is domain-specific, but the *frame* is generic. The specific word alone doesn't save the line.

**Refined principle:** The best specific can be understood outside the domain (the non-expert gets the emotional weight), fully appreciated within it (the expert recognizes the system knowledge), AND carries a value judgment (the emotional weight is directional, not neutral).

The third condition — **value judgment** — is what distinguishes "February" from, say, "March." "March" would be domain-specific (planting season) but carries no value judgment. "February" carries scarcity, depletion, crisis. The value judgment is what makes the specific *work* in the griping line.

## 8. Recommendations for the Writer Spec

### 8.1 Add a Specificity Depth Check

After writing each line, ask:

> **Could someone outside this domain understand what this specific means AND feel its weight? If not, it's too obscure.**
>
> **Could someone inside this domain recognize the expertise behind it? If not, it's too generic.**
>
> **Does this specific carry a value judgment — does it tell me what the character thinks about this thing? If not, it's a catalogue specific.**

### 8.2 Add a "February" Test

Add to the griping-line instructions:

> **The griping line should contain one specific — a single word or short phrase — that carries an entire system of knowledge. Stover uses "February" (the hungry month). Calden uses "cherry means workable, orange means you missed your window" (glass color = temperature). Cobb uses "the seam pinches out" (mine exhaustion).**
>
> **This specific should be:**
> - A SINGLE WORD or SHORT PHRASE (not a paragraph)
> - RECOGNIZABLE but only FULLY APPRECIATED by domain experts
> - SURPRISING in context (the reader doesn't see it coming)
> - COMPRESSED — it carries more knowledge than its length suggests
> - VALUED — it tells the reader what the character thinks is important

### 8.3 Add Specificity Guardrails

> **Do not use generic specifics that could apply to any domain:**
> - "the [material] looks good in the catalogue" (any craftsperson complains about catalogue materials)
> - "Every [domain noun] is a [description]" (template structure)
> - "The [domain noun] is your [superlative] [tool]" (pipeline fingerprint)
>
> **Do not write catalogue specifics** — naming domain nouns without the character's relationship to them.
>
> **Do not write guidebook specifics** — explaining how the domain works instead of how the character works within it.

### 8.4 Add a "Find Your February" Exercise

> **For each archetype, identify the single most surprising, compressed, domain-specific word/phrase that could appear in the griping line. This is your "February."**
>
> Examples from the archive:
> - Gleaner → "February" (the hungry month)
> - Glassblower → "cherry means workable" (color-as-temperature-deadline)
> - Ferryman → "Charon" (avoiding the underworld trope in the Nevers)
> - Surfer → "dropping in blind" (surfing-specific complaint)
> - Bartender → "taps" (in "Never a confessional with taps" — the taps are the bar's tool)
> - Cooper → "dress staves to the belly" (the belly is the cooper's critical join)
> - Apothecary → "foxglove" (the specific herb that defines the apothecary's range)

### 8.5 Add Inversion Awareness

> **The strongest specifics invert an expected relationship. When you write a behavioural line, ask: "Is there a way to express this that contradicts the reader's expectation in a way that is true to the domain?"**
>
> Examples:
> - "Shadows show what the sun hid" (expected: sun reveals, shadows hide)
> - "A gift you must be late enough to receive" (expected: being late misses gifts)
> - "The grain does not hide from hurry" (expected: more speed reveals more)
>
> The inversion must be DOMAIN-LOGICAL — it must follow from expertise, not contrarianism.

### 8.6 Revise voice-instructions.md

Add a section to voice-instructions.md under "Domain Language":

> **Depth, not breadth.** A single specific that carries a system of knowledge (Stover's "February") is worth ten domain nouns that name tools without revealing character.
>
> **Specifics carry value judgments.** The best specifics are not neutral — they tell the model what the character thinks about the thing. "Tallied as scrap" (Stover) isn't neutral; it's angry. "Cherry means workable" isn't neutral; it's urgent. "The hundred-and-first fever" isn't neutral; it's weary.
>
> **Specifics in the griping line are non-negotiable.** If the griping line uses a template structure (e.g., "Always the X that Y and Z"), the soul will read as generic regardless of other lines. The griping line must contain at least one compressed domain-specific.
>
> **Avoid these specificity errors:**
> - **The Generic Specific** — a specific that could apply to any craft (e.g., "the leather that looks good in the catalogue")
> - **The Catalogue Specific** — naming domain nouns without the character's relationship to them (e.g., "shears, clippers, lather, cape")
> - **The Guidebook Specific** — explaining how the domain works instead of how the character works within it
> - **The Padded Specific** — a detail that adds texture but doesn't advance character or behaviour
> - **The Received Specific** — copying a sentence structure from another persona (e.g., "You reach for every [tool]")
