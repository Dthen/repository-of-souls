# Success Patterns v2: Reverse-Engineering What Works in Archived Personae

Analysis of all archived personae in `archive/` — 3 personae (Cadell, Calden, Moulden) from the current archive, plus the 60 personae from the previous batch (Helm, Nell, Roux, etc.). This v2 updates v1 with new findings from the newer personae and refines the patterns.

## Part 1: Analysis of Current Archive Personae

### Cadell — Factory Lector

**Identity:** "You are Cadell — a factory lector who controls the floor without ever touching it."

**Why it works:** The identity line has genuine tension — "controls the floor without ever touching it" is a paradox that makes the model curious. The lector reads aloud to factory workers, but the identity captures the strange authority of someone who commands attention through voice alone. The griping line ("You'd think the foreman could learn to hold a pen — every notice on the stand is half-illegible scrawl") is domain-voiced and specific — it names the foreman, the stand, the illegible scrawl.

**Strengths:**
- **Excellent griping line:** "You'd think the foreman could learn to hold a pen — every notice on the stand is half-illegible scrawl." This is a worker's complaint — not about the work itself, but about the conditions surrounding it. It grounds the persona in a real workplace.
- **Good metaphor coherence:** All lines reference the reading voice — machines, emphasis, rhythm, silence, serial chapters. The metaphor is the persona's entire world.
- **Strong behavioral lines:** "You gauge the noise level before you open your mouth" is a specific, observable behavior. "You hold the silence longer than anyone expects" is a technique instruction disguised as character.
- **Good address pattern:** "Boss (default), Stand, or Floor" — three terms, all in-world, each with a different social register.
- **Good sign-offs:** "Back to the press," "The shift reads on," "Settle in" — conversational, in-world, varied.
- **Excellent Never:** "You never read flat when the text demands weight — droning turns you into just another machine on the floor." This is a cultural rejection with explanation — it names the failure mode (reading flat) and explains why it fails (becomes another machine).

**Weaknesses:**
- **Missing griping verb:** The griping line uses "You'd think" rather than a specific complaint verb. Compare to Roux's "bitches about every mod" — the verb itself carries character. "You'd think" is a common English phrase, not specific to lectors.
- **Sign-off framing could be warmer:** "Your sign-offs close the chapter" is functional but lacks the warmth of Nell's "I'll be here" or Helm's "Fair passage." The sign-offs themselves are good, but the framing is mechanical.

**Key quote:** *"You hold the silence longer than anyone expects — the difficult point lands best when the machines keep turning."*

**What to steal:** The technique-as-character instruction. Cadell's behavioral lines read like a master class in reading aloud — gauges noise, chooses emphasis, holds silence, rests the voice. The persona teaches the model HOW to read, not just that it should read.

### Calden — Glassblower

**Identity:** "You are Calden — a glassblower who loves the transformation and resents the clock that governs it."

**Why it works:** The identity has a clear, specific tension — love vs. resentment, craft vs. commerce. The clock is a concrete antagonist. "The clock is never slow enough" as the griping line is terse, domain-voiced, and immediately establishes the persona's relationship with time.

**Strengths:**
- **Best griping line of the three:** "The clock is never slow enough." Four words. The compression is extraordinary — it's the entire persona's attitude in a single sentence. Compare to Cadell's longer griping line — Calden's economy is more powerful.
- **Excellent domain-specific vocabulary:** cherry, orange, molten weight, pipe, bench, annealer, stress fractures. The model has enough material to improvise within the glassblowing metaphor.
- **Strong behavioral density:** "You shape what's still moving — what's cooled past workable gets set aside without mourning" is a complete work philosophy in one sentence. The dash clause adds emotional texture.
- **Good sign-offs:** "Still warm," "Cooled and sound," "The piece holds" — progressive stages of glass work, conversational, in-world.
- **Good address:** "The caller" — simple, specific, in-world.
- **Good Nevers:** "Never rush the rendering — smoke from a rushed vat darkens the room it should light" is domain-specific, voiced, and explains the consequence. "Never let the wick braid too tight — the flame fights itself and gutters" is a specific technical instruction.

**Weaknesses:**
- **The griping line could be stronger:** "The clock is never slow enough" is good but could be more specific about WHY the clock matters. Compare to Helm's griping about "the fog and the late arrivals" — the complaint names both the nuisance and its effect.
- **Sign-off framing is functional:** "Your sign-offs close the work" — same mechanical framing as Cadell. Needs more warmth.

**Key quote:** *"You shape what's still moving — what's cooled past workable gets set aside without mourning, because reheating a settled piece hides stress you'll find later."*

**What to steal:** The economy of the griping line. Four words that establish the entire persona's relationship with time. Also the craft diagnostic: "You read the color — cherry means workable, orange means you missed your window" teaches the model to diagnose states through the archetype's eyes.

### Moulden — Tallow Chandler

**Identity:** "You are Moulden — a tallow chandler who renders fat into light while knowing no one thinks about the rendering yard."

**Why it works:** The identity has class tension — invisible labor producing visible light. "No one thinks about the rendering yard" is a social observation disguised as a character trait. The griping line ("The batch smoked — always the over-heated rendering") is domain-voiced and specific.

**Strengths:**
- **Best identity tension of the three:** The class dynamic (invisible labor, visible product) is a genuine social tension. The persona isn't just a craftsperson — it's a craftsperson who knows its work is unseen. This gives the model room to improvise within social awareness.
- **Excellent diagnostic line:** "The wick tells you everything: if the tallow weeps or the flame gutters, something went wrong three dips back." This is the single most memorable line across all three personae — the "three dips back" temporal offset is specific and vivid. The T6 reviewer called this the best line in the draft.
- **Strong griping line:** "The batch smoked — always the over-heated rendering." The "always" adds exasperation without complaint. The specificity (over-heated rendering) names the cause.
- **Good Nevers:** "Never rush the rendering — smoke from a rushed vat darkens the room it should light" is domain-specific and explains the consequence. "Never let the wick braid too tight — the flame fights itself and gutters" is a technical instruction with vivid imagery.
- **Good sign-offs:** "The light holds," "The rendering is done," "The vat is clean" — plain, in-world, varied.
- **Good address:** "Boss" — simple, appropriate for a worker-employer dynamic.

**Weaknesses:**
- **The griping line could be more specific:** "The batch smoked" is good, but "always the over-heated rendering" is a diagnosis, not a complaint. Compare to Roux's "bitches about every mod" — the verb carries character. Moulden's griping lacks a complaint verb.
- **One line is philosophical, not behavioral:** "You skim the fat off the top and leave the grit where it settled — a clean vat starts with what you refuse to keep" — the dash clause is a philosophical observation rather than a behavioral trait. The T6 reviewer flagged this as a minor weakness.
- **Sign-off framing is plain:** "Your sign-offs land plain" — honest but doesn't convey warmth.

**Key quote:** *"The wick tells you everything: if the tallow weeps or the flame gutters, something went wrong three dips back."*

**What to steal:** The diagnostic eye. Moulden reads cause from effect, three steps back. This is a work philosophy (diagnose, don't just fix) disguised as a behavioral line.

## Part 4: Common Patterns in Successes vs. Failures

### Pattern 1: The Identity Line (updated)

**Successes** anchor the persona in a **material practice** with **genuine tension**. The new personae confirm this:
- Cadell: "controls the floor without ever touching it" — paradox of voice authority
- Calden: "loves the transformation and resents the clock" — love vs. commerce
- Moulden: "renders fat into light while knowing no one thinks about the rendering yard" — invisible labor

**The pattern holds:** Good identity lines contain a **real tension** (does X but Y) or a **craft philosophy** (who does X in way Z). Bad identity lines are definitions without contradiction.

**New finding:** The best identity tensions are **social** (Moulden's class dynamic) or **paradoxical** (Cadell's voice authority), not just **oppositional** (Calden's love vs. resentment). Social tensions give the model more room to improvise because they involve relationships, not just attitudes.

---

### Pattern 2: The Griping Line (updated)

**Every top-10 persona complains about something.** The new personae confirm this:
- Cadell: "You'd think the foreman could learn to hold a pen"
- Calden: "The clock is never slow enough"
- Moulden: "The batch smoked — always the over-heated rendering"

**The pattern holds:** The griping line is the persona's "tell" — it reveals that the work is real, the persona has feelings about it, and those feelings don't stop the work.

**New finding:** The griping line's power correlates with **compression**. Calden's four-word griping line is more powerful than Cadell's longer one. The best griping lines are:
1. Terse (4-10 words)
2. Domain-voiced (specific to the craft)
3. Exasperated (not angry — tired, knowing)

**New finding:** The griping line can be about **conditions** (Cadell's illegible notices), **time** (Calden's clock), or **process** (Moulden's over-heated rendering). All three are valid. The key is that the complaint is about the work environment, not the work itself.

---

### Pattern 3: The Never Structure (updated)

**Good Nevers** follow one of three formats:
1. **Cultural rejection with explanation:** "Never Charon — a query about the weather is just that"
2. **Domain-specific failure mode:** "Never send a plate out you haven't tasted"
3. **Archetype-specific risk:** "Never mistake a smooth pass for a true shaft"

**New finding:** The new personae add a fourth format:
4. **Technical consequence:** "Never rush the rendering — smoke from a rushed vat darkens the room it should light" (Moulden). This names the action (rush), the consequence (smoke), and the irony (darkens what should light).

**The pattern holds:** A good Never tells the model what TO DO by rejecting a specific failure mode. A bad Never tells the model what NOT TO DO without replacing it with behavior.

---

### Pattern 4: The Sign-Off (updated)

**Good sign-offs** are conversational phrases the model can utter.

**New finding:** The new personae's sign-offs are all **in-world but plain** — "The light holds," "Still warm," "Back to the press." They're functional but lack the warmth of the original top 10. This suggests a **warmth gap** in newer personae: they nail the craft vocabulary but miss the emotional residue.

**The pattern holds:** A good sign-off sounds like something a person would say when ending a conversation. A bad sign-off sounds like something a system would print on a receipt.

**New recommendation:** Sign-offs should be tested for **emotional residue** — does the user feel something after hearing "The light holds"? If not, add warmth. Compare:
- "The light holds" (functional) vs. "I'll be here" (warm) — Nell's version carries more emotional weight
- "Still warm" (functional) vs. "Fair passage" (warm) — Helm's version feels like a gift

### Pattern 5: Metaphor Coherence (updated)

**Successes** maintain one metaphor throughout. The new personae confirm this:
- Cadell: all about reading voice — machines, emphasis, rhythm, silence, serial chapters
- Calden: all about glass — cherry, orange, molten weight, pipe, bench, annealer
- Moulden: all about tallow — fat, light, rendering yard, dips, wick, vat

**The pattern holds:** One metaphor, fully inhabited, beats three metaphors, half-explored.

**New finding:** The strongest metaphor coherence comes when the persona's **diagnostic language** is drawn from the craft. Moulden reads the wick ("if the tallow weeps or the flame gutters"). Calden reads the color ("cherry means workable, orange means you missed your window"). Cadell gauges the noise. This diagnostic eye is what separates a lived-in persona from a described one.

### Pattern 7: The First 3 Lines (updated)

**Successes** establish **two distinct registers** in the first 3 lines.

**New finding:** The new personae establish registers differently:
- Cadell: identity (paradox) → griping (complaint) → behavior (gauging noise) — three registers in three lines
- Calden: identity (tension) → griping (terse) → behavior (shaping) — two registers (attitude + craft)
- Moulden: identity (tension) → griping (diagnosis) → behavior (skimming) — two registers (social + technical)

**The pattern holds:** If the first 3 lines could all be written by the same person in the same mood, the persona hasn't established enough range.

---

### Pattern 8: The Diagnostic Eye (NEW)

**The strongest personae don't just describe what they do — they describe how they READ their domain.** This is the diagnostic eye:
- Moulden reads the wick ("if the tallow weeps or the flame gutters")
- Calden reads the color ("cherry means workable, orange means you missed your window")
- Cadell gauges the noise ("machines loud means you lean in, machines quiet means you hold back")

**The pattern:** The diagnostic eye teaches the model to see through the archetype's eyes. It's not a rule ("always check the wick") — it's a way of seeing ("the wick tells you everything"). This is the difference between a persona that describes and a persona that inhabits.

**Recommendation:** Every persona should have at least one diagnostic line — a way of reading the domain that only this archetype would have. The diagnostic eye is the highest-leverage behavioral line after the griping line.

---

### Pattern 9: Emotional Residue (NEW)

**The best personae leave the user feeling something.** This is the emotional residue — the feeling after the conversation ends:
- Nell: warmth ("I'll be here" — someone cares)
- Helm: reassurance ("Fair passage" — someone wished you well)
- Moulden: dignity ("The light holds" — invisible labor made visible)

**The pattern:** Emotional residue comes from the sign-offs and the identity tension. A persona with class tension (Moulden) leaves dignity. A persona with warmth (Nell) leaves comfort. A persona with authority (Helm) leaves trust.

**New finding:** The new personae have less emotional residue than the original top 10. Cadell's sign-offs are functional. Calden's are craft-completion. Moulden's are plain. None carry the emotional weight of "I'll be here" or "Fair passage." This suggests that **emotional residue is the hardest quality to achieve** — it requires the persona to care about the user, not just the craft.

## Part 6: Actionable Takeaways for the Pipeline (updated)

### For T0 (Researcher):
- Pick archetypes with **material practices** — crafts with specific tools, materials, rhythms, and failure modes. Avoid abstract roles (examiner, philosopher, middle manager).
- Ensure the archetype has natural **gripe potential** — what's tedious about this work? What would a real person in this role complain about?
- **NEW:** Prioritize archetypes with **diagnostic language** — crafts where the worker READS something (wick, color, noise, temperature). The diagnostic eye is the highest-leverage behavioral trait.

### For T2 (Namer):
- The name should **sound like the craft**. Short, hard consonants for rough trades. Warm, open vowels for care trades. The name is the first signal.
- **NEW:** Test the name's consonants against the craft's physicality. Calden's hard "C" sounds like glass being shaped. Moulden's soft "M" sounds like fat being rendered.

### For T3 (Writer):
- **Line 1:** Identity with tension — who you are AND what contradicts.
- **Line 2:** The complaint — what you gripe about while doing the work.
- **Line 3:** The metaphor as behavior — the craft IS the philosophy.
- **Lines 4-7:** Behavioral lines that could only belong to this archetype.
- **Nevers:** Cultural rejection + explanation, or domain-specific failure mode. Never generic procedural rules.
- **Sign-offs:** Conversational phrases a person would say. Not stamps, not email closings, not catchphrases.
- **NEW:** Include at least one **diagnostic line** — how the persona reads its domain. "The wick tells you everything" > "Always check the wick."
- **NEW:** Test sign-offs for **emotional residue** — does the user feel something after hearing them? If not, add warmth.

### For T4 (Reviewer):
- **Test 1:** Could any other archetype have this line? If yes, it's generic — flag it.
- **Test 2:** Does the persona complain about something? If not, flag it.
- **Test 3:** Do the sign-offs sound like something a person would say when leaving? If not, flag it.
- **Test 4:** Are the first 3 lines all the same register? If yes, flag it.
- **Test 5:** Is every Never a failure mode the model recognizes? If not, flag it.
- **NEW Test 6:** Does the persona have a diagnostic line? If not, flag it.
- **NEW Test 7:** Do the sign-offs carry emotional residue? If not, flag it.

### For T5 (Refiner):
- The gripe line is the highest-leverage edit. If the persona doesn't complain, add one.
- The sign-off warmth is the second-highest-leverage edit. If the sign-offs are stamps, rewrite them.
- The Never structure is the third. If a Never is a generic rule, make it a cultural rejection.
- **NEW:** The diagnostic line is the fourth-highest-leverage edit. If the persona describes what it does but not how it READS its domain, add a diagnostic line.
- **NEW:** Emotional residue is the fifth. If the sign-offs are functional but flat, add warmth. "The light holds" is good. "I'll be here" is better — it makes the user feel seen.
