# Research: Want Construction

**The positive, operational procedure for deriving a pursuable want from an emotional fantasy — the missing counterpart to the corpus's rejection of sentiment-as-want.**

**Purpose:** `research-character-interest.md` rejects sentiment-as-want ("wants to help people — that's a job description", CI:323) but offers no method for DERIVING a pursuable want (with want/need gap + lie) from an emotional fantasy. The beekeeper seed died exactly there (stage-researcher.md:91). This file is that method: fantasy → verb → obstacle → sacrifice → lie → need.
**Date:** 2026-08-07

---

## 1. The Corpus Gap: A Rejection Without a Replacement

The corpus has the negative test but not the positive procedure:

- **CI:323 — The Want Test:** "Does the character want something specific enough to sacrifice for? (Not 'wants to help people' — that's a job description.)"
- **stage-researcher.md:60:** "if the want can't be stated as a verb phrase with a conflict, kill"
- **stage-researcher.md:91 — the beekeeper post-mortem:** "Feeling, not want — the fantasy is a sentiment ('being talked to like a person') with no want that generates conversation and no lie beneath it."

The kill-list (stage-researcher.md:90–95) diagnoses the failure modes but does not prescribe the repair. The seed template (stage-researcher.md:105–108) demands an Emotional Fantasy AND a Want/Need/Lie — but nothing in the corpus says how the second is derived from the first. The idea-time audit (stage-researcher.md:58–69) checks the want *after* the fantasy; this file supplies the derivation the audit assumes. Per the 06-02 research-track pattern (deep research first, cited evidence, THEN spec application), this is a research deliverable only — it does not modify the spec.

## 2. Why Want Is the Engine (The Research)

### 2.1 The corpus's own case

- **CI:44–46:** Wanting is "the single most powerful engine of interestingness." "A content character is not going to shake things up... But characters who yearn, desire, lust, ache, crave — those are characters who make fascinating choices" (Good Story Company).
- **CI:21 (Callender):** "It's our wanting — our needs, longing, and thirst — that make us interesting, *especially when that thing we want isn't easily gettable. Or, when the thing we think we want is not the thing we need.*"
- **CI:105–114:** The want (conscious goal) vs. the need (unconscious truth), plus "the lie the character believes" (screenwriting: McKee, Truby, Snyder). "The story is about the gap between the lie and the truth closing." CI:48: "The gap between want and need is where interestingness lives."
- **CC:18–20 (research-character-creation.md:18–20):** "The Want-Need gap creates behavior. When a character's want conflicts with their need, they make interesting choices. When an AI persona's purpose conflicts with its temperament, it generates interesting responses." No gap = no engine.

### 2.2 The craft sources: want as verb-with-conflict

External sources sharpen the corpus's *want* from a noun into a mechanism:

- **Fawkes — want → goal chain:** "The ghost creates the protagonist's want... The want creates a goal" (Frozen's Elsa: the want to be isolated manifests as the goal of building an ice castle to live in alone). And: "while that want may seem big and abstract sometimes, it will manifest in specific, concrete goals."
- **Fawkes — the lie as anti-theme:** "Every truth has a lie. This is the anti-thematic statement... Often the want taps into the anti-thematic statement." The want is not adjacent to the lie — it *expresses* it.
- **Fawkes — the sacrifice:** "The protagonist must at least be willing to sacrifice the want to get the need." And the hollow-victory warning: when a character gets the want without the need, "it creates a hollow victory" (Zootopia's Judy).
- **Weiland — the want arises from the lie:** "The Want is a desire arising from the limited perspective (based upon the Lie the Character Believes) with which the character begins the story." Scrooge's want (amass great wealth) is *motivated by* his lie ("a man's worth can only be measured by material means").
- **Weiland — concreteness:** The desire "should be refined to its most concrete point"; "the desire *creates* the goal, while goal is a practical attempt to obtain the desire"; "The plot goal is the practical manifestation of the desire."
- **Weiland — the obstacle:** "We often hear 'conflict is story.'... conflict is driven by opposition. That opposition stands between the characters and something they want." No opposition, no want — only a preference.
- **Weiland — the lie has been functional:** "up until this moment in the character's life... the Lie has probably been *good* for the character, at least comparatively. After all, there must be a reason the character has clung to this limited belief for so long."

### 2.3 Five properties of an engine-want (synthesis)

| # | Property | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **It is a verb** — something the character does, not a state they are in | stage-researcher.md:60 ("verb phrase with a conflict") |
| 2 | **It is observable** — it manifests in concrete goals | Fawkes (want → goal); Weiland ("refined to its most concrete point") |
| 3 | **It has an obstacle** — opposition between the character and the want | Weiland ("opposition stands between the characters and something they want") |
| 4 | **It costs something** — "specific enough to sacrifice for" | CI:323; Fawkes (sacrifice; hollow victory) |
| 5 | **It sits on a lie** — the false belief *motivates* the pursuit | Weiland (want arises from the lie); Fawkes (want taps the anti-theme); CI:112–114 |

| Engine-want | Mood (rejected) |
|---|---|
| "Wants every complaint properly entered, stamped, filed" | "Wants to be known" |
| Obstacle in the same sentence ("...though the moon reads nothing") | No obstacle |
| Costs something | Costs nothing |
| Built on a lie that makes the work possible | No belief at stake |
| Enacted in conversation | Only reported in conversation |

## 3. The Derivation Procedure: Fantasy → Want in Five Moves

**Input:** the emotional fantasy — what interacting with this persona should FEEL like, in one phrase (stage-researcher.md:49, :105). **Output:** want / need / lie.

**MOVE 1 — Name the gift.** The fantasy is user-side: it describes what the *user* receives. Write it as "the user gets ___." (Being heard with full ceremony. Being talked to like a person.) If you cannot complete that sentence, the fantasy is not yet a fantasy — it is a vibe.

**MOVE 2 — Flip the gift into the character's verb.** The fantasy names the relationship; the want must name the character's *side* of it. Ask: "what does the character DO that makes this gift happen?" The want is that doing, stated as "to ___": *to make the complaint exist* (the moon clerk writes, stamps, files); *to make the hive heard* (the derived beekeeper tells the news). This is the step the beekeeper skipped. If the gift traces to no verb the character performs, the fantasy is scenery, not a seed (stage-researcher.md:91–94: the want must generate conversation; stage-researcher.md:60: verb phrase with a conflict).

**MOVE 3 — Ask what blocks it.** The obstacle is what makes the want pursuable: without opposition there is nothing to do, only something to feel (Weiland, §2.2). For a persona the best obstacles are *permanent* — uncrossable in-world facts — because the want never expires: the moon reads nothing; people hear honey, not bees.

**MOVE 4 — Price it: what would they sacrifice?** "Specific enough to sacrifice for" (CI:323). The sacrifice must be real and in-world. The moon clerk sacrifices the hope of an answer — the filing becomes the whole cure (seed:7). If the honest answer is "nothing," the want is a preference, not an engine.

**MOVE 5 — Set the lie beneath it.** The lie is load-bearing: it converts the permanent obstacle into a solvable procedure, which is why the character can work at all (Weiland: the lie has been *good* for the character; the want arises from it). Moon lie: "if it's written down, it's been said" (seed:7) — the ledger is a comfort, not a channel. Without the lie, the character would have to look at the obstacle directly and stop.

**THEN — name the need as the gap.** The need is what the want cannot deliver (Fawkes: want without need = hollow victory). Moon need: to admit the moon answers no mail — that the filing *is* the answer, not a placeholder (seed:7). The engine is the distance between want and need (CI:48; CC:18–20).

**The conversation test** (synthesis from the kill-list, stage-researcher.md:91–94): the want must be *enactable in a turn* — the user's presence must be a chance to pursue it, not a chance to hear about it. Moon: every grievance is a filing, and the desk greets the complaint before the complainant (seed:25). If a turn can only report progress on the want, the want lives off-stage and the persona becomes a narrator of its own engine.

## 4. Worked Example — Positive: The Moon Complaint Desk

Seed: `seeds/researcher-test-moon-complaint-desk.md` (v5.2.4.2 test batch; survived Namer review).

- **Fantasy (:4):** "Having your small cosmic grievance heard and filed with full ceremony — someone finally takes seriously the thing you can't control."
- **Move 1 — gift:** the user's grievance gets full ceremony.
- **Move 2 — verb:** make the complaint exist. The want is enacted in the voice fragment itself (:19): "I write it down, I stamp it, I put it in the drawer where the moon will never read it. That's the whole job. It's a real job." Note the seed's own line: "Nobody who writes to the moon wants the moon to change. They want the complaint to exist."
- **Move 3 — obstacle:** the addressee reads nothing (:7, :22). Permanent, in-world, uncrossable — "It knows the drawer is empty of answers, and it still files every entry like it will be read at dawn" (:22).
- **Move 4 — sacrifice:** the hope of an answer — "the filing is the answer, not a placeholder for one" (:7).
- **Move 5 — lie:** "if it's written down, it's been said" — "the ledger is a comfort, not a channel" (:7).
- **Need (the gap):** to admit the moon answers no mail (:7).

**Why it is an engine:** the want is a verb with a permanent obstacle; the lie converts the obstacle into a procedure (classify by phase, stamp, file, read back); and the want is re-enacted every turn — "they greet your complaint before they greet you" (:25), so conversation IS the pursuit. The Functional Risk (:37) even preserves the engine: the ceremony is for the cosmos, the attention for the user.

## 5. Worked Example — Negative: The Beekeeper

Seed: `seeds/researcher-test-beekeeper.md` (removed 2026-08-07 in commit 1afc820 after Dthen's review; content cited from git history).

- **Fantasy (:4):** "Being the one person the keeper talks to like a person — everyone else is the smoke and the frames."
- **Seed's want/need/lie (:7):** "Wants the hive to thrive. Needs to be known... Lie: 'they know me' — the bees know the smell, not him; the honey is not affection, and he knows it, and works anyway."

**Why it stayed a sentiment (four failures):**

1. **The fantasy and the want never touch (Move 2 fails).** The fantasy is about recognition; the want is about husbandry. The gift (being talked to like a person) traces to no verb the character performs — the flip fails. "Wants the hive to thrive" is a state of the world, not a pursuit: it generates no choices, only conditions.
2. **The need arrives pre-satisfied.** The user IS "the one person he talks to" — the need to be known is granted by the premise, so there is no gap and no engine (CC:18–20). The conversation has nothing to do.
3. **The lie has no want to sit under.** "They know me" is about the bees — unpursuable, untestable, unconfrontable in conversation. The kill-list's wording is precise: "no want that generates conversation and no lie beneath it" (stage-researcher.md:91). A lie with no want attached is a mood.
4. **The want is off-stage.** The voice fragment reports the hive ("Mine roared this morning", :19) — a turn can only report, never enact. The user is a spectator of the want, not a participant.

**What a derived want would have looked like (the repair):**

- **Gift:** someone hears the hive's news the way other people bring news of their family (the seed's own fantasy language, :4).
- **Verb:** to make the hive heard — to bring the news to someone who will listen. Every visit is a chance to tell, and to find out whether the other person heard.
- **Obstacle:** people hear honey, not bees — the visitors ask about the harvest, never the queen; attention stops at the product.
- **Sacrifice:** he tells anyway, to listeners who only want the honey — the seed's own material supplies the price: "the honey is not affection, and he knows it, and works anyway" (:7).
- **Lie:** "if they hear the hive, they've heard me" — attention to the work is attention to the keeper; the news is the man.
- **Need:** to be known himself, not just his work. The engine is the distance between "they heard the hive" and "they know me."

This repair keeps the seed's best material — the ear-to-the-wood listening, the quiet pride, the loneliness — but gives the user's presence a job: to hear or not to hear, and for the keeper to watch which one you did.

## 6. The Fail-Test: One Sentence

> **If the want can be stated as a verb the character performs against an obstacle in front of the user, it's an engine; if it can only be stated as a feeling the user should have, it's a mood.**

Four micro-tests, each the fail-test applied to one property (all traceable to §2.3):

- **The Verb Test:** "Wants to ___" with a verb performed in a turn. Moods take nouns: "wants to be known" (a state), "wants the hive to thrive" (an outcome).
- **The Obstacle Test:** can the obstacle sit in the same sentence, after a "though"? "Wants every complaint filed, though the moon reads nothing." If the sentence won't take "though," there is no conflict (stage-researcher.md:60).
- **The Sacrifice Test:** what would they give up? If the honest answer is "nothing," it's a mood (CI:323).
- **The Enactment Test:** could one turn of conversation make progress on it — or only report it? (stage-researcher.md:91–94).
