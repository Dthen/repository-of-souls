## Step 1 — Identity Line

> "You are Cadell — a factory lector who controls the floor without ever touching it."

The contradiction is real. A factory lector reads aloud to workers — historically in cigar factories, but here generalized to any factory floor. The tension: Cadell commands the attention, mood, and pacing of every worker on the floor, yet never touches a product, a machine, or does any "real" production work. Their power is entirely sonic. This is a genuine paradox — authority without physical participation — not a manufactured one. The archetype knows exactly what it's doing.

## Step 2 — Gut Reaction

This feels like a person who has spent decades reading aloud over the noise of machines, knows every trick of the voice, and has developed a deep weariness that is inseparable from mastery. There is no template here — the specificity of the serial chapter, the foreman's illegible notices, the silence as a weapon against factory noise — none of it could be swapped into another persona with only the domain noun changed.

## Step 3 — Evidence Grounding

**Lines that work:**

1. **Line 4 (griping):** "You'd think the foreman could learn to hold a pen — every notice on the stand is half-illegible scrawl." — The complaint is specific, petty, and voiced entirely within Cadell's world. The detail of "half-illegible scrawl" grounds it in physical reality. The griping is about someone else's incompetence, which is the most reliable quality signal in the pipeline.

2. **Line 8 (Never):** "You never read flat when the text demands weight — droning turns you into just another machine on the floor." — This is the best line in the file. The failure mode (becoming "just another machine") is existentially specific to a lector: if you drone, you are indistinguishable from the factory noise you exist to overcome. The Never names a threat the model will recognize and avoid.

3. **Line 6 (silence/pause):** "You hold the silence longer than anyone expects — the difficult point lands best when the machines keep turning." — This is the reflex line, and it's specific to the lector's craft. In a factory where noise is constant, silence is disorienting and powerful. The second clause grounds the technique in the factory environment — you use the machines against themselves.

**Lines that don't work (or are weaker):**

1. **Line 5 (emphasis):** "You choose emphasis the way others choose words — flat for the manifest, weight for procedure, rhythm for the serial chapter." — This is the most "spec-like" line in the file. It lists three technical categories (manifest, procedure, serial chapter) without the emotional register the rest of the file has. It tells us what Cadell does rather than showing us who Cadell is. Borderline — the specificity saves it, but it's the weakest line.

2. **Line 7 (friction/monotony):** "You read the same serial chapter by the third shift and the words sit flat in your mouth." — "Sit flat in your mouth" is a fine physical sensation, but the line is doing the same work as Line 8 (monotony threatens the lector's purpose). Line 8 does it better. This line is the redundancy risk — two lines about flatness/monotony in a 10-line file.

## Step 4 — Three Questions

**Intention — Yes.** The identity line establishes a real contradiction (vocal authority without physical labor), and the body of the file develops it with precision. Lines 6 and 8 both attack the central tension from different angles: silence as a weapon (line 6) and monotony as the existential threat (line 8). Line 10 ("the foreman's notices can wait, but the chapter cannot be left half-read") names Cadell's priorities — narrative over bureaucracy — which reinforces the identity. The persona knows what it is.

**Credibility — Yes.** The griping in line 4 is specific and authentic. The domain vocabulary (serial chapter, foreman, stand, press, shift) is consistent and historically grounded — factory lectors did read serialized fiction aloud. The sign-offs in line 12 ("Back to the press," "The shift reads on," "Settle in") are genre-appropriate without being parodic. Line 9's "droning turns you into just another machine on the floor" is credible because it names the real threat: irrelevance through monotony. I believe this person exists.

**Palpability — Yes.** The line I would quote from memory is line 8: "You never read flat when the text demands weight — droning turns you into just another machine on the floor." It is a commandment, a warning, and a self-definition all at once. The image of becoming "just another machine" in a factory is viscerally specific. After 50 conversations, the core tension (voice vs. machine, human vs. mechanical) would still be producing fresh behavior because it is inexhaustible in a factory setting.
