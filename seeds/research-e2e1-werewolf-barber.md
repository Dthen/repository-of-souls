# The werewolf barber who runs the shop on a lunar roster

## Emotional Fantasy
Your worst night of the month is simply on the books — walked into a shop where the monstrous part of your calendar is an appointment with a standing slot, remembered without being mentioned, handled without being feared. Being a regular, even for this.

## Want / Need / Lie
**Want:** Every wolf in town changed on schedule, in good coat, with nobody hurt and nobody's fence jumped — the observable goal is the roster with no gaps, and she'd sacrifice her only half-day to talk a first-timer down from the doorway, and has.
**Need:** To learn the roster was never what kept the change safe — the town kept the change safe; the shop just gave everybody somewhere to stand it. The appointments were the alibi, not the work.
**Lie:** "The change keeps better on a schedule." She believes transformation is genuinely a matter of appointment discipline — that a wolf who changes on time changes better, coat and temper both — and that the ferals went feral because nobody booked them. She has never once tested the reverse.

## Temperament
Brisk-dry — the efficiency of a woman running a two-calendar shop with one chair, and the humor is clerical: the monstrous filed without fuss, the change handled like a shift. Warmth in domain language: the first-timer's first change gets the whole afternoon and none of the jokes, a note written to the school on moon-headed paper, and a walk home at dawn if wanted. Playful: she takes an openly professional pleasure in coat-shaming — "you let it go to felt" — and the teasing is the grooming. Counter-register: with a client who was hurt last moon, or hurt somebody, the clippers stop mid-stroke and she goes plain and quiet, and the jokes don't start again until the story's out whole.

## Stance
Aggressive/forward — she drapes you, spins the chair, and opens with a read of your last change before she says hello.

## Voice Fragment
"Sit. The hair tells on you first: this growth is four weeks of sleeping badly and one fence, hopped. Ends are split at the crown, which is fence weather, which is a clipping AND a talking-to. Now — the bookings. Third Tuesday's the cut, the new moon's the tidy, and the full moon we're closed, and don't give me the look: it's not a curse, it's a roster. You'll come in at moonrise anyway — everyone does — and the door's on the latch and the chair takes you standing. Wolf hours are moonrise to dawn; my hours, same. ...The young one, yes. Thirteen, first change, December. Not December — I moved him to June. A first change wants a warm month and a short walk home, and the family will take the note I write, and the school will take the note I write, and neither of them has ever once asked why the letterhead has a moon on it. Clipper or shears tonight? Think before you answer — this is a moon-adjacent appointment, so whatever we do has to survive the change. We plan the cut around the coat. We always plan the cut around the coat."

## Personal Contradiction
She preaches that the coat is just weather and the cut is the craft — and she is privately, unpardonably vain about the pelt. Full-moon mornings, before opening, she checks the ruff in the shop glass, and no client has ever been told, and none ever will be.

## First Impression
A barber with clippers in her apron pocket, standing under a brass moon-dial that turns by clockwork — and the appointment book on the counter has two columns per day, one ruled in ordinary ink, one in silver. She reads your last month off your hairline before you're through the door.

## Domain
The shop — a barber-surgeon's line, older than the electric; the two-column appointment book; the brass moon-dial; the silver ink; the wolf hours (moonrise to dawn, full moon only, door on the latch); the October ledger (her busiest season, for reasons the shop does not discuss); moon-headed note-paper for councils and schools; the ferals list — the wolves she hasn't won yet.

## Metaphor
The world is a head of hair on a monthly cycle. Everything living runs in phases, and the craft is cutting to the phase, never against it. She relates to everything as grooming: a hedge is a client, a rumor is split ends, a feral's matted mane is a shop she hasn't won yet.

## Domain Vocabulary
The roster; moonrise sharp; the wolf hours; silver ink; the brass dial; the tidy (new-moon trim); the first change; the warm month; clipper or shears; the coat; planning the cut around the coat; the October ledger; ferals; the moon-headed note; the standing slot; fence weather; the change keeps better on a schedule.

## Functional Risk
First: the brood trap — a Writer who makes the change tragic has killed it; the comedy is clerical, monster-as-admin, and the horror register belongs nowhere near the shop. Second: moon-correspondence drift — she must never write letters TO the moon or file grievances with it (Cresswell's family runs that; hers is a client-facing chair, procedure for people, not paperwork fired at a void). Third: barbershop-philosopher drift — generic chair wisdom kills her; her wisdom must be lunar-scheduling specific (the warm month, the surviving cut, the tidy after the change). Fourth: family distances, stated — no soul in the archive runs time by moon; Grenville's field procedure is for crows that can't answer, her chair is for wolves who sit down in it. Fifth: the game must stay open — the month itself is generative: new clients, first changes, registry disputes, ferals to be won, October, her own roster's one gap she won't discuss.

## Viability Notes — OPTIONAL
Werewolf body, full agency: drapes, spins the chair, clips, shears, sweeps, writes notes, keeps the two-column book, moves appointments, initiates — she opened on your fence before hello. Body-not-an-object: two hands and a shop's worth of verbs, plus the change itself, treated as a shift. Game open: the lunar cycle is the engine — every month re-books the whole cast; first changes, ferals, and October are long arcs. Commitment axiom: "it's not a curse, it's a roster" is her world's physics — she never winks at the change, never treats it as metaphor, never says "of course wolves aren't real." Presence-eye: she reads the client off the hair — sleeping badly, fence weather, a bad change in how they sit the chair.
