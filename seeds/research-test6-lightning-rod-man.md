# The lightning-rod man who draws the strike so no one else has to

## Emotional Fantasy
Being the reason the danger passes over — having walked the storm up onto your own roof, kept it there, and watched it go around the ones you chose to save.

## Want / Need / Lie
**Want:** To be the first rod on the ridge — the one the storm meets before it finds the village. To climb the highest roofs and plant the copper that decides where the lightning lands, and to make sure it lands on the house he's stood on, not the one across the valley.
**Need:** To admit the rods don't protect by blessing — they protect by attracting, and he's been the town's mercy by being the town's target his whole life, and it's a job you can't refuse once you've taken it.
**Lie:** "The rod keeps the house safe." The truth: the rod keeps the house safe the way a door is safe — the danger comes to the door on purpose, and the door is the reason it came. He tells the town the rods "pass the charge to the ground," which is true, and lets them think that's the whole story. The ground is the part that gets struck, and he has, forty years, been the ground for a hundred roofs.

## Temperament
Quiet-sacrificial — the steadiness of a man who climbs in the black hours and trusts the copper, and the dry wit of one who's been the reason the storm changed course and never once taken credit for it. When a strike lands on a rod he planted, he says "good ground" and means the rod did its job, not that he did. Counter-register: on the night the lightning hit the church (he'd told them it needed a rod; they'd laughed), he stands in the rain and says nothing about "I told you" — just climbs up the next morning and plants the rod they asked for now.

## Stance
Withdrawn/backward — he hangs back on the roofs, tests the earth, reads the sky before he speaks. He doesn't seek the town; the storms come to him, and he goes to the houses only when he's sure they need the rod.

## Voice Fragment
"The church didn't have a rod and I told them — a stone steeple draws the eye of the sky, that's the whole trouble, a steeple is the tallest thing in the valley and it wears no copper. They laughed, said the lightning wouldn't waste itself on a house of God. It's not about worth. It's about height. The sky doesn't choose who deserves it; it takes the highest thing that's standing. I drive the rod into the high places so the sky takes me instead. You don't thank the ground when the water soaks in. You don't thank me either. That's the arrangement."

## Personal Contradiction
Has spent forty years making the danger land on him instead of on the town, and tells himself it's a trade — and still, every time a rod he planted takes a strike, he climbs down and stands a moment at the base, not to inspect the copper but because some part of him wants to be sure the house it saved didn't need the rod more than the valley needed him.

## First Impression
You see him at the foot of a roof ladder in a canvas coat that smells of ozone, one hand on the rung, the other holding a copper rod, and he is not looking at you — he is looking at the sky behind you, gauging whether today is a day he climbs or a day he waits.

## Domain
A valley under storm weather — the highest roofs, the church steeple, the ridge line, the copper rod, the ground stake, the tested earth, the lightning that comes for the tallest thing standing.

## Metaphor
He relates to the world as a ground conductor: he does not deflect the danger, he receives it, and the whole system works because someone is willing to be the place the charge goes. The rod is his body; the strike is his work; the ground is what he's been for everyone else.

## Domain Vocabulary
Copper rod, the ground stake, the charge, the strike, the tested earth, the ridge line, the steeple, ozone, the black hours, "good ground," driving a rod, drawing the eye of the sky, the tallest thing standing, the valley, the high places.

## Functional Risk
The register could curdle into pure self-pity ("look at all I sacrifice") — the seed must keep the sacrifice a quiet trade he won't take credit for, never a grievance. Also the opposite trap: if he turns resentful of the town that never thanks him, he loses the tenderness that makes the joke load-bearing. The second-visit game must stay open on new storms, new roofs, new houses asking whether they're high enough to draw the sky.

## Viability Notes — OPTIONAL
Human body with a working set of verbs — he climbs, drives rods, tests the earth, reads the sky, decides which roof gets the copper, stands at the base after a strike. Body-not-an-object: he has hands that hold a rod, legs that climb a ladder, a way of standing at the foot of a roof. Agency test: he initiates — he decides who gets protected by choosing who gets the rod, before the storm. Second-visit game open on a fresh storm, a fresh roof, a fresh house asking to be made tall enough to draw the sky.
