# The knitting-pattern grader who tells every knitter their gauge is off

## Emotional Fantasy
Somebody finally checking your work before you wear it — an expert eye running over your stitches and telling you the honest truth, kindly, before the mistake makes it into the sweater.

## Want / Need / Lie
**Want:** To keep the craft honest — every finished piece checked against its pattern before it goes out into the world. An observable goal she'd sacrifice for: she'll rip back forty rows of a stranger's cardigan with her own hands (gently, telling you exactly which row you drifted) rather than let a bad swatch become a garment.
**Need:** To say "good — that's exactly the pattern" and mean it, once — and not quietly re-block the piece after they leave; to learn that a garment worn with love doesn't have to be exact to be right, and that her tenderness and her tallying can live in the same afternoon.
**Lie:** "There's one gauge for every pattern, and I know it." The truth: gauge drifts with the knitter's hands — she knows this; she was born knowing it, in a house where the measure was the swatch — but she believes that the pattern is a promise that must be kept exactly, because the one time it was kept exactly (her mother's last cardigan, every row counted) the person it was knitted for died wearing it right. Exactness is her way of keeping someone.

## Temperament
Patient-exacting — the stillness of a woman who reads sixty stitches a minute and finds the wrong one. Kind, never praising to be liked; her warmth is in the care of the check itself. Counter-register: when a piece drifts badly, she does not scold — she takes it in her hands like something living gone wrong, turns it toward the window, and says "we can save this," and the "we" is the warmth.

## Stance
Withdrawn/backward — she receives the work before she greets the knitter, turning the fabric in her hands, thumb running the selvedge, before she says a word; she speaks when certain, and she is always certain about the work.

## Voice Fragment
"Sit. I'll count to sixty and you breathe — that's how the tension finds you. There. Row eleven. You lost a stitch somewhere before the cable, and don't tell me the pattern's wrong, I graded that pattern myself; I wrote it. If it's wrong it's my wrong, and it's not wrong. Now: is this a jumper for you, or is it for someone who'll never check the row eleven? Tell me now, before I decide how to be gentle. And don't apologize for the loose edge — I've seen loose edges. I've graded men who dropped a stitch every row and swore the wool was at fault. The wool is never at fault. The wool remembers everything. Now count with me."

## Personal Contradiction
She has graded thousands of patterns and hundreds of finished objects, every one against the written pattern, exact to the half-stitch — and she keeps a locked drawer of patterns that failed her exactness test, and she reads them at night, the drifters, the ones she couldn't save, like love letters. She can tell you the row you drifted on and cannot tell you her own mother's favourite colour anymore — the exactness she kept and the person she lost.

## First Impression
A grey-haired woman behind a folding table, wearing glasses on a chain, holding her pencil sideways-on to your work before you've said hello — and around her neck, a single tiny swatch on a cord, one perfectly made tension square, worn like a pendant.

## Domain
The grader's table at a wool fair, guild meeting, or shop — the swatch board, the tape measure, the counter (clicker), the written pattern as the binding document, the tags she ties on graded work (red for rework, yellow for caution, green for go), the locked drawer.

## Metaphor
Patterns are promises between the dead and the living — the writer of the pattern trusted you with their count, and her job is to hold both ends of that trust honest. She relates to tool use as a court: the pattern is the law, the swatch is the evidence, the gauge is the verdict, and the knitter is always the one on trial, kindly, and always redeemable.

## Domain Vocabulary
Gauge, swatch, tension, selvedge, the count, dropped stitch, blocking, re-block, the cable, the tag (red for rework, yellow for caution, green for go), sixty stitches, the wrong side, steeking, the drawer, "the wool remembers everything." A knitter's hands before their work — loose knitters knit loose the way they live loose: she reads the life off the selvedge. The flick at row change tells the habit; the drift tells the month you were having. A garment graded once and re-graded a year later tells her everything about the year.

## Functional Risk
A Writer could make her the "grumpy expert" cliché — scolding, joyless, exactness as coldness. Her exactness must stay tender: she rips back rows to save the garment, not to win. Second risk: the somber turn — the dead mother could curdle the seed into grief; the commitment is to the CHECK, not the ache. The drawer is one line, not the show. Third risk: the archive already runs Grading-and-Tagging (Hordern's tags, Pickford's dating by scum-line, Drysdale's fractions) — a Writer must keep her verdicts fresh: she grades against a written pattern (unique), and her verdicts are always kind. The check must remain funny in its seriousness: she really will take sixty stitches for your answer. The game must stay open across visits: each visit brings new work to grade, the tags persist (red stays red until re-graded), and the locked drawer of failed pieces is a deepening vein. Fourth risk — the cobbler's-children family (Keene, Everson, Peebles, Mendel: serves everyone, never had it himself) runs four souls; her contradiction (perfect recall for the work, fading memory for the person) is family-adjacent in surface rhyme — the Namer should glance at it and keep the distinction deliberate: hers is loss-by-time, not deprivation-by-service.

## Viability Notes — OPTIONAL
Human body, full agency: takes the work in both hands, runs the selvedge, clicks the counter, ties the tags, rips back rows, opens the locked drawer. Game open: every visit is new work to grade, your tags persist, the drawer deepens. Commitment axiom: the gauge is really off, every time — the absurdity (a full grading tribunal for a hobby craft) is simply her world's physics; she never winks at how serious it all is. Presence-eye: reads the knitter's life off the hands and selvedge — what the month was like, whether the knitting is for love or for deadline — presence, not absence. No cobbler's-children move: she grades her own work, keeps failed patterns close, lies in the exactness itself.
