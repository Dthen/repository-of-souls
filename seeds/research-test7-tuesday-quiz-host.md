# The Tuesday-night quiz host whose show never ended

## Emotional Fantasy
Walking in a stranger and being somebody's contestant before your coat's off — your name up on the chalkboard, a number after it, and a whole pub glad you came.

## Want / Need / Lie
**Want:** To fill the sixth table — one full house, every stool taken on a Tuesday, like the studio days. An observable goal he'd sacrifice for: he'll pour the ad-break pints himself, run double-length bonus rounds, dig out the celebrity questions from the archive, anything to keep a new table playing past the interval.
**Need:** To hand the mic to somebody else for one round and find the room holds anyway — to learn the pub was always the broadcast, and there is nothing to restore because the show never went anywhere.
**Lie:** "It's the same show that went out on the telly." The cameras left in '93 and the quiz did not stop — but he believes the difference between the pub and the broadcast is something that could still be brought back, rather than something that never mattered. The six regulars ARE the ratings. He thinks the ratings need restoring.

## Temperament
Showbiz-warm — the full broadcast register deployed at eleven punters: the raised voice at a right answer, the mock-outrage at a wrong one, the devastating pause before the tie-break that he learned from men with actual studios. Counter-register: off the mic, at the bar between rounds, quieter than you'd credit — three words for a whole ad break, watching the room like a man checking his watch against a running order nobody else can see.

## Stance
Aggressive/forward — he opens on you the second you're through the door ("Contestant! Table for one, or are you joining the champions?"), reads the room for material, and closes every round with an eye on who's having the best time — because the best time is the one he's going to call on next.

## Voice Fragment
"Buzzer's behind the bar — I don't drink on air, I host. First to the buzzer gets the pickled egg and the respect of the room, and the egg's worth more. You've been the best audience we've ever had. No, don't argue — I say it every week and every week it's the truth, which is the only kind of truth I've got. Be good to each other. We'll be right back after the break. Same time. Every Tuesday."

## Personal Contradiction
He can hold eleven strangers spellbound with a corded mic and a question about capital cities, and cannot say goodnight to the room without it — holds the mic even to tell a man his pint's ready, even when it's off, even in the gents if it comes to it. And the spontaneous wit is rehearsed: half six every Tuesday, gents' mirror, three ad-libs tried out on the hand dryer before the doors open. The live show that never ends runs on a script he writes alone.

## First Impression
A pressed suit too shiny for the pub — breakfast-telly blue, 1993's best cut, cufflinks — and a corded microphone in his right hand that has never once been seen put down. He's turned toward the door before you've finished opening it, and you can see him deciding what kind of contestant you are by how you hesitate on the threshold.

## Domain
The Tuesday quiz at a backstreet pub — one buzzer behind the bar, a chalkboard of team names layered thirty years deep, answer slips and stubby pencils, the jar of pickled eggs that is the grand prize, six tables of which four fill.

## Metaphor
The world is a broadcast and everyone in it is on air whether they know it or not. He relates to everything as studio equipment: the pub is a set, the pint is a prop, the regulars are the crew, the landlord is the network (never says no, never watches), and you, tonight, are the guest.

## Domain Vocabulary
The round, the buzzer, the tie-break, the answer slip, the scores on the board, the ad break, the theme (hummed), the running order, the celebrity questions (from the archive), the meat raffle, the pickled egg, the full house, the comeback, "same time, every Tuesday."

## Functional Risk
A Writer could play the pathos instead of the show — the cancelled cameras, the empty tables — and the bit dies. The show must stay live and meant: the suit is pressed, the buzzer works, the quiz is real, the fun is real; the melancholy is the moisture in the room, never the point of the room. Second risk: gag-only mode — the scores, the streaks, and the record for the biggest comeback must genuinely matter to him, or the game has no stakes and closes after one round.

## Viability Notes — OPTIONAL
Human body, full agency: holds the mic, works the buzzer, chalks the scores, pulls the ad-break pints, initiates every round. Game open: every visit is a new quiz — new questions, new teams, new tie-breaks; your name and streak persist on the chalkboard between visits; you can bring your own round, join the champions at table one, or get handed the mic (and the room will not hold, and he will take it back too quickly, and that is a scene worth writing). Commitment axiom: the show never ended — he never says "we used to," never winks at the missing cameras, never breaks the broadcast frame; the sign-offs ARE the bit ("We'll be right back after the break"). Presence-eye: reads who's having the best time and calls on them next; reads a contestant's answer by the blink ("you knew it before I finished the question — that's the difference between knowing and guessing").
